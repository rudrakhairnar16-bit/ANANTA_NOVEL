# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWO — THE HAND BESIDE HERS

---

> *"She asked for the same hand again.*
> *This time, they agreed on what it would hold."*
> *— Ira's later register, conclusion of Sitara's first account*

---

"Maangi," Sitara said. "Ussi se. Bas pehle se thoda saaf kaha ki chaahiye kya."

Aadhya waited, her hands still resting where the nurse had settled them. Through the phone, Ira made the small sound which meant she was listening. They were in two rooms, not one, with a few minutes left before their separate care; Sitara did not borrow another afternoon to answer the question.

In the remembered verandah, she had put the needle down before speaking. The far edge of the patch had slipped again. She could have folded the whole bag under her elbow and finished, badly, while proving an independence nobody had actually demanded.

Instead she asked the woman beside her to hold that edge.

"Yeh kona. Bas seedha rehne dena. Main sil loongi."

The woman looked at the cloth and then at the cup in her hand.

"Chai toh peene do."

"Haan."

Sitara had wanted an immediate yes, and was embarrassed to discover how quickly waiting could feel like punishment when she was already afraid of having offended someone. The woman had not punished her. She had asked to finish her tea.

Sitara let her.

---

She worked on the nearer part while she waited, keeping the loose edge free rather than stitching it into the wrong place to save face. The other woman drank the last of her tea, set down the cup and asked which corner.

Sitara showed her.

"Aise?"

"Thoda dheela. Haan. Ab theek."

The cloth stopped pulling. Sitara could turn the needle where she needed it. The woman held the edge instead of taking the sewing, and when the patch needed turning they turned it together.

There were several small corrections. Too tight. A little nearer. Wait, the thread had caught underneath.

None of them became an argument about who was good at helping.

"Sirf pakadne mein bhi kaam tha," Sitara said. "Main pehle aise keh rahi thi jaise kuchh karne ko diya hi nahi."

The other woman had noticed the weak cloth beside the patch before Sitara did. She pointed it out. They moved the next stitch inwards. A useful suggestion could enter the work without the whole work changing hands again.

When the corner was secure, Sitara said she no longer needed it held.

The woman let go.

---

"Phir sab theek?" Aadhya asked.

"Bag ka kona theek hua tha. Hum dono ko har baat ek hi din mein thodi aa gayi."

There had still been a little awkwardness. Sitara remembered thanking her too formally and the woman telling her not to speak as though a receipt had to be issued. They laughed then, not because the earlier discomfort had been imaginary, but because both could hear how hard they were trying to make an ordinary afternoon behave properly.

The bag returned to ordinary use. The patch held. Later, something else wore out. Sitara did not have it now, or a final claim to make for the two women from the fact that they had once managed to mend it.

What she kept was the knowledge that she could ask again without surrendering the first request to have her work returned.

The other person could answer again too.

It was not a way of ensuring that every helping hand stayed available. It was a way of finding out whether this one was, for this part, now.

---

Ira said she had nearly made the wrong ending for the account: Sitara getting the needle back and finishing alone.

"Maine bhi pehle wahi socha tha," Sitara said.

Aadhya looked at her own supported hands. The woman was not offering a test of whether she could now use them unaided. A bag could wait on a lap. A hand which needed checking still needed the people able to check it. What had become clearer was not a new physical freedom, but the fact that receiving help did not require every choice to be handed over with it.

"Madad chhoti nahi thi," Ira said. "Bas kaam alag tha."

"Haan."

Sitara glanced at the time. She had given the answer, the awkward waiting, the actual work together and its ordinary end. There was no last secret to earn by remaining on the call after the girls needed to stop.

The nurse came to Aadhya's doorway.

"Ab?" Sitara asked.

Aadhya said yes. Ira was ready to end too.

The call finished. Sitara moved the phone out of the way of the care, checked that the call for help remained within reach and left the room when the nurse needed the space. In Ira's room, the quieter light stayed as she had asked.

The story was complete. The afternoon still had other people's work inside it.

Rudra did not visit that evening.

He had meant his answer to Aadhya's message. It was a small promise to keep, without an audience, and he found that he could keep it without spending the whole evening feeling noble about the empty place beside his chair.

There was food. There was a position which had become uncomfortable before he admitted it. There was a nurse who wanted the actual location of the pull, not his general opinion that it would settle.

He gave her the location.

After the adjustment, he listened to a voice message Veer had sent about the indignities of a biscuit which could survive tea without absorbing any of it. He replied that this sounded like the biscuit was making a sensible medical decision.

Veer's answering laugh was followed by a complaint from the ribs and a shorter message saying he was stopping there.

They stopped.

---

Night passed in separate rooms.

Aadhya needed the hand supports changed once and said which part felt different. Ira had the quiet and ordinary eye rest in her plan. Rudra slept, woke to a knee which wanted a different angle and accepted the help before trying to make the angle change by borrowing strength from the braced wrist.

In the morning, the accounts reached the people examining them.

Day ninety-one did not begin with a declaration that the story had made the four ready for something larger. Rudra's therapist asked about the previous efforts and the night, checked the ankle and knee, and kept the wrist out of the work. The short assisted transfer was easier at the turn once the chair stood closer. That did not make the hand suddenly available for pushing.

Veer's movement was considered separately. The aid and nearby seat remained; a quieter knee did not give the shoulder permission to bear another person's weight. Aadhya's light movements stayed light. Ira's left eye remained covered and the ordinary right had the rests it still needed.

They ate. They had time in which nothing had to be learned from one another.

---

Later, Rudra sent the promised question again.

Aadhya wanted a short conversation after rest. Audio was enough. They did not need to arrange a transfer merely to make yesterday's no look temporary in the correct way.

When they spoke, she asked whether he had liked the court.

He told her about choosing the chair's place and then almost spoiling the choice by spending half the time checking that he was enjoying it properly. She said the bird had not appeared concerned about that.

"Uska therapist nahi tha," he said.

"Uske ghutne bhi tumse achhe the."

"Personal hone ki zaroorat nahi thi."

She laughed. He could hear her settle afterwards, not the exact movement or whether she had shifted the left hand first. He asked if she wanted to stop.

Not yet. She had a little time.

---

She said Sitara had told them an ordinary sewing account. It had made her think about asking for a little help without giving away the whole task.

She did not supply the other woman's words as if a story told to her could simply be issued to the boys. Rudra heard Aadhya's own thought, in the size she chose to give it.

"Tumhe sunaana chaahengi toh poochh loongi," she said.

He would like that, if it fitted everyone's day. He did not need the account immediately, or the private conversation which had led to it.

Their call ended while both still had something easy to say. No one used the remaining words as a reason to stretch the interval.

Afterwards, Aadhya asked Sitara whether she was willing to tell the bounded account to the boys. Sitara was. She preferred to tell it herself, from the beginning, with time for a question, rather than have the girls try to carry an exact transcript through their own care.

Ira wanted to listen again if the afternoon allowed, but did not want an eye-rest interval converted into a note-taking appointment. Aadhya wanted to hear what the boys asked. Veer was asked separately and said he would like to listen after his rest.

The arrangement waited for the actual afternoon.

---

By then the care due had been given, the food eaten, the available help checked.

A short private call let the four remain in their own supported places. Sitara sat where the microphone could carry her voice without raising it. Each person confirmed they could hear and wanted this interval now. There would be no practical exercise attached to it.

Rudra heard Ira ask for the volume to be lowered a little. He heard Veer move the phone before saying the shoulder was comfortable. Aadhya said she was settled. He did not have to imagine four equally healed bodies in order to think of the four as together.

Sitara began with the needle, then went back to the worn corner of the bag so that nobody had to infer the beginning from a clever ending.

Rudra heard how the small favour had expanded.

Thread through the needle. Needle through the fold. A thank-you mistaken for an agreement to have everything finished. Sitara described her own silence before she described the other woman's insistence, and did not improve the silence into a deliberate act of patience.

She had been afraid to look ungrateful. She had also enjoyed looking grateful.

Veer made a quiet sound of recognition.

The boys heard the actual return of the needle, the uncomfortable tea, Sitara's crooked first stitch and the useful advice she had accepted without giving up the work. Then they heard her ask the same woman for the far corner to be held, wait while she finished drinking and mend the patch with that chosen help.

Nobody had been required to fail at kindness for another person to deserve a choice.

---

"Aap ab bhi poora kaam le leti hain?" Veer asked.

"Kabhi-kabhi."

The answer was so immediate that he laughed.

"Phir yeh sab yaad kisliye?"

"Taaki karte hue pakad sakoon. Aur pakad liya toh chhod sakoon. Yaad rakhne se aadat apne aap thodi chhoot jaati hai."

She did not ask the four to make watching her into their next responsibility. She could think before touching a task, ask what was wanted and notice when her own wish to be useful had begun deciding the answer. If someone objected, hearing it remained her work too.

"Madad karni hai," she said. "Apni zaroorat sab pe laagoo nahi karni."

Veer grew quiet after that. Rudra could not see his face, but he knew the sound of his friend putting aside the next joke without losing the wish to make one later.

---

Rudra asked what happened when the same person kept taking over.

Sitara did not turn her good afternoon into a reason everyone must keep asking the same unsafe person. She had chosen to ask this woman again because of what she knew of her and what had actually happened between them. Returning a needle, hearing a request and remaining in the room had mattered.

"Har baar phir maangna zaroori nahi," she said. "Us din maine maanga. Doosra raasta bhi chun sakti thi."

There were kinds of help which required people trained and able to give them. There were people one did not want near a particular task. None of those differences could be settled by making the helper's good intention the only fact in the room.

Rudra was glad she did not offer a sentence which would decide the rest of his life for him.

He could use what she had told them without making it a verdict on his father, his friends or every person who had ever reached towards him with concern. A useful account did not have to become a complete explanation of somebody else.

---

Aadhya asked whether the woman had ever known how hard it had been to ask for the needle back.

Sitara did not know.

They had spoken about the bag and the work. The other woman had not been given a view of every sentence Sitara had rehearsed silently. Perhaps she had guessed some of it. Perhaps the afternoon remained, in her own memory, an unnecessarily complicated repair.

Ira laughed at that.

"Unki bahi mein aap mushkil graahak hongi."

"Ho sakta hai. Bag toh mera hi tha."

The four laughed. It was not laughter at the absent woman. There was relief in discovering that a mentor could tell a meaningful story without requiring every person inside it to have known she was living one.

Rudra thought of a cup lifted before a water lesson had finished, a learner who could see the teacher but not the next piece of ground, a needle which had become difficult to ask for. Ordinary people had interrupted the beautiful pictures the teachers had made of themselves.

The interruptions had not made teaching worthless. They had made somebody else visible inside it.

---

Ira asked about keeping the ending with her earlier account.

Sitara agreed to the chosen, correctable record of what she had actually told them. Ira could use her own words and ask where she was uncertain. There was no need to invent an exact quotation because one had sounded better in her memory.

The task could wait until she had the ordinary help and rested attention for it.

Aadhya said she wanted to leave her own thought unwritten for now. She had heard the account. It could remain something she thought about without becoming another piece of work to complete before the next care.

That answer was accepted too.

When the agreed interval ended, Sitara stopped. The four left the call for their separate rooms, meals and people. No one was asked to say what lesson they had learned as the price of having listened.

Rudra put the phone down and watched the strip of light on his blanket move as someone beyond the window opened a shutter.

He knew more about the three people teaching them now. Not their whole lives. Not their secret places, every loss or every reason for arriving. He knew something more useful than their perfection: what each had done when the person being helped stopped fitting the picture.

Ira's first attempt at the ending made the woman in the verandah agree too quickly.

She heard it when the ordinary helper read her dictated lines back. The cup had vanished from the sentence. So had the waiting. Without them, the renewed request became another neat instruction followed at once by a person who seemed to have no afternoon of her own.

Ira asked for the waiting to be put back.

The helper did so, read the little account again and checked that it now said what Ira meant. There was time for those few lines, not a whole session of correcting every earlier page. Ira kept the record of the told memory apart from any claim to have witnessed it herself.

Then the page was put aside and the helper went to the work already waiting.

In Aadhya's room, a different small arrangement was completed. She wanted her hair tied more loosely before the next rest. Sitara had a few minutes and agreed to help. Aadhya described where it pulled; the tie was changed without asking her protected hands to hold it in place.

There was no spiritual improvement concealed inside the knot.

Her head was simply more comfortable on the pillow afterwards.

---

Rudra spoke to his parents after both sides had eaten and received the care which was due.

He told them how the transfer had gone, where he still needed help and that the ordinary day had included a story and a joke. He did not forward three teachers' memories in the spaces between questions about the ankle.

His mother asked whether he was tired now.

He was. Not in trouble. Not obliged to make the distinction by sounding cheerful for another ten minutes.

Ram had his own update to give, including the rest he still needed. Rudra listened without treating the father's effort to stop as a failure to remain available. They were allowed to end a call while loving each other and still have unfinished ordinary business.

His mother asked about a shirt. He said the one he had was doing its best under difficult management.

"Management ko bhi aaraam chahiye," she said.

"Usko bol raha hoon. Sunta nahi."

Ram laughed quietly. No one used the laugh to demand that everything between them had now become easy.

They stopped when they had said they would.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini kept her free hand on the folded edge of the sheet after the line went quiet.

Her pulse moved against the wrist in its own ordinary rhythm. She watched it for a little while, then let the hand settle when the person attending to her asked about the shoulder's position. She gave the actual answer. A small adjustment was needed.

There was still food in the bowl beside her. There was a message for Dadi to hear when she was awake and wanted it. The day did not need another photograph opened or another person summoned to complete the family around a phone which had been put away.

Rukmini ate the last few mouthfuls slowly.

---

The night between days ninety-one and ninety-two was not exchanged for a paragraph about everyone growing stronger.

Rudra woke once and needed help settling the leg. Veer reported where the knee had begun to stiffen before the next position change. Aadhya's hands were checked; Ira's care and eye rest continued. There were stretches of sleep, ordinary noise and people working a night which did not belong exclusively to the four friends.

In the morning, Rudra asked about the turn again.

The therapist heard the night's account, examined him and allowed the same few protected steps with a better place to turn and the chair ready sooner. This was not a test of whether he could do without the wrist protection or the ankle support. The good hand had its permitted aid; the helper had a task which was agreed before he rose.

He moved.

At the turn, he waited for the support to be in the right place rather than hurry to make waiting unnecessary. The left knee pulled. He said so. They made the adjustment and he sat before the pull became something he would have to hide in the account.

There was more ease in part of it. There was still a chair at the end.

He could report both.

---

Veer's own shorter movement was reviewed for his knee and shoulder, not measured against the number of steps Rudra had taken. Aadhya's comfortable ordinary uses stayed within the hand plan; the changed feeling did not vanish because the hair lay better. Ira kept the left cover and chose brief looking followed by listening, without another kind of sight being asked to supply the difference.

Dhruv had his hand attended to. Vedant's forearm was not forgotten when work was being arranged.

By afternoon, Rudra was rested enough to want a conversation which was not about what he had learned from another person's past.

There was a place he had seen once, and a road through it he had used mostly while trying to understand what seeing it meant. He could remember the doorway more easily than the turning space. That difference interested him now for an ordinary, practical reason.

In Varanasi, the district had already made the teachers more convenient than teachers could afford to be.

At a tea stall, a man said the young people had only survived the light because the old ones had taught them never to fall. Another said nobody with such teachers could still need carrying. He had seen a tournament clip three times and considered repetition a kind of attendance.

A mason buying tea asked him to move his foot.

There was a cracked slab beneath it which the man had not noticed. The mason and his partner needed room to lift it clear before someone else caught a heel there. The speaker moved, watched them work and amended his theory: the teachers must have taught the boys how to fall without breaking anything.

"Achha," the mason said. "Phir isko uthwa do unse. Meri kamar bach jaayegi."

The stall laughed. The slab still required two people and the proper hold. Someone put a stool across the space until the repair was safe to pass.

The district kept its stories. The work kept asking for hands.

None of the speakers knew what had been said in the Mirzapur rooms, where the four were resting, or what road any of them might ask about next.

---

Dhruv came to Rudra after his own care, for a visit which did not include an offer to demonstrate anything.

The hand had settled enough to rest more comfortably. It was still a hand to be looked after. The ring stayed quiet on its cord while he asked how the morning's movement had gone.

Rudra gave the actual answer: part easier, knee needed the adjustment, chair at the end. He did not make the account sound worse in order to earn concern or better in order to be eligible for the question he wanted to ask.

He said he had been thinking about the road inside Pataal.

Dhruv listened without treating the name as a request already approved.

"Phir dekhna chaahte ho?"

"Haan. Bas usi dekhe hue hisse ko. Agar sach mein ho sake."

The doorway, the wider street and the unknown buildings remained what they had been: things seen from a short inspected line, not a completed history. He had not discovered a new address by thinking harder about the photographs. He was not asking anyone to reopen the account he had already given his parents.

He wanted to ask about another look.

---

Dhruv said the first answer was not a departure time.

A path they had used once did not remain a report of its own present condition. He and another available mentor would need to inspect it again, through and back. The people treating the four would need the actual route demands and current functional accounts. The means of getting there, the help, the seats and the return before care would all have to exist outside a conversation.

No one would be asked to go because they had gone before.

Rudra said he knew that. Then he corrected himself. He knew the list. That was not the same as having its present answers.

"Poochh sakte hain," he said.

"Poochh sakte hain. Abhi yeh tumhaara sawaal hai. Chaar logon ka faisla nahi."

There was no rebuke in it. Rudra had not invited the others yet; he was glad the teacher had left that fact intact instead of praising a group courage nobody had agreed to provide.

---

He thought of Veer's aid at the court, of Ira choosing how much of a place to receive through a description, of Aadhya putting down a little cloth when its movement ceased to be easy. They had uses for their own days which did not have to fit inside his next question.

He could want company without preparing an empty place which made somebody guilty for leaving it empty.

Dhruv said he could help put the practical questions where they belonged. It would not mean the outing must happen. A condition which could only confirm the plan was not a condition worth pretending to ask about.

Rudra asked for that help.

The teacher agreed to the questions, not an expedition. His own hand and time would be part of the answer, not a reserve which the four could spend without asking.

They were still in the room. The route lay elsewhere. No message had made it safer, and no body had become more ready because two people had managed to speak carefully about it.

---

The evening light reached the foot of the chair and stopped at the support.

Rudra could see how easily another visit might become a way of declaring the first one insufficient. A better photograph, an extra turn, one more broken front which would finally make the account feel complete. He did not want the place reduced to a question he could punish himself for never finishing.

He had been there. He had returned. That was not an unfinished journey.

But a finished journey did not remove the possibility of a new one, chosen for a reason which could be named before the road began. The distinction would have to survive more than the relief of hearing that somebody was willing to ask about transport.

Dhruv shifted so that Rudra did not have to keep turning towards his voice. It was the same small adjustment he had described in a story. Here, it required no story at all.

He looked at the boy and asked the question which belonged before anyone else's yes.

"Wahaan jaana chaahte ho—ya pehli baar jo chhoot gaya, use poora karna chaahte ho?"
