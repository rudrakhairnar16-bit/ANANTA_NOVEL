# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHT — THE SPACE THE TIMBER LEFT

---

> *"The later account gave the lift to four people.*
> *It did not count the hand which stopped reaching for the wood."*
> *— Correction to the district rescue sheet, collected the following winter*

---

"Neeche! Sir ki taraf!"

Sitara's shout reached the woman before Sitara could.

The clinical worker stopped rising. She let go of the leg and dropped towards the man's head, shoulder first, into the narrow place his reaching hand had just vacated. Her knee struck rock. The baulk swept across the back of her tunic.

Cloth tore.

She went flat.

At the lowered end, the timber turned on the jack's narrow contact. The worker took his hand away from the handle instead of trying to keep the load straight with his arm. A corner of the wood bit down into the packing beneath it.

The top block split at its edge.

The blocks below held.

The far end slid down the inward face of the wall and struck the rubble at its foot. Dust jumped into the lamp. The baulk stopped at a different slope, twisted towards the patient, its near corner still across his lower leg.

He screamed.

The woman beside his head lifted her face out of the grit. Her back was clear of the wood. She could breathe.

"Koi haath neeche nahi!" the worker at the packing shouted.

Sitara had already taken one step from the board. She stopped with her palms open. There was nothing useful a bare hand could put under that weight.

---

The constable reached the knife arm as the far end struck.

His lathi hit the man's forearm. The blade turned away from the working space, scraped the officer's sleeve and caught skin beneath it. The constable drove his shoulder against the arm before the man could draw it back.

They hit the low wall together.

The knife remained between them.

"Haath chhod!"

The attacker tried to pull towards the gap. One boot landed on the stone he had kicked from under the timber. It rolled. His knee went down against the wall's broken foot, and the constable went with him rather than let the armed hand swing free.

The backup officer came off the lip.

Sitara pointed him along the guard's clear approach, away from the trapped leg and the timber's new fall line. He passed the board without putting a foot over it. The first officer had the attacker's wrist against the wall; the second took the free arm and forced it behind him.

The man struck backwards with his head.

His mouth hit stone when the first constable turned him away from the knife. Blood appeared at the lip. The fingers opened at last.

The blade fell on the streetward side of the wall.

The officer put his boot between it and the hand. His partner secured the wrists, checked the restraint, then brought the man away from the fallen knife and onto a clear patch beside the rock. There he could sit with both feet visible and the wider street in the officers' view.

He was breathing hard. The first constable was too.

No one called the struggle finished until the knife was out of reach.

---

The worker at the jack looked towards the woman on the ground.

"Lagi?"

She pushed herself onto an elbow. Dust made a grey print along her cheek. The torn cloth hung loose at the back, but she could turn her shoulders and draw a full breath. When she moved the knee which had struck rock, pain brought her hand down against it.

"Ghuttna laga. Lakdi nahi."

"Uthna mat abhi," Sitara said.

The woman looked at the wood before looking at her own leg.

"Usko kheenchna nahi."

"Nahi kheench rahe."

Sitara came along the clear near side, keeping below the timber's new angle without putting herself beneath it. She knelt beside the woman's head and asked before touching her shoulder. The woman nodded. Together they shifted her farther towards the lip, out of the load's possible reach.

Only then did she sit.

The patient was still calling for her. Each cry ended on the same small intake of air, as though pain had made a knot which his lungs kept finding.

"Main yahin hoon," she told him. "Pair mat kheenchna."

Her bag was where Sitara had moved it before the rope parted. Nothing heavy had landed on it. Sitara passed it over.

At the wall, the first constable glanced down at his sleeve.

A dark patch was spreading below the tear.

---

The backup officer took over the outward watch with the restrained man directly before him. His partner stepped back from the wall, pressed cloth against the forearm and showed the clinical worker where the knife had reached.

She asked whether he could move the fingers, whether they felt different, how fast the cloth was wetting. He answered and kept the pressure where she indicated. Her voice had lost none of its precision; her hands trembled when she opened the bag.

She rested them against her thighs for a moment.

The pinned man saw it.

"Jaldi," he said.

"Haan," she answered. "Par dobara giraa ke nahi."

The workers were looking at the new position of the timber. One pointed to the damaged upper block. His partner looked at the jack, then at the far end's bed of loose stone.

Neither touched the handle.

The space they had made before the knife came was gone. They would have to earn it again.

The clinical worker cleaned her palms before putting on fresh gloves.

Sitara held the opened packet where she could reach it without twisting. The woman examined the constable's cut, dressed it and made him keep the hurt arm out of the work. The bleeding slowed under the dressing. He would need a proper review after they were out; the fact that his fingers answered him was useful, not a discharge.

She then looked at the man who had cut him.

The backup officer brought the lamp closer without letting the restrained hands disappear from view. One wrist was swelling where the lathi had struck. The split lip had stopped bleeding freely. He could answer questions about his breathing and where he hurt.

"Isko bhi baahar dikhana hai," she said.

The first constable said he would record what had happened.

"Jo kiya, woh bhi."

"Haan. Dono."

Treatment had not erased the rope in two pieces or the wood across the other man's leg.

---

Sitara helped the woman check the knee. There was a swelling under the cloth and an abrasion where it had ridden up. She could straighten the leg, and with Sitara beside her she put a little weight through it, then sat again. The impact had hurt. It had not given her permission to pretend she could kneel and lift as before.

"Main sambhaal ke chal sakti hoon," she said. "Uthaane mein meri ginti mat karna."

The two workers heard. Their next plan used that answer.

From a flat place on the near side she could reach the patient without sitting under the timber. One worker put her bag within reach. He asked before moving it; she showed him the side she wanted open.

The man's voice was growing hoarse.

She gave him the next thing to do, watched him do it, then checked what the new fall had changed. The leg was still caught. Nobody could measure the damage by how loudly he had shouted before, or by how quietly he was trying now.

---

The restrained attacker looked towards the gap behind the wall.

The backup officer shifted so that the man's body could not hide his hands from the original guard.

"Baahar chalna hai. Dheere."

"Mera haath—"

"Dekhwaayenge. Jhatka mat do."

They checked the restraint around the swelling wrist and adjusted what needed adjusting without letting him have a free striking hand. The injured constable could keep the outward watch for the short escort. He did not take the prisoner's weight or offer the cut arm as a handhold.

Sitara went ahead to check the return.

The board still lay beside the patient. The narrow turn beyond the lip was unchanged by the falling timber. She walked the old line to Earth, called to the officer there and waited where the approaching pair could see her.

They came through on their own feet.

The passage took nothing from the man because he had used it with a knife before. He had to lower his head under the same rock, turn a shoulder at the same pinch and watch the same uneven ground.

At the mouth, the waiting constable took charge of him. The ambulance driver was still with the cheek-wounded man, who remained seated where the clinical worker had left him. The two injured men were kept apart. The broken staff lay under police control, not back in its owner's hand.

The road-turn officer stayed at the road.

---

Sitara gave the Earth-side officers the changes in plain order.

A cut rope. The timber moved. The clinical worker got clear but hurt her knee. A constable's forearm cut and dressed. The trapped man still alive, not yet out. The third man disarmed and brought here, with injuries of his own.

The officer called for another medical vehicle and police relief and transport. He repeated that nobody arriving should walk into the opening without the current briefing. The request was a request, still travelling down an ordinary phone line; Sitara could not spend people who had not arrived.

The medical driver checked the cheek-wounded man's responses again. No new change required the clinical worker to be called away that moment. He showed Sitara where the ambulance stretcher waited for the more urgent patient.

She used her own phone from Earth.

Vedant answered after the second ring.

"Hum theek hain," she began, then corrected the sentence before it could become a promise larger than the facts. "Main theek hoon. Lakdi hili thi. Do logon ko chot lagi. Ilaaj ho raha hai."

She gave him the same account, including the still-trapped leg and the man just brought through. There was no request at its end for the patients, Dhruv or their helpers.

"Kisi ko bhejna nahi," she said.

"Nahi bhej raha. Tum bhi apna dekhna."

She looked at the dust on her unhurt hands.

"Dekhoongi. Abhi waapas usi chhote hisse tak."

She and the backup constable checked the line as they returned. At the lip, the first officer answered them from the bend. Both workers were still in their places. The clinical worker was speaking to the patient.

The route had carried three people out and two back. Beyond the guarded wall, the dark remained unexamined.

The lead worker squatted where he could see the jack's base.

The timber had turned above it. The base itself had not shifted on the stone, but the contact above it was wrong now, too close to an edge. He showed his partner. They spoke briefly, looking at the same places rather than asking the injured man to wait through competing guesses.

The lower packing was sound. Beside it lay two loose, undamaged blocks and the thin packing pieces the workers had brought for the last small gains.

At the far end, the baulk lay against the foot of the wall on broken stone. The wall's bottom course was still firm. The worker who had watched the rope part checked that bearing from the guarded side, without reaching under the wood or climbing into the unseen gap.

The new slope could be supported. It could not be trusted as it lay.

"Pehle dono sire," he told his partner.

They used the loose blocks to arrest the far end's sideways movement against the sound wall foot. The torn rope was left where it could no longer pretend to be a restraint. At the near end, the packing carried the weight while they corrected the jack's position from outside the crush space. The damaged upper block stayed in view; they did not pull it from a loaded stack to make the work look tidy.

Sitara moved the board a little farther down the clear side when they asked. It scraped grit with a small, domestic sound, like a bed being moved in a room which had become too crowded.

The clinical worker watched the patient rather than the tools.

He was looking at the bandage on the constable's arm.

"Woh chala gaya?"

"Baahar le gaye," Sitara said.

He shut his eyes.

She did not supply a feeling to go with the movement.

---

The backup constable kept the lip and the near approach in view while his partner watched the street. Both knew where the recovered knife lay. Neither took a handle on the board merely because a waiting hand looked idle.

The lamp threw a hard bar of light along the baulk. Beyond it, the wall seemed to move whenever a shoulder interrupted the beam. Sitara made herself look at the stones which stayed where they were.

The lead worker asked the clinical worker whether she needed more time.

She did.

There were things she wanted ready before the pressure came off the leg, and changes she needed the others to tell her about at once. She explained her next move to the patient in words he could follow. He answered his name too quietly for Sitara to catch; the woman heard, repeated it to him and went on with the work.

Sitara did not lean closer to collect it.

A name obtained for care was not automatically a story she had been given to take home.

---

In Mirzapur, Vedant waited until Rudra's check was finished before passing on Sitara's new report.

"Lakdi giri?"

"Hili. Woh bach gayi. Ghuttne pe lagi hai. Constable ka haath kata, dressing ho gayi. Teesra aadmi baahar laaya hai."

Rudra looked towards the end of his bed. The ankle was still supported, the wrist protected. For a moment he saw the lamp at the bend instead: a person's back where the wood had been, hands which had not known him before that evening doing work his own hands could not do.

"Unko bataana, main—"

He stopped.

Vedant waited without completing it for him.

"Abhi mat bataana kuchh. Kaam karne do."

"Haan."

The words he had nearly offered would have made a worker stop, listen and reassure him. There would be time for thanks when thanks no longer arrived as another task.

He asked whether the pinned man was out.

Not yet. That was the report's limit.

Rudra took it without turning the unknown minutes into a place he could fill by trying to stand. The nurse adjusted the light before leaving. He told her it was comfortable and let his eyes close, still awake.

---

At the bend, the clinical worker asked for the first small lift.

The jack took the changed contact. Wood creaked. The patient gripped his own shirt rather than a rescuer's wrist.

The worker stopped almost immediately.

His partner looked along the packing, called the change and secured the small gain before the handle moved again. The far end remained in its bed. There was no answering scatter of wall stone.

Sitara could see a narrow shadow open above the compressed cloth at the man's leg.

It was not enough.

He saw her looking.

"Nikal raha hai?"

She could have spent the fraction they had gained on reassurance. Instead she bent where he could hear her without lifting his head.

"Jagah ban rahi hai. Abhi tum nahi hiloge."

The clinical worker gave the same instruction with a hand against his shoulder. He let the shirt loosen between his fingers.

The jack moved again.

This time the packing caught the new height with a blunt, solid sound. Everyone waited for the far end to answer.

It stayed.

The patient began to move before there was room.

It was only a turn of the hip, the body's blind argument that less weight must mean freedom. His face changed before the cry came. The clinical worker stopped him with her voice.

"Meri taraf. Bas dekho."

She waited until his gaze held.

"Abhi hum hilaayenge. Tum madad karne ke liye bhi pair nahi chalaaoge. Samjhe?"

He nodded too quickly.

She asked him to tell her what he had understood. The answer came back, broken but right. Only then did she signal the worker.

The next lift opened enough room to see the trapped cloth, not enough to draw the limb through without touching wood. They held it there. The second worker filled the gained space with the thin packing pieces and checked the far bed again.

Dust settled on the patient's shirt. He watched it as though the falling specks were something he could count without pain.

Sitara pulled the board close along the near side.

---

The clinical worker explained the move to the others.

The injured leg would be supported in the position it could tolerate, without a desperate straightening pull. She would direct the transfer from beside the head. Her knee would not be asked to carry another person's weight because the task had reached its hardest part.

Sitara and the workers placed themselves where she indicated. The backup constable came to the near side after his partner confirmed the outward watch. His sound hands could help with this move. The dressed constable remained between them and the wider street, the lathi in his unhurt hand.

At the final small rise, the leg came clear of the timber.

The packing was secured before the worker left the jack. He watched the load settle into what they had built, then took his place at the patient. The far end stayed against its blocked bearing. The blade on the near rock was still beyond the patient's reach.

The timber would have another movement later, when nobody lay underneath it. This movement belonged to the man.

"Taiyaar?" the clinical worker asked.

Each person answered for their own grip.

---

They moved him the short distance onto the board.

The worker supporting the leg followed the clinical worker's directions without trying to make its shape look right. Sitara had the shoulders. The other worker and the backup officer controlled the middle of the body and the board's position. It was an awkward, careful transfer, interrupted once when pain made the patient try to twist away from the hands helping him.

"Ruko."

Everyone stopped.

The clinical worker spoke close to his face. He could hear her without lifting his head.

"Chhod nahi rahe. Saans lo. Phir karenge."

His fingers caught Sitara's sleeve. She let him hold cloth, not her wrist, and waited until the pressure eased enough for her to work.

The second attempt finished it.

His body lay on the board. The leg was free. The baulk remained above an empty patch of stone.

For a moment Sitara could hear only the breathing around her: the man's harsh and rapid, the workers' deliberate, her own much louder than she expected. One of the workers wiped his forehead with the clean back of a wrist. A pale stripe appeared through the dust.

No one cheered.

The clinical worker was already examining what had been hidden.

---

The trouser cloth had been driven tight against the injury. She dealt with what she needed to see and left the rest covered. The man turned his face away. Sitara held the lamp where asked, keeping its glare out of his eyes.

His boot was still beneath the wood.

"Joota," he said.

The worker nearest the jack looked at the small dark shape. Getting it meant reaching back under the loaded corner.

"Abhi nahi."

The man swallowed. His gaze stayed on the place where the boot had been separated from him. Sitara could not tell whether he was thinking about the cost of replacing it, the foot it had held or something which had nothing to do with shoes. She did not need the answer to keep the light steady.

The clinical worker asked for another dressing.

---

The backup constable laid out the board's straps while she worked.

The patient flinched when one came across his middle.

"Baandh rahe ho?"

"Girne se bachaane ke liye," she said. "Yeh ilaaj ka hai. Dard badhe toh bolo."

He looked from her to the constable.

"Phir?"

The officer understood enough of the question.

"Baahar ilaaj. Uske baad jo poochhna hoga, poochhenge."

No promise that the leg had purchased silence. No threat that an answer was needed before the next dressing.

The man let the strap be settled. His hands lay where the officer could see them, shaking against the board's edge.

Sitara helped place the folded padding without pressing on the injured area. The clinical worker checked the fit and moved one piece. It was a small correction. Everyone waited for it.

Then she looked at the exposed foot again.

The lamp had not changed. Its colour had.

The woman checked the foot, then the other one.

She loosened a part of the padding, made sure nothing they had added was pressing where it should not and examined the injured side again. The patient said he could feel her touch, then hesitated when she asked where.

The answer would not settle.

"Abhi nikaalna hai," she told the others. "Seedha ambulance."

The backup officer went ahead along the checked line to Earth. From the bend he could not turn a shout into a clinical handover at the road. Someone had to carry the words the rest of the way.

While he went, the workers checked the straps and the places where their hands would change at the turn. Sitara cleared the last bag from beside the board. The clinical worker kept examining the foot, her face close to the ordinary light.

The man watched her.

"Kya hua?"

"Pair ko jaldi hospital pahunchana hai."

"Kaatega?"

The question landed without any of the force he had once put behind his knife.

She answered him at once.

"Abhi yahan woh faisla nahi kar sakte. Jaldi leke chal rahe hain."

He began to reach down. She caught the movement before his fingers touched the dressing and put his hand back where it could rest. There was no useful test he could do to his own foot by pinching it harder.

---

On Earth, the officer reached the ambulance and gave the report himself.

The trapped man was free, on the board. The clinical worker wanted immediate transport for the leg. They were bringing him now. She would come out with him, walking with a hurt knee; the second constable inside had a dressed cut. No fourth attacker had been seen from their limited positions.

The medical driver opened the loading space and called the receiving emergency team with the information sent out. He repeated the limits of a relayed examination instead of turning it into a diagnosis. The ambulance's stretcher stood ready on the clear approach behind the road bend.

The cheek-wounded man looked at it.

"Meri gaadi?"

"Doosri maangi hai," the driver said. "Aapko dekhne waali abhi aa rahi hai."

He checked the man's responses while they waited. The officer beside him kept both treated men in view, with the third attacker seated separately, hands secured and the swollen wrist visible. The request for relief had been acknowledged. No new officer or medical crew was there yet.

The backup constable went back to the lip and on to the board.

---

The original guard could see no one moving in the strip beyond the wall. He said exactly that. The words did not extend around its hidden side.

The knives and the cut line would need recovery. The jack and packing could not simply be kicked away from the changed load. Their positions were noted; the immediate passage out took priority over making a completed scene of the place.

Sitara looked at the baulk before putting both hands through the board's handles.

It had left a pale rectangle on the grit where the man's body had lain. Beside it were the prints of the woman's dropped shoulder and knee. A torn thread from her tunic hung on a rough corner of wood.

Later, people might point to the empty place and say how easily a man had been pulled from it.

The worker at the head asked Sitara whether she could take the grip she had offered.

She tested it with the board still down, told him where her arm was comfortable and waited for the others. Her hands were beginning to tire. That fact joined the plan rather than being hidden from it.

The two workers took the ends. Sitara and the backup officer would support the side handles where the width allowed, change position before the pinch and set down if any grip failed. The clinical worker stood with help and checked her own balance. She took the lamp from Sitara, slung the bag clear of her sore knee and chose the place from which she could watch the patient's face.

The injured guard would come last. He had no carrying task.

---

"Uthaao."

The weight came into four pairs of hands.

One end rose a little too quickly. The worker at the foot called it; they corrected before stepping. The clinical worker asked the patient to keep looking towards her voice.

Sitara followed the boots ahead. Stone. Clear edge. Another small step.

They brought the board towards the lip, with the guarded street behind them and the narrow return still to be negotiated. At the first uneven place they set it down as agreed, keeping the injured leg supported instead of tilting the whole man to save a few seconds.

The guard came up behind them. Nothing had followed into his view.

The clinical worker crouched on her sound knee beside the board. The patient had gone very quiet, but he answered when she spoke. His hand found the edge and held it, empty.

She checked the exposed foot once more in the steadier light at the lip.

Then checked again.

Sitara watched the woman's fingertips stay still.

"Paaon mein nabz nahi mil rahi," she said.
