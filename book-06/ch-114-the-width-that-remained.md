# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND FOURTEEN — THE WIDTH THAT REMAINED

---

> *"The first measurement belonged to a road.*
> *The second belonged to the people trying to leave it."*
> *— Ira's later register, reconstruction of the broken lip*

---

Rudra's right foot struck loose stone below the sheared edge.

The drop was short. The twist was not. Pain ran through the ankle support and up the outside of the leg; he lost the attempt to stand before he could decide what to do with it. His left hip hit the sloping rock. His good palm skidded out of the hollow, skin scraping, and he went sideways towards the rubble with the collar dressing still caught under his other hand.

"Neeche raho!" Sitara shouted.

He was already down. He made himself stop trying to rise.

The recovering wrist stayed against his chest. He did not throw it behind him. His forearm and hip took the last short slide, and the heel of the good hand found a rough projection which slowed him without holding his whole weight.

There was stone beneath his thighs. Air beside the broken edge. His right foot lay lower, turned against a loose fragment.

He could breathe. He told her that first.

---

Sitara had to take two more steps to reach him.

She placed the first on the middle she had already seen, then stopped with her feet apart where a fresh pale line crossed its outer part. She could reach his upper arm from there if she lowered herself. She could not stand at his side on the piece which had just left.

"Pair kheenchna mat. Kahaan atka hai?"

"Edi ke paas. Patthar."

He looked instead of moving it to demonstrate.

The wall-end scraped on the far side of the shoulder. The man was pulling at its high edge, trying to bring it round the projection between them. One end dragged; the other climbed a little and fell back with a knock which Rudra felt through his hip.

Sitara crouched on the sounder middle.

Her first touch was below Rudra's shoulder, not at the wrist holding the cloth. She asked him to leave his weight on the rock while she looked at the foot.

A loose wedge had settled against the heel. Nothing held it there except the angle and his own weight. Freeing it would not repair the ankle. It would allow them to move without forcing that particular turn.

"Meri taraf dekho," she said. "Uthna nahi. Bas thoda wazan idhar."

He shifted through the hip rather than planting the right sole. She steadied the upper arm while his good hand found the lower projection again. The wedge loosened enough for her to push it aside with the edge of her shoe.

The foot came clear.

The pain stayed.

---

He wanted to apologize for the time.

Stone shrieked against stone before he could say it. The man had changed his grip on the wall-end. His scraped arm pressed against the rough top; blood marked it where he hauled. This time the near corner came round the projection.

"Mujhe kheench kar khada mat kijiye," Rudra said.

"Nahi kar rahi."

There was an edge in her answer. She needed him to hear the instruction she was giving, not the rescue he feared he would make necessary.

He let his left leg lengthen a little. The knee hurt when it reached the bend's last part, and he stopped short of that. Sitara moved one foot back, still on the middle, keeping her hold high on his arm. They shifted him a hand's breadth across the sloping surface.

His trousers caught. He stopped again.

She freed the cloth from the broken point instead of pulling harder through his shoulder.

The wall-end struck the projection.

A chip hit his sleeve. The whole piece could not pass through the gap at that angle. The man let its far end settle, stepped closer and reached over it.

Sitara saw the hand coming.

"Ab meri taraf. Neeche hi."

Rudra pushed from the good palm and the side of his left thigh. She guided rather than lifted. His right foot dragged over grit without finding a surface he dared stand on, and then his hip crossed the small ridge separating the broken shelf from the middle.

The man's fingers closed on empty shirt cloth left against the rock.

They tore it loose.

Rudra was on the middle, sitting badly, but no longer on the piece which was going.

---

Sitara put herself between his head and the reaching hand.

She did not try to stop the next blow with her palm. She ducked, caught Rudra at the upper back and helped him lower farther towards the return side of the projection. Knuckles struck rock above them. Dust fell into her hair.

For an instant the man had his forearm across the broken shoulder. His body remained beyond the grounded wall-end. To follow them he would have to go round it or move it again.

"Roop?" she asked, close enough not to shout.

"Nahi hai."

"Theek. Jo hai, usse chalte hain."

She meant his available hand, the strip beneath them, the next place to sit. He heard the relief inside the roughness: she was not asking him to find another body for her to bring home.

He spat blood to the side, away from her hand.

"Ankle par khada nahi ho paa raha."

"Toh abhi khade nahi honge."

Behind the shoulder, the man began dragging the wall out of his own way.

Sitara moved backwards on bent knees, keeping Rudra's upper body away from the fractured edge.

They could not make each movement small enough to be painless. The left knee pulled when he brought it closer; the right ankle sent a sharper warning when the heel caught. He named the sharper one. She changed the angle before trying again.

His good hand left a thin smear on the stone.

"Haath bhi chhil gaya," he said.

"Dekha. Wazan jangh par rehne do."

She shifted her hold to his upper back for the next short move. He kept the recovering wrist clear and the dressing against the collar. The sound of the wall being dragged followed them across the middle.

The turn towards the pinch was ahead.

A few feet could be a distance. He had learned that in rehabilitation without once imagining that somebody would be trying to cross the same feet behind him with a wall in his hands.

---

The man let the masonry drop.

Its near corner struck the narrow place he had been using to come round. The piece settled crookedly, high enough to block his direct step. He put one hand on it, leaned forward and tried to pull his leg over.

Sitara saw his foot find the wrong face of the stone and slip.

She used the interval.

"Agli chattan tak. Saath."

Rudra moved as she counted the effort aloud, not breaths, just the beginning and the end of one shared shift. The rock scraped his hip through the trousers. He kept his mouth open rather than bite down again on the torn cheek.

They reached the return side of the shoulder.

The man's hand struck the top behind them. A fragment broke free and clattered past Rudra's lowered head. Sitara bent over him until it stopped moving. Her own knuckles hit the rock; she swore once, quietly, then put the hand back where it could help.

He heard her breathing change.

"Aapka haath—"

"Laga hai. Chala sakti hoon. Tum ghoomo mat."

She flexed the fingers where she could see them, then changed the hold. It was an answer about this movement, not a promise to carry him through anything.

---

At the pinch, sitting squarely would leave his knees across the opening.

Sitara looked before asking him to turn. The middle still gave them a low strip on which to move one leg at a time. The broken outer side was behind them now; nobody needed to step out onto it to make the old photograph true.

She went through first on one knee, keeping her shoulder within his reach.

"Achhe haath se meri baanh. Kalai ko khaali rakho."

He held above her elbow with the scraped hand. He did not hang from it. His weight remained low, moving across the stone through his hip while she helped him keep his chest upright. The left knee came round only as far as it would go without the sharper pull. She waited while he changed the heel's position.

Behind the turn, the man climbed over the wall-end at last.

His landing shook loose grit from the shoulder.

"Ruko."

The first word he had given them was an ordinary human order.

Sitara did not answer it.

She helped Rudra move the right foot through the pinch, clear of its edge. He hissed when the support brushed stone. Then both legs were on the return side.

They could no longer see the man's whole body.

That was cover, not disappearance.

---

Something heavy struck the outer face of the turn.

The sound filled the passage. Rudra folded his head towards his chest; Sitara's sleeve came over the cheek cut, a brief shield against grit rather than the blow itself. A broken piece skidded into view and stopped against the opposite wall.

Another stone had come after them. They had seen neither its lifting nor its release from this side of the bend. The new scrape on the corner showed where its main weight had hit.

"Aage," Sitara said.

They moved before looking for a second piece.

The old shaft stood farther along their return, still leaning where nobody had put a hand to it. Sitara kept their awkward line away from its iron end. Rudra's elbow passed the rock beside it; he could see how little room there would be for a man trying to follow upright with another broad load.

The thought did not tell him the man would stop.

A foot scraped behind the pinch.

Sitara got Rudra past the next ordinary turn, where the passage floor became easier beneath his hip. She could see the last approach to Earth now.

Her voice had a place to go.

"Yahaan madad!" she called. "Rudra hai. Pair par wazan nahi le paa raha. Andar mat badhna—main dikha rahi hoon."

The mouth constable answered from the opening.

"Dono dikh rahe ho. Ruko, jagah banaate hain."

For the first time since the lip broke, somebody ahead of them had seen the help they needed.

The constable did not come past the last visible turn.

He lowered himself at the Earth opening and let Sitara show him the remaining movement. Rudra could bring his hip forward along the low floor if someone kept the right foot from catching. Sitara did that. The officer steadied his upper arm at the end, without taking the recovering wrist.

The first patch of Earth grit looked like all the grit before it.

Rudra knew the difference because the officer's phone was ringing.

He came fully out before anyone asked him to explain.

---

"Naya aadmi peechhe hai," Sitara said. "Bahut taaqat. Mod par patthar maara. Raasta band samajh kar mat chhodna."

The mouth constable called that to his partner, who was close enough on the Earth approach to hear. The road officer repeated the changed warning over his phone. Police help was still coming. A request had not put another pair of hands here yet.

The medical worker saw Rudra behind the outer cover.

Dhruv remained on the stretcher, leg supported, answering when asked. Vedant sat beside it with his forearm protected. The worker checked their immediate condition again before turning to the newly arrived patient; neither man was sent forward to make room by walking away.

The medical driver went back to the vehicle for a folding carry sheet and blankets.

Sitara stayed with Rudra at the low ground outside the mouth. The constable watched the opening from their side of the rock, lathi ready, with nobody mistaking it for something which had already stopped a wall.

Inside, a heavy scrape ended in a blow.

Dust came round the last bend. No man came with it yet.

"Ginti mat karna," Rudra muttered.

"Kiski?" Sitara asked.

"Kitni baar aapko waapas aana pada."

She looked at him long enough for him to stop trying to make it a joke.

"Abhi baahar ho. Idhar raho."

---

When the driver returned, they opened the sheet on the level Earth strip beside Rudra.

The clinical worker directed the short move onto it. Sitara stayed at the upper body; the driver supported the legs, and the officer helped at the near side while keeping the mouth in view. They stopped when the ankle's position changed sharply, adjusted the support and finished before lifting.

Rudra did not have to stand for the transfer.

The four carried him back behind the outer rock cover, farther from the opening, and lowered the sheet onto the folded blankets. The mouth constable returned to the view of the last bend. His road partner had kept the Earth approach clear during the move, not acquired the power to watch around the inside corner as well.

Only then did the worker ask Rudra for the account in order.

"Seedha saans le paa rahe ho? Chakkar? Behoshi hui?"

"Saans aa rahi hai. Behosh nahi hua. Muh mein khoon hai. Peechhe takkar lagi thi."

He pointed with the good hand instead of lifting his shoulders to prove where.

Sitara supplied what she had actually seen: the failed lip, short slide, hip impact, trapped heel and difficult return. The earlier blows belonged to Rudra's report. She did not improve the fall into a height she had never measured.

---

The worker checked his breathing, pulse and pressure, then examined the visible cuts and the places Rudra named.

Rudra stayed awake and answered. There was tenderness where his back had hit stone, pain through the guarded arm, and a shaking in the scraped hand which did not stop because it was being watched. His right ankle hurt too much for a trial stand. The left knee hurt when the position changed.

No one tried the stand.

The worker checked the right foot's circulation, movement and feeling with the support handled carefully, then arranged it again for protection. Those checks could not answer what an image and a fuller examination might show. The knee and hip needed assessment too.

The old collar dressing came away under the worker's hands.

The shallow place had opened again. Clean pressure replaced the damp fold Rudra had been holding. The cheek cut was cleaned and covered; the mouth was examined for the bleeding he kept tasting. He could point to the bitten place inside it. That did not make the back impact or the arm's pain disappear from the handover.

"Sab bata diya?" the worker asked.

Rudra looked at the scraped palm.

"Yeh bhi. Aur kalai ko body pakadne mein nahi lagaaya."

The worker checked it anyway.

---

The first vehicle had one stretcher place and the seated space its crew had already checked for Vedant and the accompanying worker.

It did not have another safe place for Rudra to lie with the leg protected.

The clinical worker called for a second medical vehicle, giving the exact ordinary road approach, Rudra's present findings and the armed threat. The receiving facility got the changed patient count. Dhruv and Vedant's first vehicle would not leave until there was an actual plan for Rudra's care here.

The original car driver called Sitara.

"Do minute bache hain," he said. "Phir kaise karna hai?"

She answered at once.

The two minutes belonged to him even while somebody else's emergency was using the road.

Sitara told the driver exactly what had changed.

Rudra was out, injured and under the medical worker's hands. Another medical vehicle had been requested. The man inside had followed as far as the bends, and heavy impacts were still audible. No one would ask the car to block the opening or its driver to help carry a patient.

"Mere paas se road abhi saaf hai," he said. "Par main gaadi yahaan se andar nahi laaunga."

"Nahi laani."

She checked the remaining transport money against the amounts already reserved for care and other people's work. They could pay for one more ten-minute waiting interval at the agreed rate, with the original return still included. She offered that, not a promise that the danger would politely end before his clock did.

He agreed to the extra ten minutes beyond the present two, only while the road behind his bend remained open. If it ceased to be clear, he would leave and tell the officer from farther out. The constable heard the arrangement.

Sitara repeated the new limit and put it in the phone's note beside the booking.

The driver stayed beyond the bend, facing out.

He had not become their wall.

---

Dhruv watched the worker check the collar covering again.

"Khoon ruk raha hai?" he asked.

"Dabaav se kam hai," the worker answered. "Phir check karunga."

It was a smaller answer than the one all of them wanted. Rudra could use it. He did not have to decide whether the man meant a lifetime when he was describing a dressing.

Dhruv's shin had started aching more now that he was still. He said so. The worker checked the leg's position and the support; the report stayed in the account for the receiving team.

Vedant had stopped testing his fingers. They lay where the supported forearm allowed them to lie.

"Paani khatam ho gaya tha," he said to Rudra.

"Dekha tha."

"Phir bhi haath utha diya. Aadat se."

He looked angry at the memory, not pleased with a teacher's example. Rudra remembered the dry hollow and the empty hand lowering. Knowing what to do had not kept either of them from beginning the wrong movement.

"Maine bhi uthaya tha," Rudra said.

Vedant looked at the new dressing.

"Bheja nahi."

The teacher nodded once. Neither of them made the unseen street beyond the man empty by approving of the refusal.

---

Sitara asked Rudra whom she could update while the worker finished.

"Teenon ko bas itna—nikal aaye. Chot hai. Check chal raha hai. Unko yahaan aane ko mat kehna."

"Main nahi keh rahi."

He gave permission for his own account, not Dhruv's or Vedant's findings. She asked the two men separately before adding that they too were receiving care. Her message to the three friends named the changed task, not a successfully completed visit: photographs stopped, new attack, Rudra and all three mentors outside, transport being arranged.

No image went with it.

Veer called first. Sitara kept the conversation short and out of the worker's way. Rudra could hear his friend's voice when she offered the phone.

"Jhooth waala theek mat bolna."

"Ankle bahut dard kar rahi hai. Muh kata hai. Hosh mein hoon."

A pause.

"Haan. Yeh theek answer hai. Main apna check chhod kar nahi aa raha. Phone idhar hai."

Rudra shut his eyes for a moment after the call ended. It was easier to hear the road without looking for Veer on it.

---

Aadhya's reply came as words for Sitara to read, not a demand for a picture of blood. She wanted to know whether the receiving team had been called. Ira asked whether Rudra wanted his parents contacted for him or preferred to speak after the immediate checks.

He chose the latter.

"Unko khud bolunga. Jhooth nahi. Bas pehle yeh ho jaaye."

The worker was arranging the ankle's support and asking about a change in the tingling near his toes. Rudra answered that question before anything else. The worker checked again and altered the padding where it pressed; the new sensation eased, and its appearance went into the handover anyway.

Sitara put the phone away.

Her knuckles were swelling slightly. The worker saw them when she helped hold a blanket clear, cleaned the small split and covered it. She could move the fingers; persistent pain or a change in use would need further review. She was told not to volunteer that hand for another heavy lift.

She accepted the instruction without hiding it from the others.

A sound came from the mouth, a scrape interrupted by three uneven blows.

The constable moved back along the outside cover.

"Andar waala kona toot raha hai," he called. "Sabko road ke mod ke peechhe le chalo. Abhi."

This time there was no argument about whether the old route could still be called open.

They moved Rudra first.

Sitara did not put the newly covered hand back on a heavy handle. The two constables took those places with the medical driver and worker, under the worker's directions. For the short carry, Sitara stood where she could see the Earth opening and the clear approach, calling any change rather than trying to hold either with her body.

"Abhi koi baahar nahi," she said.

They lifted the sheet together.

The blankets and protected leg moved with Rudra. A jolt through the knee made him speak; they shortened the next step and kept going, away from the opening rather than back towards a flatter place near it. The officer at the rear could still look over his shoulder. Nobody went inside to discover whether the next blow was ready.

The road bend took the mouth out of Rudra's sight.

On the far side, the ambulance stood in the widening beside the approach, facing the clear outward road. The car was farther out, with room to leave. They lowered Rudra onto the blankets on the firm strip beside the medical vehicle, off the running surface.

The worker checked his position after the carry.

The mouth constable went straight back to his outside cover. His partner resumed the road, and Sitara left the temporary watch when he told her he had it.

The change of places was spoken aloud.

---

The medical driver brought Dhruv next.

The stretcher's wheels were released only for the move. He rolled it along the Earth strip already used to bring the equipment, keeping the supported leg clear at the uneven patch. The clinical worker watched the patient and the support, stopping the movement before a blanket corner could go under a wheel.

Vedant waited seated until Sitara returned for him.

He rose on his own legs with the forearm held close. She guided him with the unhurt hand at the upper arm, not a grip through the injured forearm and not his full weight. They took the short approach in stages. At the bend he needed a pause for his breathing to settle after the effort; she waited, watching his face rather than the vehicle.

"Chakkar nahi," he said. "Bas rukna hai."

They stopped until he was ready for the remaining steps.

Dhruv's stretcher went into its checked place in the vehicle. The driver secured it. The worker rechecked the leg's support and the restraint before helping Vedant to the belted seat available to him.

There was room for the worker to accompany them when they left.

Rudra would need the other vehicle.

---

Rudra could see Dhruv through the open rear.

He had not known until now that he needed to see where the older man's injured leg had gone. At the mouth, everything below the knee had become a dark patch and somebody else's report. Here it was supported, covered and attached to a man who was complaining that the blanket had caught his sound foot.

The driver freed it.

Dhruv thanked him. Then he looked across at Rudra without lifting himself against the restraints.

"Tumhaara number aa raha hai," he said.

He meant the second vehicle.

Rudra almost laughed. The torn cheek turned the beginning of it into a wince.

The worker came back to him, checked the collar covering and asked about the tingling. It had not returned after the padding changed. The hand still shook a little. Rudra gave both facts, not only the better one.

The receiving call was updated again. The second crew had acknowledged the road approach and the need to stop behind the bend until the officer directed them. They were travelling; they were not yet here.

The first crew stayed.

---

Sitara sat on the car's open side seat for a moment, feet on Earth, leaving the door out of the road.

The driver showed her the start of the newly agreed waiting interval. There had been no invisible extra promise between that time and the next. She confirmed it and asked him to keep the exit as his choice, not hers.

He nodded and stayed at his own place.

She closed the door after rising. Her knees felt the repeated crouching when she stood. The covered knuckles throbbed, and there was dust in the place beneath her tongue where she could not seem to swallow it away.

Rudra watched her come back.

"Aap bhi baithiye."

"Baithe the."

"Doosri baar bhi allowed hai."

She lowered herself beside the blankets, keeping the sore hand on her thigh. For a little while neither of them supplied a useful sentence. The driver and the worker had patients to check; the officers had a road and an opening. Sitara let those jobs remain with the people doing them.

---

At the mouth, the constable heard a dragging sound stop just beyond the final turn.

He had withdrawn from the opening to the outside shoulder, where the rock hid most of his body. He could see only a sliver of the inner wall. Dust sifted across it, slowly enough for him to distinguish it from the heavier chips which had come before.

"Baahar mat aana," he called. "Police hai. Haath dikhaao."

A hand appeared on the visible edge.

There was blood across its knuckles and a larger scrape along the arm. The fingers opened, found another hold and closed. A second hand joined it lower down. The stone between the two grips made a hard ticking sound.

The officer backed away from the projecting corner above the approach.

He called the warning to his road partner before taking the next step. Behind the bend, the patients could be moved only so quickly, and he needed that movement to begin before he lost the view.

The ticking became a crack.

The upper grip shifted. Grit poured over the man's wrist, then stopped because a wider piece had moved against it.

The constable put his heel back towards the clear Earth strip.

The corner broke loose above his backward step.
