# ANANTA — BOOK 06 — MERGED

# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND ONE — THE HELP SHE HAD NOT ASKED FOR

---

> *"She had asked for the needle to be threaded.*
> *Getting her own work back took longer."*
> *— Ira's later register, first part of Sitara's account*

---

"Haan," Sitara said. "Sui waapas maangni padi thi."

Aadhya looked at her.

"Silai kar rahi thi. Dhaaga daalne mein madad maangi thi. Doosri aurat ne poora kaam le liya."

The answer arrived without a wound which needed unveiling. A needle, a piece of cloth, a request which had grown larger in somebody else's hands. Aadhya had been prepared to hear about a student or a household in trouble. She had not expected the woman beside them to have had difficulty getting a small task returned.

"De di?"

"Maangi tab. Usse pehle main hi baithi rahi, shukriya kehti hui."

Ira listened with the ordinary eye at rest. The cloth on the table remained where she had wanted it. Aadhya could see how easily its small relocation might have become a cleared surface, a reorganized room, a place made beautifully convenient for everyone except the person who had learned where things were.

She wanted the rest of the account.

Sitara said it would take more than the few minutes before their care. She could tell them later if they still wanted it; there was no need for this evening's question to borrow time from either girl's night.

They said they wanted to hear, not that their next day had already agreed to the arrangement.

---

The nurse came when expected.

Aadhya returned to her own room with help. No one asked the hands to carry the folded support merely because the object was light enough to look inconsiderable. Ira had the light and quiet she needed. Sitara went to the work which remained hers, leaving neither girl responsible for keeping her company until it was done.

At the next check, Aadhya described a patch of uncomfortable dryness and the altered feeling which made it hard to tell whether one position was pressing too much. The nurse looked, attended to what needed attention and settled the hands without asking Aadhya to prove the discomfort by reproducing it.

It eased. The strange feeling did not disappear with it.

Later, waking, she wanted to pull the sheet higher and used the help within reach. She was annoyed that something so ordinary could still require another person. The annoyance was allowed to survive the thank-you.

She slept again before morning.

---

Day ninety began with the hands, not the story.

The review took account of the night, the small ordinary uses allowed the previous day and the places where feeling remained unreliable. Aadhya could still make the light movements which had been comfortable. That was not a harder grip, an invitation to keep trying until the fingers grew accurate, or a return to training.

With the therapist, she moved a little cloth across the supported surface and stopped when the work began to change its ease. She described the sensation rather than guessing what answer would keep the next permission available.

Afterwards she ate and rested.

Ira had her own examination and care. The left remained covered; the ordinary right eye had its short comfortable intervals and its rests. She wanted to listen to Sitara but did not want to make another move between rooms just to put the listening in the same place.

A short audio call could do what a gathering could not usefully improve.

---

The suggestion reached Aadhya after rest, and she liked it.

She could remain with her supports in her own room. Ira could keep the light as she needed in hers. Sitara would sit with Aadhya; the phone could carry the voice between them without asking either girl to keep a screen in view.

They checked that each wanted this now, and that the interval fitted the care still due. The phone was placed where it could be heard without a stretch. The call was private; nobody had begun recording it or added the boys because four friends were accustomed to sharing a room.

Ira said she could hear. Aadhya said the hands were comfortable. Sitara asked whether they wanted the account from the beginning rather than from the sentence about the needle.

They did.

For a moment there was only the faint sound of another room through the speaker: something being set down, a door closing with care, Ira asking for the volume to stay where it was.

Then Sitara began, not with the things she had learned to teach, but with a cotton bag she had been trying to mend on an ordinary Earth afternoon.

The seam had opened near a lower corner.

The bag was not beautiful. It had carried vegetables, books and once a container which had not been as securely closed as she had believed. Washing had left one pale stain. The corner needed a patch inside before she closed the outer seam again; otherwise the next ordinary load would pull against the same worn place.

She had turned it inside out and laid the patch ready.

There was light enough to see the cloth, but the end of the thread had begun to fray. Sitara cut it clean, tried again and missed the needle's eye. A woman visiting the house was sitting closer to the verandah's open edge.

Sitara asked whether she would put the thread through for her.

"Do," the woman said.

She did it at once.

"Lo, yeh bhi main kar deti hoon."

The needle went into the fold before Sitara had taken it back.

---

At first the work looked so much easier from the other woman's hands that Sitara smiled.

She thanked her for threading the needle. The woman made another stitch, caught the fold neatly and began working along it. Her stitches were small and capable. Sitara had no wish to pretend otherwise merely because she had begun to want the work returned.

The patch remained on the floor beside her.

She picked it up, expecting that the movement would explain what she had not yet said. The woman glanced at it and went on sewing. A piece of cloth in a person's hand did not tell another person the whole plan for using it.

Sitara saw that plainly enough later.

At the time, she thought she had somehow made her intention visible and the other woman had chosen not to notice.

That small unfairness belonged in the account too.

---

Ira's voice came through the speaker.

"Aapne patch ke baare mein bola nahi?"

"Abhi tak nahi. Bola hota toh samajhne mein madad milti. Par maanga dhaaga hi tha, poora kaam nahi."

"Unko laga hoga aap khush hain," Aadhya said.

"Haan. Main muskura bhi rahi thi. Isi liye toh kehna zaroori tha. Mera chehra meri poori baat nahi keh raha tha."

She did not ask them to decide that one of the two women had secretly intended harm. The mistake could be understood without being made necessary, or made entirely the fault of the person who had not managed to object quickly enough.

A request for a small help had been answered. Another task had been taken up without a clear answer from the person whose task it was.

The two things had happened close together. They were still two things.

---

In the remembered verandah, Sitara tried saying that she had thought of putting the patch inside.

"Laga denge," the woman replied. "Pehle yeh toh ho jaaye."

The answer was not foolish. Another way of repairing the corner could work. They might have discussed the order of it and chosen hers instead. What made Sitara uncomfortable was not that she alone knew how a bag must be mended.

She had wanted to mend this one.

She had set aside a little time, found the cloth, opened the weak seam far enough to see what lay around it. The work was hers in a quieter sense than ownership of the object. She had wanted the ordinary satisfaction of making something useful again with her own attention.

She had not asked to be relieved of that attention.

A cup clinked somewhere inside. Somebody wanted to know whether the strainer had been washed. The household went on having an afternoon around them. There was no audience waiting to judge whether Sitara could make an adequate boundary. That had not stopped her imagining one.

The visiting woman drew another length of thread through the cloth.

---

Aadhya shifted the supported hands. Sitara noticed and waited while she found whether the position needed actual help.

It did. Aadhya used the call and said what she wanted changed. The nurse checked, adjusted and made sure the new position was comfortable before going on.

The phone stayed within hearing. Ira did not have to ask what secret event she had missed in the pause; Aadhya told her it had been a position change and that she was settled.

Then she wanted the account to continue.

What interested her was not the correct seam. She could imagine somebody taking a bowl, a bag, a small piece of work from her hands now and being certain that speed had settled every question worth asking.

It was harder to imagine herself objecting without first making the person feel perfectly good about being stopped.

"Bura lagne ka darr tha?" she asked.

Sitara said there had been that. There had also been the pleasure of looking like somebody who received kindness gracefully.

She had liked that picture of herself.

For several stitches, she had liked it more than she liked admitting what she wanted.

She had not found a beautiful sentence.

The first thing she said was that the bag could be left with her now. The words came out so lightly that the woman answered them as an offer to spare her trouble.

"Arey, ho jaayega. Tum baitho."

Sitara was already sitting. She remembered that with a small, unreasonable irritation. Even the place where her body happened to be had become an instruction about what it should continue doing while somebody else finished her work.

She put the patch down where both could see it.

"Sui mujhe do," she said. "Baaki main karoongi."

The other woman looked up.

"Kuchh galat kar diya?"

"Nahi. Silai theek hai. Main khud karna chahti hoon."

There was the difficulty, stripped of the excuse Sitara had almost wanted. If the stitches had been bad, she could have asked for the work back while making the repair itself responsible. They were good. The other woman's hands had not failed at the task she had taken.

Sitara still wanted it returned.

---

She began to explain the patch, the corner, the use the bag would get. Some of the explanation was useful. Some was an attempt to find an amount of detail which would make the other person stop without ever feeling stopped.

The woman heard her out, then said she could do it that way too.

Sitara almost agreed.

It would have been simpler for the next few minutes. There would have been a repaired bag at the end, and two women spared the inconvenience of acknowledging that their kindnesses had not fitted together as neatly as the stitches.

She could imagine accepting it, praising it and being cross afterwards in a way the other woman would have no fair chance to understand.

She did not want that afternoon either.

"Mujhe tumhaari silai theek nahi karni," she said. "Mujhe apna kaam karna hai."

The woman held the cloth a moment longer.

"Main toh madad kar rahi thi."

"Dhaaga daalne mein hui. Uske liye shukriya. Ab sui mujhe do."

It was not a louder voice. It was an answer which stopped moving out of its own way.

---

Aadhya had been expecting a quarrel to begin at the next stitch.

Instead, Sitara described the woman drawing the thread through the stitch already started. The needle came free. She secured it in a spare fold so neither of them had to catch a loose point, then put the bag between them.

Sitara took it.

There was no snatching, no injured finger, no sudden revelation that the other woman had been cruel all along. She had stopped. The work had come back. Those were real changes, even though the verandah did not immediately become comfortable again.

"Phir?" Ira asked.

"Phir kuchh der bahut shaanti thi. Achhi waali nahi."

Aadhya smiled despite herself.

Sitara smiled too. She could tell the truth of the quiet without insisting it had secretly been a triumph. The other woman moved her hands into her lap. Inside the house, somebody found the strainer. The ordinary afternoon had not arranged a gentler noise to cover what had happened.

Sitara turned the bag towards herself and found that she was holding the patch too tightly.

She loosened the fingers before she began.

---

"Sorry bola?" Aadhya asked.

"Honthon tak aaya tha. Baar-baar."

There had been an apology worth making for letting her own annoyance grow while hoping another person would guess it. There had not been a reason to apologize for wanting the needle back. In the moment, the two had been difficult to keep apart.

She had said that she should have spoken earlier about the patch. She had not said she should never have interrupted the sewing.

The distinction had not repaired the whole afternoon.

Ira was quiet for a while. Through the speaker, Aadhya heard the slight sound of her shifting against the pillow, then a request for the line to remain as it was. She was comfortable; she wanted a moment to think without the quiet being mistaken for the call dropping.

Sitara let the moment be a moment.

When Ira spoke again, she said she had expected the story to become about learning to accept help. It was strange to hear that accepting the help could be the part which had happened easily.

"Ek cheez mein li thi," Sitara said. "Doosri mein nahi chaahiye thi. Dono jawaab ek saath ho sakte hain."

---

Aadhya looked at the hands on their supports.

She could not settle what they were ready to do by wanting a task to remain hers. The morning's review, the actual feeling and the people attending to both were not obstacles which a good sentence could remove.

But there were still choices which belonged to her inside the help she needed.

She could ask what was happening before someone changed a position. She could say that a thing within reach mattered where it was. She could want the quiet without wishing the person who had helped her into it had never come.

None of that required her to become an easier patient than she actually was.

Sitara was not holding a needle now. There was nothing for the girls to watch her do correctly and copy. She had given them an account in which she had wanted something ordinary, hesitated, spoken poorly, tried again and received only part of the relief she had hoped the words would bring.

Aadhya found it more useful than a perfect refusal.

The first stitch Sitara made after getting the bag back was crooked.

She told them that before Aadhya could imagine a pair of perfectly vindicated hands producing the repair the afternoon had been waiting for. The thread caught a little of the outer cloth which she had meant to leave free. She had to stop, ease it back and begin again.

The other woman could see.

"Mushkil wahaan hui," Sitara said. "Apna kaam waapas lekar galti bhi apni hi dikh rahi thi."

Aadhya laughed softly. She understood the unfair wish for the first attempt after an objection to be beyond criticism.

There had been a temptation to hide the stitch inside the fold and hurry on. Nobody would be hurt. It would probably hold. It would also leave the patch pulling in a way she had specifically wanted to avoid.

She unpicked it.

The woman said the edge could be flattened a little more. Sitara looked, saw that it could and used the suggestion.

Accepting that piece of advice did not hand the needle back.

---

A sound from Aadhya's phone announced a message.

She could see enough of the screen to know it was Rudra, but not the rest without shifting towards it. She asked Sitara to read his message aloud. Ira heard the request through the call and said she could wait while Aadhya answered.

He was asking whether she wanted a little company later, if the evening's care allowed. He had not come to the door first and made the question harder to answer.

Aadhya wanted the quiet evening she had been picturing before the message arrived.

She was glad he had asked. The gladness did not create the wish to see him now.

"Aaj nahi," she dictated. "Thak gayi hoon. Kal phir poochhna."

Sitara read it back before sending. Aadhya said yes to those words, without an invented explanation about the hands having become worse.

The message went. There was no answer yet. Rudra could be resting, occupied with his own care or deciding what to say. She did not need to fill the space with another message which made her first answer easier to mistake for an invitation after all.

She asked for the story to continue.

---

In the verandah, Sitara had turned the bag again to reach the next side of the patch.

The other woman had got up to fetch tea. She asked whether Sitara wanted some. Sitara did. The cups arrived and one was set within reach; nobody announced that the women had now demonstrated the correct way to be with each other.

For a while they drank.

Then the woman said, "Pehle bol deti."

"Haan," Sitara had answered. "Bolna chaahiye tha."

She did not add that the other woman could have asked before beginning. It was true, but she was tired of the conversation turning towards which single person must hold all of it. She had already asked for the sewing back. The woman had already returned it. There were other things between them which would not be made simpler by treating the bag as a verdict.

She did not know then whether the visit would feel ordinary again before it ended.

Telling it now did not give her access to what the other woman had thought while making the tea. She could remember the cups and the sentence. Anything more about the silence would have been her own reading of it.

---

Ira asked whether she could keep an account of what Sitara had chosen to tell.

"Haan. Jo suna hai, woh," Sitara said. "Meri baat jahaan yaad na rahe, apni taraf se mere shabd mat banaana. Poochh lena."

Ira said she wanted to use her own words, with the distinction clear. She could do it later with the ordinary help she needed, not spend the rested eye trying to make a record now.

The permission covered a told afternoon. It did not give her an explanation for the woman who had arrived at the haveli, a right to fill the years around the bag or a claim on every memory Sitara had kept.

She had not asked for those things.

Aadhya noticed how different a story felt when it did not offer itself as a complete account of the person sitting in front of her. Sitara was there, able to be asked what she meant, not trapped inside the remembered verandah until a listener declared her fully understood.

---

The tea cooled while Sitara worked.

The patch lay flatter now. The stitches were not as neat as the other woman's, but the cloth did not bunch when she turned the corner. She could see what needed doing next and had the needle in her hand to do it.

Then the far edge slipped.

It was an ordinary awkwardness. She could hold one part steady, or turn the bag so the light fell where she needed it, but doing both without pulling the patch out of place would be easier with another hand.

The woman was still there.

Sitara could see her cup, her fingers resting around it, the place beside her which had not become farther away in any distance a floor could measure.

The person who had wanted her work back had not stopped needing help.

Aadhya asked whether needing the hand again had made Sitara wish she had let the other woman finish everything.

"Us pal laga tha," Sitara said. "Apni baat pe tikne ka matlab shaayad ab sab kuchh akele karna padega."

"Unhone kaha?"

"Nahi. Yeh maine apne aap se kaha. Unki baat samajhke."

She had been looking at a cup and a pair of resting hands while composing an answer in the other woman's mouth. Perhaps the woman would be cross. Perhaps she would hold the cloth readily. Sitara had not yet found out, but her embarrassment had begun speaking as though finding out would merely confirm what she deserved.

She remembered being annoyed with the bag.

That made Ira laugh through the speaker, an unexpected small sound which lightened neither the cloth nor the difficulty. It simply let the ridiculous part be ridiculous.

"Usse toh kone pe hi phatna tha," Sitara said. "Mere swabhaav ka hisaab karke nahi."

---

A reply arrived on Aadhya's phone.

She asked Sitara to check it. Rudra had said that was fine, he would ask again tomorrow. He hoped she could rest. There was no request that she replace the company with a longer explanation of why she did not want it.

She felt the relief before she could make it more dignified.

It did not tell her what he would feel about every future refusal. It told her he had heard this one. He had not come to the doorway to see whether being physically present might change it.

She asked for a simple thank-you to be sent, then left the evening as she had chosen it. Gratitude did not need a little invitation attached to make it honest.

Ira heard the exchange. She said she might want a quiet evening too and would give her own answer if she was asked, rather than allowing Aadhya's no to stand in for both of them.

"Mere naam pe chhutti mat lena," Aadhya said.

"Tumhaari chhutti tumhe mubaarak. Meri main loongi."

Sitara smiled, then waited until they wanted the account again.

---

What she had told them so far was enough to change the picture of the verandah.

The visitor had not failed because she lacked kindness or skill. Sitara had not failed because she still needed another hand after asking for the needle back. A patch had to lie against worn cloth. Two people had wanted the ordinary satisfaction of being useful, and neither person's wish made the other's disappear.

That did not make their positions identical. The bag was Sitara's task to give or keep. The other woman was still entitled to decide whether she wanted to hold the edge now; getting the work back did not place the helper's hands permanently at her disposal in a smaller role.

"Phir woh bhi na keh sakti thi," Ira said.

"Haan."

"Achha nahi lagta."

"Shaayad nahi. Phir bhi poochhna aur kaam de dena alag hain."

Aadhya could feel how quickly a useful sentence might become a way of demanding a perfectly willing world. The right to ask did not produce the answer. It made an honest answer possible, including one which left a bag unfinished for a while.

---

Sitara checked the time before continuing.

The girls' next care would begin soon. There were still a few minutes, not the rest of an afternoon which could be taken because a story had become interesting.

Ira said she was comfortable listening for those minutes. Aadhya wanted them too. Sitara did not ask for a promise to hear the rest tonight.

Outside Aadhya's room, a worker asked whether a bucket could be left near a wall while the farther stretch of floor dried. Another voice asked for it to be put clear of the turn. It was moved. Aadhya heard the little scrape stop, and the sound of work continuing.

The world had not become better at asking because the three were talking about it. People were already making room, forgetting, being reminded and making room again in ordinary ways which did not wait for a teacher's account.

Aadhya liked being able to hear them.

---

She looked at the light cloth she had moved with the therapist that morning, now put aside where it did not tempt the fingers into another trial.

The small movement had been hers. So had the help which made it possible to stop when the ease changed. She did not need to choose a version of the day in which only one of those facts could be true.

Across the call, Ira asked for no change to the light in her own room. Someone acknowledged her and went on. Aadhya could not see the room, but she had heard the request and its answer. For this part of the afternoon, that was enough to know.

The remembered bag remained between the two women in Sitara's account, the needle returned, the far edge loose. Aadhya could picture the distance to the other pair of hands. She no longer pictured it as a distance a brave person could abolish by deciding never to need them again.

Getting her work back had not been the whole difficulty. It had brought Sitara to another beginning, with the same person still beside her and no guarantee that a clearer request would feel easier to make.

Aadhya looked at the woman who had once sat with that unfinished corner in her lap.

"Uske baad aap usi se dobara madad maang paayin?"

# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWO — THE HAND BESIDE HERS

---

> *"She asked for the same hand again.*
> *This time, they agreed on what it would hold."*
> *— Ira's later register, conclusion of Sitara's first account*

---

"Maangi," Sitara said. "Ussi se. Bas pehle se thoda saaf kaha ki chaahiye kya."

Aadhya waited, her hands still resting where the nurse had settled them. Through the phone, Ira made the small sound which meant she was listening. They were in two rooms, not one, with a few minutes left before their separate care; Sitara did not borrow another afternoon to answer the question.

In the remembered verandah, she had put the needle down before speaking. The far edge of the patch had slipped again. She could have folded the whole bag under her elbow and finished, badly, while proving an independence nobody had actually demanded.

Instead she asked the woman beside her to hold that edge.

"Yeh kona. Bas seedha rehne dena. Main sil loongi."

The woman looked at the cloth and then at the cup in her hand.

"Chai toh peene do."

"Haan."

Sitara had wanted an immediate yes, and was embarrassed to discover how quickly waiting could feel like punishment when she was already afraid of having offended someone. The woman had not punished her. She had asked to finish her tea.

Sitara let her.

---

She worked on the nearer part while she waited, keeping the loose edge free rather than stitching it into the wrong place to save face. The other woman drank the last of her tea, set down the cup and asked which corner.

Sitara showed her.

"Aise?"

"Thoda dheela. Haan. Ab theek."

The cloth stopped pulling. Sitara could turn the needle where she needed it. The woman held the edge instead of taking the sewing, and when the patch needed turning they turned it together.

There were several small corrections. Too tight. A little nearer. Wait, the thread had caught underneath.

None of them became an argument about who was good at helping.

"Sirf pakadne mein bhi kaam tha," Sitara said. "Main pehle aise keh rahi thi jaise kuchh karne ko diya hi nahi."

The other woman had noticed the weak cloth beside the patch before Sitara did. She pointed it out. They moved the next stitch inwards. A useful suggestion could enter the work without the whole work changing hands again.

When the corner was secure, Sitara said she no longer needed it held.

The woman let go.

---

"Phir sab theek?" Aadhya asked.

"Bag ka kona theek hua tha. Hum dono ko har baat ek hi din mein thodi aa gayi."

There had still been a little awkwardness. Sitara remembered thanking her too formally and the woman telling her not to speak as though a receipt had to be issued. They laughed then, not because the earlier discomfort had been imaginary, but because both could hear how hard they were trying to make an ordinary afternoon behave properly.

The bag returned to ordinary use. The patch held. Later, something else wore out. Sitara did not have it now, or a final claim to make for the two women from the fact that they had once managed to mend it.

What she kept was the knowledge that she could ask again without surrendering the first request to have her work returned.

The other person could answer again too.

It was not a way of ensuring that every helping hand stayed available. It was a way of finding out whether this one was, for this part, now.

---

Ira said she had nearly made the wrong ending for the account: Sitara getting the needle back and finishing alone.

"Maine bhi pehle wahi socha tha," Sitara said.

Aadhya looked at her own supported hands. The woman was not offering a test of whether she could now use them unaided. A bag could wait on a lap. A hand which needed checking still needed the people able to check it. What had become clearer was not a new physical freedom, but the fact that receiving help did not require every choice to be handed over with it.

"Madad chhoti nahi thi," Ira said. "Bas kaam alag tha."

"Haan."

Sitara glanced at the time. She had given the answer, the awkward waiting, the actual work together and its ordinary end. There was no last secret to earn by remaining on the call after the girls needed to stop.

The nurse came to Aadhya's doorway.

"Ab?" Sitara asked.

Aadhya said yes. Ira was ready to end too.

The call finished. Sitara moved the phone out of the way of the care, checked that the call for help remained within reach and left the room when the nurse needed the space. In Ira's room, the quieter light stayed as she had asked.

The story was complete. The afternoon still had other people's work inside it.

Rudra did not visit that evening.

He had meant his answer to Aadhya's message. It was a small promise to keep, without an audience, and he found that he could keep it without spending the whole evening feeling noble about the empty place beside his chair.

There was food. There was a position which had become uncomfortable before he admitted it. There was a nurse who wanted the actual location of the pull, not his general opinion that it would settle.

He gave her the location.

After the adjustment, he listened to a voice message Veer had sent about the indignities of a biscuit which could survive tea without absorbing any of it. He replied that this sounded like the biscuit was making a sensible medical decision.

Veer's answering laugh was followed by a complaint from the ribs and a shorter message saying he was stopping there.

They stopped.

---

Night passed in separate rooms.

Aadhya needed the hand supports changed once and said which part felt different. Ira had the quiet and ordinary eye rest in her plan. Rudra slept, woke to a knee which wanted a different angle and accepted the help before trying to make the angle change by borrowing strength from the braced wrist.

In the morning, the accounts reached the people examining them.

Day ninety-one did not begin with a declaration that the story had made the four ready for something larger. Rudra's therapist asked about the previous efforts and the night, checked the ankle and knee, and kept the wrist out of the work. The short assisted transfer was easier at the turn once the chair stood closer. That did not make the hand suddenly available for pushing.

Veer's movement was considered separately. The aid and nearby seat remained; a quieter knee did not give the shoulder permission to bear another person's weight. Aadhya's light movements stayed light. Ira's left eye remained covered and the ordinary right had the rests it still needed.

They ate. They had time in which nothing had to be learned from one another.

---

Later, Rudra sent the promised question again.

Aadhya wanted a short conversation after rest. Audio was enough. They did not need to arrange a transfer merely to make yesterday's no look temporary in the correct way.

When they spoke, she asked whether he had liked the court.

He told her about choosing the chair's place and then almost spoiling the choice by spending half the time checking that he was enjoying it properly. She said the bird had not appeared concerned about that.

"Uska therapist nahi tha," he said.

"Uske ghutne bhi tumse achhe the."

"Personal hone ki zaroorat nahi thi."

She laughed. He could hear her settle afterwards, not the exact movement or whether she had shifted the left hand first. He asked if she wanted to stop.

Not yet. She had a little time.

---

She said Sitara had told them an ordinary sewing account. It had made her think about asking for a little help without giving away the whole task.

She did not supply the other woman's words as if a story told to her could simply be issued to the boys. Rudra heard Aadhya's own thought, in the size she chose to give it.

"Tumhe sunaana chaahengi toh poochh loongi," she said.

He would like that, if it fitted everyone's day. He did not need the account immediately, or the private conversation which had led to it.

Their call ended while both still had something easy to say. No one used the remaining words as a reason to stretch the interval.

Afterwards, Aadhya asked Sitara whether she was willing to tell the bounded account to the boys. Sitara was. She preferred to tell it herself, from the beginning, with time for a question, rather than have the girls try to carry an exact transcript through their own care.

Ira wanted to listen again if the afternoon allowed, but did not want an eye-rest interval converted into a note-taking appointment. Aadhya wanted to hear what the boys asked. Veer was asked separately and said he would like to listen after his rest.

The arrangement waited for the actual afternoon.

---

By then the care due had been given, the food eaten, the available help checked.

A short private call let the four remain in their own supported places. Sitara sat where the microphone could carry her voice without raising it. Each person confirmed they could hear and wanted this interval now. There would be no practical exercise attached to it.

He told the attendant where the ankle support had shifted in the jolt. There was no time for the careful comfort of the room, but the man could keep the leg clear of the wheel while moving them back. Rudra accepted that smaller help.

Ahead, Dhruv knocked another cut aside. The sound was ugly and close.

Behind the low wall on their right, stone grated under a different foot.

Rudra turned towards it.

He saw the edge of a second man's clothes moving through the gap they had looked past on the way in. This man was not facing the first attacker.

He was looking towards the broad turning place.

"Peechhe," Rudra said. "Mod pe doosra aadmi."

The attendant looked before pushing the chair another length. That look kept them from arriving at the turning place as though it were still empty.

The second man carried a longer staff with an iron ferrule. He stepped out of the gap, crossed the visible strip of broken ground and took the line towards the rock bend. No wall opened for him. No darkness carried him from somewhere Rudra could not see. He used a piece of ground the visitors had not walked.

The first attacker called something sharply.

"Peechhe roko!"

The second man planted his staff across the approach to the bend.

There was an answer in the movement which needed no name for the men. The first throw had not merely tried to hurt whoever stood nearest. While the group turned, somebody else had gone for the way they would use to leave.

Sitara stopped before the staff reached Veer's legs.

Ira stopped with her. Veer set the aid where it could take the weight the knee would not confidently take alone. Sitara had Ira following one forearm; her other hand steadied Veer for the moment he needed to settle.

Aadhya kept her body clear of both, close enough to see the staff and say where it lay. She did not put the hands between iron and a friend's knee.

The man looked from the aid to the covered eye, then at the chair coming towards him more slowly than the people with it wanted.

"Yahin," he said.

He struck the ferrule against stone.

It was not a warning which made an injury elsewhere. It made a sound. The danger was the arm holding the staff, the distance it could reach and the feet already standing where the return line narrowed towards the bend.

Aadhya could see those things.

"Aage nahi," she told the attendant. "Daaye haath mein lamba hai."

He brought the chair short of the sweep.

---

Rudra wanted the sound of the other fight to stop.

Behind him, blade and stolen shaft struck together again. Dhruv had not been left unharmed by the work of protecting the group; one blow forced the tender hand against the shaft before he could draw it back. Rudra heard the breath leave him. The grip on that side did not return.

He changed the angle of his body instead, keeping the sound hand and the length of the shaft between the blade and the boy.

"Ghoomo mat abhi," he called. "Jitni jagah hai, wahi rakho."

There was no request to win both fights with a body which could barely use the aid without assistance. Rudra heard a task he could actually do: tell the attendant not to spend the last clear space trying a turn which would expose the chair's side.

He said it.

The attendant checked the wheels and held their position. The blood on his upper sleeve had spread a little. His hands were still steady enough for the work, but the wound would need care, not a story about how ordinary helpers never noticed pain.

---

Ira asked Sitara where the passage lay now.

"Saamne mod. Uske pehle aadmi. Hum usi taraf dekh rahe hain."

She gave the distance in ordinary near places: the edge of the broad patch, the low break, the start of the bend. She did not supply an unseen path behind the ruins because a patient had asked for a way out.

Ira opened the ordinary eye long enough to find the nearest known edge, then rested it again. The covered left remained covered. There was no live defence still standing for her to return even if the eye had been fit to attempt it. Veer's brief curve had gone.

She told Sitara she understood where they were.

That was useful too. The next instruction would not have to cross the whole distance from confusion before a foot could move.

---

Rudra could see the rock bend beyond the second man's shoulder.

They had come through it minutes before. The lip was still there, the same ordinary obstacle which needed help, not a locked mouth asking his blood to be fed. The vehicles were on the other side. Vedant and the extra helper would still be expecting a short visit to end within its time.

They had no account of this fight yet.

Nobody could call to them by wishing the distance shorter.

Rudra's good hand tightened, then eased before it could pull the injured side into work. The red-black at the collar sharpened. Gold lay inside it, raw and of no use as a wall around the people with him.

The man with the staff glanced at the colour and shifted his grip.

Rudra could not tell what the man recognized. He could tell where the next movement would go.

The ferrule lifted. The attendant set his feet for the chair's weight. Sitara brought Ira one careful step back; Veer held the aid instead of throwing himself forward to make his body another thing which would have to be carried.

The staff came down towards the front wheel.

The attendant pulled the chair short.

He got out one word.

"Hato."

Then there was only enough breath to keep breathing.

The staff man put a hand to his cheek.

Blood ran between the fingers. He tried to bring the broken weapon up with the other hand and found it shorter than the distance he had been controlling a moment before.

Sitara used that distance.

She took Ira along the clear side of the broad patch, with Aadhya following where she could see the pieces which had fallen. Veer moved the aid clear of the splinter beside it. The chair came after them, more slowly, with the attendant keeping Rudra's leg away from the wheel.

"Dard. Zyaada."

Behind them, Dhruv's recovered shaft met the blade again.

The sound kept the withdrawal from becoming a rush.

---

Aadhya heard something crack against the wall above the chair.

She could not tell whether it was loose stone shaken by the burst or another piece thrown by the first attacker. She named where it fell. The attendant kept the wheel to the clear part. There was no need to solve its whole history before avoiding it.

At the start of the rock bend, Sitara stopped long enough for Ira to find the new direction with the ordinary eye, then let her rest it again.

"Ab lip ki taraf," she said. "Meri awaaz pe."

The two girls went with her around the bend.

Veer wanted to keep pace. The knee would not accept the hurried turn. He shortened the movement, set the aid again and stayed clear of the chair instead of falling into its path in an attempt to be first out of danger.

Rudra could still see the broken staff in the attacker's hand.

The man stepped towards him, stopped at the sound of the blade meeting Dhruv's shaft and looked past the chair to the fight behind it.

For a moment his face was simply a hurt face, trying to decide where the next blow might come from.

---

The burst had gone.

Rudra had no new layer around the collar, no certainty that repeating the same desperate opening of the hand would produce the same brief space. The first had thrown splinters near his own friends. His breathing still would not let him speak a whole sentence.

The attendant asked whether he could move the leg at all without adding to the pain.

Rudra tried the smallest change and stopped.

"Abhi nahi."

That answer made the lip ahead more difficult. It did not make the ankle available to force a better answer out of it.

The attendant heard him and kept the chair still for the moment it took to settle the leg. They were nearly at the bend; the low stone beside it could keep a direct throw from reaching the chair once they were around. It could not carry the chair over the uneven lip by itself.

Veer looked towards that cover and then towards Dhruv.

"Pehle is mod tak," he said.

It was a distance he could name without promising the next one.

Dhruv gave ground on purpose.

One step brought him nearer the turning place. Another kept the blade from finding the side where his tender hand had stopped helping him. The iron head of the recovered shaft stayed between the attacker and the people withdrawing.

Rudra saw the man with the cut cheek move back towards the first attacker instead of following the chair immediately. He put the broken staff up to catch a blow aimed at his companion. His bloodied hand slipped on the wood.

The blade man caught his sleeve before he fell.

They had given the group a moment by refusing to let one of their own go down alone.

Dhruv spent it on the way back.

Veer reached the chair as it came around the rock bend.

He did not catch its handles. The shoulder would not make a reliable handle for somebody else's weight, and the attendant already had the chair under control. Veer pointed with the good hand to the place where the foot support would catch if they turned too sharply.

"Thoda peechhe. Phir ghoomna."

Rudra saw it too. The wheel could pass before the supported leg could. The attendant made the little reverse movement they had examined in peace, then brought them clear.

For a moment, the low rock kept the direct line of the attack off Rudra's chest.

He used the moment to breathe.

Farther along the street, a third figure came into view.

He carried a plain short spear. The blade man pointed him towards the companion with the bleeding cheek and broken staff.

"Atala waale, ghaayal lo! Sutala waale mod pe!"

Rudra heard both names. He could not put a birthplace inside a stranger from an order shouted in a fight. He could see the third man take the injured man's elbow, pull his weight into a steadier position and keep the spear's point turned towards the people leaving.

The blade man came forward again.

Whatever the labels meant between them, the work was being divided. One held the hurt man. One went after the return. There might be others beyond the visible street; the four had no count of the broken fronts.

Rudra repeated the two names quietly, enough for Veer to know he had heard them.

"Suna," Veer said. "Ab pehle nikalte hain."

Dhruv drew back to the bend.

The blade touched the recovered shaft and slid down it. He changed the sound hand's grip before the edge reached his fingers. The tender hand would not close. He kept it out of the next exchange rather than pretend pain was the only thing stopping it from being useful.

The opponent glanced past him at the split staff and the fresh mark in the wall. He did not rush blindly into the space Rudra's burst had crossed.

Dhruv used that caution. He did not try to turn it into a victory.

"Sitara!" he called. "Lip pe ek aur aadmi chaahiye. Pair pe nahi aa paa raha."

She was a short way ahead, still near enough for an ordinary voice to reach around the rock. Her answer came back that she had heard.

Then her next directions went towards the Earth side, with the girls.

No warning had reached Vedant by another route. It would arrive with people who had walked out carrying it.

The attendant moved the chair while the space was available.

Rudra could hear the man's breathing over his own. The cut arm left a smear when the sleeve brushed the chair. There was no time to clean it properly here; there was time to say that the man needed help too and not let the plan silently use him as though he had none of the group's injuries.

At the lip, Rudra tried to say what had changed.

"Jhatke ke baad… pair nahi."

The attendant understood. They stopped short of the assisted stand which had worked on entering. An earlier permission did not make the weight-bearing possible now.

The chair's brake held. The man checked that the support was not trapping the leg against the rim, then kept Rudra settled behind the turn in the stone. They would need the second fit person, not the nineteen-year-old with the strained shoulder offering to become one.

Veer said he could wait where he was without leaning on the chair.

A stone hit the bend behind them.

Dust fell. Dhruv's shaft struck the blade twice in quick succession. He had room to yield only until the next part of the passage; behind that were the people whose slower movement he had chosen to protect.

Veer lifted the good hand a little, felt the pull under the ribs and lowered it without trying to call back the pale curve. His first defence had cost something which the need for another had not repaid.

"Abhi nahi hoga," he said.

Rudra nodded. He had nothing better to offer by insisting that the answer change.

He was not a wall. He was a man making the next few moments usable.
