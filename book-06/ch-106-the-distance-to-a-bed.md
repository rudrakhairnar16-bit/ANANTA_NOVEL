# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND SIX — THE DISTANCE TO A BED

---

> *"The account said they had escaped when they reached Earth.*
> *The body still had a distance to travel."*
> *— Ira's later register, beside the time of admission*

---

The medical worker caught Rudra's head before it could fall sideways.

His other hand checked for the pulse. Air was moving. He called to his colleague, and the extra helper stayed where he had been placed, waiting for the instruction instead of trying to shake the boy awake.

Aadhya stepped back until the stretcher could come closer.

"Rudra," the man said. "Meri awaaz sun rahe ho."

There was no answer yet.

The cuff gave a low reading. He repeated it, watched the breathing and fitted the oxygen after checking the clip again. The ambulance driver brought the stretcher alongside. Together with the helper they took Rudra's weight, keeping the supported leg clear and the braced wrist against him.

Nobody asked him to stand.

His eyes moved before they had finished settling him. The medical worker said his name again, and this time the gaze found the face above it for a moment.

"Haan," Rudra breathed.

The man did not take the word as the end of the emergency. He checked again and kept the oxygen in place.

---

Aadhya could see the short mist of breath against the mask.

It was a small, infuriatingly ordinary sign to be looking for after what had crossed the ruined street. She wanted the sign larger. She wanted it to be something which filled the whole space in which her fear had risen.

The worker asked her to move to the other side so they could take the stretcher towards the ambulance.

She moved.

Ira heard the wheels begin and asked whose chair was moving.

"Stretcher," Aadhya said. "Rudra ka. Hosh mein jawaab diya hai. Ab le jaa rahe hain."

The words reached Veer too. His good hand opened against his knee and stayed there. He did not make another question out of the breath he had been holding.

Sitara came close enough for Rudra to see her without turning.

"Hum peechhe aayenge," she said.

He tried to nod. The medical worker told him to leave his head still for now.

---

Vedant gave the earlier care papers and the receiving contact to the crew.

The worker asked for one companion, someone who could give the old history without needing a stretcher of his own. Vedant showed him the protected forearm and said what he could do and what he could not. The man looked at the support, asked another question and offered the belted seat in the front cabin.

The back would remain the patient's working space.

Vedant spoke to Sitara before taking that seat. The second medical vehicle was still coming. Their intact vehicle's driver had been checked and was waiting. The constables had the party's contact numbers and knew there were people yet to leave. They would not be left to invent a return from an emptied approach.

"Phone chaalu rahega," he said.

"Apna bhi dhyaan," Sitara answered.

He looked down at the forearm as though she had named somebody travelling beside him.

"Haan."

---

The driver shut the ambulance's rear doors only after his colleague had finished the straps and the supported leg was settled.

Through the brief open space, Rudra had seen the extra helper taking his hands away. Aadhya stood behind him. Veer was in the spare chair, Ira beside Sitara, the bandaged adults farther back, all of them still on the road's protected side.

The door closing did not remove them from where they were.

He asked something against the mask.

The medical worker bent to hear it.

"Baaki…"

"Doosri gaadi aa rahi hai. Aapki saans dekh raha hoon."

Rudra let the unfinished question go. The man had answered the part he could answer.

The ambulance moved. The first uneven stretch lifted through the tyres, softened by the stretcher, still felt in the ankle. Rudra made a small sound. The worker checked the support rather than telling him the worst was already over.

---

At the bend, the nearer constable watched the ambulance pass and gave the driver the road he had just cleared.

He did not leave his post to follow it. There were two men with weapons somewhere beyond the opening and a third whose last known condition was injured. The police vehicle stayed. His partner kept the remaining party together behind cover and told the approaching support where to stop.

The extra helper looked at the empty chair.

A little grey powder had settled into the seat. He brushed it off with his own cloth, then caught himself doing something which could wait.

He left the brake on and went back to the people who still needed him.

The next police vehicle arrived before the second ambulance.

Two more constables got down. The first pair pointed out the approach, the damaged vehicle and the opening beyond it. One of the newcomers stayed at the road turn to keep curious arrivals from adding themselves to the problem. The other took a position from which he could watch the empty strip without standing in it.

The intact vehicle's driver asked whether he could bring it forward.

They checked the visible ground first. A constable went with him as far as the cover beside the vehicle, watched while he got in and came back when the engine started. The driver brought it nose-first around the bend and stopped beside the waiting people.

The panel was dented. A bright scrape ran along its side. The tyres had not been cut. He checked them himself before saying the vehicle could still make the journey.

He did not offer the same answer for the driver whose hands were holding a bloodied fold against his brow.

---

The second ambulance came by the same road.

Its clinical worker took the first crew's relayed information, then listened to the people in front of him. The driver opened the back and brought the stretcher while the examinations began.

Veer described the shoulder, the ribs, the knee and the rut. The worker checked him seated, changed the support where it was pressing and asked about the movement over the lip. There was no request to demonstrate it again. The second stretcher would be his.

The wounded driver's bleeding had slowed beneath the cloth. He was alert. The worker checked the cut and his responses, asked about glass and vision, then arranged a proper dressing. His eyes needed to be examined again at the receiving unit; he would not drive this journey.

The attendant let the man examine the upper arm instead of saying it was only a chip. The cut needed cleaning and further treatment. He named the pull it had given during the rescue and which movements hurt now.

Dhruv put the hand which would not close where the worker could see it.

He did not make a fist to prove the failure.

---

The cut beneath Dhruv's sleeve was dressed again, with pressure on the place which was still oozing. The shoulder impact and the lost grip went into the handover. He could sit in a supported vehicle seat for the short urgent return, with no lifting or guarding assigned to the trip. The receiving clinicians would examine the hand themselves.

Sitara gave Ira's symptoms, then stopped so Ira could give her own account. Grit, watering, discomfort in the ordinary right eye, the left already covered before the attack. The worker kept the left protection in place and arranged the eye review rather than asking her to read a distant sign in the glare.

Aadhya described the hands. She had not caught a falling body or used them to fight. They had been hurried through grit and dust, and the altered sensation made her unwilling to guess that no pain meant no damage. The worker checked what could be checked there and kept the supports in place for the receiving assessment.

Nobody's silence was entered as a symptom-free answer.

---

They made the remaining journey in two vehicles.

The second ambulance had the stretcher for Veer and two available belted patient seats for the attendant and the wounded driver. Its medical worker would travel in the back with them. The extra helper was offered the spare front passenger seat beside the ambulance driver.

He agreed to go to the receiving place. His part in the rescue would not end with a wave towards a road none of the patients could use alone.

The intact vehicle took its own driver and Dhruv in front, with Aadhya, Ira and Sitara in the rear. They tested the seat positions before settling the belts. Ira had Sitara's voice on one side. Aadhya's hands had room against their supports without being made to hold somebody else's bag.

Veer's aid was secured in the ambulance. The care papers went with the crew responsible for each patient. Sitara told Vedant how they had been divided, and he acknowledged the message from the first ambulance's front seat.

The receiving team was told too.

---

The extra helper and the second crew moved Veer from the spare chair to the stretcher.

He gave the warning about the knee before the turn. They changed the position while the weight was still shared, then settled him and checked again. The aid's ordinary presence beside him mattered more than the spear which had once answered his hand.

Both empty chairs stayed at the road bend with the police, out of the direct approach. The constable recorded whom to contact about them. The damaged vehicle and the shaft through its window would remain for the scene examination; the unhurt driver handed over the owner's contact with his colleague's agreement.

The patients would have receiving chairs and beds waiting. Sitara checked that part over the phone before letting these two pieces of their borrowed independence be left behind.

The second ambulance shut its doors.

The helper was in the front seat. The hurt attendant and driver were inside, not left beside their interrupted work. Sitara checked the people in her vehicle once more, including the man who would drive it, then sent the simple message that all nine were leaving the approach.

Four constables remained there.

They had a strip of road to protect and a passage which had not ceased to exist because the patients were going to hospital.

The first ambulance reached the emergency entrance while the others were still on the road.

Rudra knew the place by a patch of discoloured ceiling which moved above him as the stretcher came in. He had seen it from a chair before leaving for the short outing. Its brown edge was exactly as unfinished as it had been that morning.

The return had changed him more than the ceiling.

The crew gave the handover beside the receiving bed: brief loss of response, pulse and breathing present, low pressure recorded and rechecked, oxygen, chest symptoms, earlier injuries and the forceful jolts. Vedant added the old care papers, then stepped out of the way while the nurse checked the name against the record.

Rudra was awake enough to answer it himself.

The doctor asked the next question of him too.

---

The monitor leads were cold against the chest.

An ECG was taken. Blood was drawn. The doctor examined the breathing and the tender area, asked about the release and the loss of response, then arranged the further chest assessment and the leg films which the history required. The ankle support was opened only by the people checking it, with the limb held still, and put back in a position which did not press the newly sore place.

The wrist remained braced. Nobody asked that hand to push him higher on the bed.

Vedant heard the words about observation and said yes too quickly.

The doctor looked at Rudra before taking the answer.

"Rukna hoga. Abhi ghar bhejne ki baat nahi hai."

"Theek," Rudra said.

He was tired enough for the word to feel like a thing set down rather than agreed to. The doctor asked whether he understood why. Rudra gave the short answer back: the breathing, the pressure, the interval he could not remember.

That was enough for this part.

---

There was no blank space inside the lost interval which had brought him knowledge.

He remembered the stretcher's edge turning grey. After that he remembered a face above the mask. Everything between belonged to the people who had been there while he could not answer. Vedant told him what they had actually done, and Rudra listened without trying to add an invisible contest to make the account more bearable.

"Kitni der?"

"Chhota waqt tha. Crew saamne thi. Woh record mein likhenge."

He did not give it a dramatic number or promise it could not happen again.

Rudra turned the good palm up against the sheet. Nothing was held inside it. He let it stay open while the nurse secured the line and said when the fingers began to feel cold.

She checked the hand.

He did not wait to find out whether reporting something ordinary would make him seem smaller than the force he had used.

---

The second ambulance arrived before his first investigation round was finished.

Veer came in on its stretcher. The attendant and the wounded driver were taken to the places prepared for their examination, with their dressings and the history of how the wounds had happened. The extra helper came through the entrance carrying the papers the crew had given him, not the weight of another patient.

The intact vehicle stopped at the receiving area behind it. Staff brought the promised chair for Dhruv and guided the girls in with Sitara. Aadhya gave the handover nurse her own name. Ira asked for the light away from the ordinary eye while she waited.

All eleven who had gone to the approach had reached receiving care.

The driver who had brought the last vehicle was offered a place to sit too. He took it after handing the keys to the helper for the moment, with an instruction about where he had parked.

---

The eye clinician examined Ira before any request for an account could become a long session of looking.

The ordinary right eye had surface irritation after the grit. Care was given for that, and the plan included rest from glare and another review if the symptoms changed. The covered left eye had its own established injury; it was not treated as a spare which could be uncovered to compensate.

Aadhya's supports were removed for the hand examination and replaced afterwards. No fresh open wound was found in the skin the clinician checked. The altered sensation remained. She was told which changes needed to be reported and that the day had not earned her a new gripping exercise.

Veer's painful shoulder, ribs and knee were examined. Dhruv's cut was cleaned and closed after assessment; his hand and shoulder needed continued care beyond the skin wound. The driver's brow was treated and his vision checked again. The attendant's upper arm received its cleaning and closure, with a proper restriction on lifting.

The ordinary work of getting hurt had arrived in several bodies. The ordinary work of treating them needed several people.

---

A nurse told Rudra that the others were being examined, each in the place they needed.

"Aa gaye sab?"

"Sab aa gaye."

He believed her without asking that every injured person be brought to the foot of his bed to prove it.

By late afternoon the first results were back and the next checks had begun.

The leg films had not shown a fresh break. The pain and swelling still mattered; the ankle would not be asked to accept weight that evening. The first chest investigations had not supplied a simple answer for everything Rudra had described. His pressure had improved under care. He was more consistently alert, but the doctor kept him in the monitored bay for the breathlessness and the interval without a response.

"Abhi observation," she said when he asked about the old room.

He looked towards the curtain and nodded. The familiar room could remain familiar somewhere else for a while.

Vedant had his forearm checked and sat with it properly supported instead of holding the entire afternoon's work against his body. Another staff member took the next contact call. There were limits to how many handovers one injured man could make accurately without rest.

The limit felt different when someone else noticed it before he failed.

---

Rudra asked for the promised short call to Varanasi.

The nurse checked with him first, then helped position the phone where he would not have to hold it up. Vedant confirmed that Ram and Rukmini were awake and available after their own care. Rudra said the nurse could explain the clinical update before he tried to speak.

Rukmini heard that he was answering, that the first investigations and treatment were continuing, that he would remain under observation. She heard that the others had reached care too. She did not hear the word unhurt, because nobody had earned it for the message.

Then Rudra's face was on the screen.

He could see his mother's supported shoulder and his father's chair. Neither image had crossed the distance more easily because he needed them.

"Maa."

"Haan."

It was nearly all he had wanted to say.

---

Rukmini's free thumb rested against the pulse at her own wrist.

She had put it there while the nurse was speaking and had not noticed herself doing it. When her son said her name, she took it away and smoothed the ordinary fold of cloth on her lap.

Ram asked whether the breathing still hurt.

"Kam," Rudra said. "Hai abhi."

His father accepted both parts.

For a moment nobody filled the small sound from the ward beyond Rudra's curtain. Ram's gaze went to the support at the edge of the frame, then back to the boy's face. He looked as though a dozen instructions had reached his mouth together and met there without agreeing on which should come first.

"Abhi kuchh karke dikhaana nahi," he said.

"Kar bhi nahi paaunga."

"Woh wajah nahi hai."

Rudra understood the correction. He did not turn it into permission to forget everything which had once been demanded of a smaller body.

"Pata hai," he said.

It was a present answer, not an ending written over the past.

---

He told them the small part he could tell now.

The checked line had been empty before they went through. Men had come from beyond the stretch they had used. There had been a fight and a withdrawal. He had opened the good hand once; the force had gone farther than he wanted, and afterwards the chair had jolted and his breathing had changed.

He did not give the burst a grander name to make its cost easier for his parents to hear.

Ram asked whether anybody had been left behind.

"Hum sab aa gaye. Driver ko bhi chot hai. Jo saath the, unko bhi."

"Ilaaj ho raha hai?"

"Haan."

The question held more than the family. Rudra was glad his father had asked it without making the hurt men props in his son's account.

Rukmini said she wanted the receiving number written down for the next update. Vedant had already given it; she asked Ram to put it beside her phone as well. Her own place in the care did not depend on how much she could ask the injured boy to repeat.

---

The nurse returned before the call grew longer than the few minutes they had agreed.

Rudra had begun pausing more between words. She asked to check the breathing again. He said yes, and his parents heard him stop instead of being told afterwards that the call had ended because everything was fine.

"Phone charge pe laga dena," Rukmini said.

He glanced at the cable on the table.

"Hai yahin."

"Bas. Ab usse kaam karne do."

It was about a charger, which could keep working while he did not. He smiled a little and found that even the small movement pulled at the sore place.

The nurse saw it. The call ended with the next update agreed through the care contact, not a time Rudra had to stay awake to keep.

The phone went onto charge.

For the first time since the spear left the man's hand, Rudra let himself want sleep without mistaking it for leaving the others alone.

The check after the call found Rudra tired and still needing observation.

He was allowed a little water with help, then left to rest while the next round of results and examination was arranged. The nurse knew whom to contact about a change. Vedant could sit outside the bay without making the space around the bed less attended.

Sleep came in small, unheroic pieces.

Once Rudra woke because the supported ankle had begun to ache differently. He called. Someone came, checked it and changed the padding. Another time he heard a trolley beyond the curtain and did not need to find out whether it carried a weapon.

He went back to sleep before the trolley finished passing.

---

Veer ate after his review, with the shoulder supported and the aid within reach of the person who would help him move. There was no return to ordinary walking that evening. The knee had its own response to the day, indifferent to whether he could explain the right decision about leaving the cylinder behind.

Ira rested in a room with the glare reduced. The ordinary eye was more comfortable after care, not available for an evening of reading. She asked Sitara to keep one correction for later: the two names were what the boys had heard, and she had heard the boys' account.

Sitara repeated the sentence back. Ira said it was right.

That was the whole record for now.

Aadhya made a short call of her own, gave her mother the actual hand findings and the changed day, then let the phone be put away. She was relieved that the skin had not opened again. The relief did not reach the places where sensation was still altered; those needed care whether or not she could make them hurt on demand.

She ate with help and let the supports do their ordinary work.

---

The helpers had people to contact too.

The attendant asked for the treatment note which named the lifting restriction. The extra helper stayed while he spoke to the person who had arranged the paid work. There would be another conversation about missed work and the costs of returning, not a signature which made an armed attack part of the original day's fee.

The wounded driver contacted the vehicle's owner after his examination. He gave the location of the scene through the police contact, said that he would not drive that evening and kept the treated brow out of the habit of wiping at it with a sleeve. The owner would have to arrange the damaged vehicle's recovery when the scene was released.

The intact vehicle's driver got his keys back from the helper and agreed on a proper rest before any further road work. Nobody's car remained indefinitely available because it had carried one frightened group once.

The receiving staff made the arrangements for the patients' necessary help. The two men who had been hired for the outing were not kept standing at the doors until their own injuries disappeared from the accounting.

---

Dhruv sat outside the treatment room with the dressed hand supported.

Sitara came to him after the girls had settled. She did not ask him for the road as a lesson. She asked where he had last seen the two men who followed them out.

He gave the answer again: both back through the same opening, blade first, the spear left behind. The cheek-wounded man had not crossed before the party's departure. He could not say where any of them had gone after the rock took the view.

"Subah khaali tha," Sitara said.

"Jab humne dekha tha."

"Haan."

The qualification hurt more than a defence of their competence would have. Their report had been true to the time they made it. People had still been hurt after trusting the bounded visit which followed it.

Neither tried to turn that into a reason to stop checking. Neither called it proof that checking had been enough.

Dhruv looked down at the fingers which would not close and asked Sitara to tell the officer he could give the fuller account after rest and the next hand examination.

She agreed to make the call.

---

Before she could, Vedant's phone rang outside the monitored bay.

It was the constable at the approach, using the number they had exchanged. Vedant moved away from the curtain before answering. Dhruv and Sitara were near enough to hear when he asked the man to repeat the description.

A hurt man had come out of the passage. Blood down one cheek, cloth pressed to it, part of a broken wooden staff in his other hand. He had put the wood down when ordered and stayed where the officer told him. First aid was being given; medical help had been requested independently of whatever part he had played in the attack.

Vedant asked whether anyone else had followed him out.

Not that the constable could see.

The man had asked not to be pushed back inside. He kept pointing towards the rock as though there was something his raised empty hands could not make the people on Earth understand.

"Jo chhuri leke aaya tha," the constable said, "bol raha hai woh andar daba hai."
