# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND NINE — WHAT WENT INTO THE ACCOUNT

---

---

"Chalo. Abhi."

The clinical worker took her hand from the foot and gave the order from her sound knee.

They lifted the board again.

There would be no further attempt beside the lip to make the missing pulse appear by searching harder. The patient needed a place with more than a lamp and a bag. She gave the finding to the head-end worker in a few words, then stood with her sore knee kept clear of the board.

Sitara watched the first corner go across the uneven place.

They had brought the empty board through this angle. Now it held a man's weight, a supported leg and hands which kept trying to grip whichever wrist came nearest. The worker at the head spoke before each change. Sitara released only when the next grip had taken the load.

The board stayed low.

At the pinch, the side handles had to change hands. They stopped on sound ground before making the change; nobody twisted the patient to carry an unbroken rhythm through the rock.

"Meri awaaz suno," the clinical worker said.

The man turned his face towards it.

"Pair thanda hai."

"Pata hai. Jaldi le jaa rahe hain."

He watched her instead of trying to see down the board.

---

The old passage narrowed around them.

Sitara could feel the handle pressing a hot line into her palm. The worker beside her saw her fingers change their hold and asked for a set-down before she had to ask twice. It cost them several seconds. Dropping one corner would have cost the man more.

The guard came last, the dressed arm against his body, the lathi in the other hand. He turned to look behind only where his feet could stay secure. No movement entered the little view they had left. He did not call the unseen gap empty.

Then the air changed.

Earth smelled of cooling dust and the ambulance's exhaust, of a cooking fire somewhere beyond the road. Sitara had never wanted an ordinary vehicle's noise so much.

The board's head came out first.

The road-turn officer kept a curious motorcyclist behind the bend. The constable with the two injured men stayed with them. The medical driver was at the waiting stretcher, its wheels already secured against moving as the board came alongside.

No one had to ask the patient to stand.

---

The clinical worker gave the handover while the carriers still held the board level.

A compressed lower leg. A second shift of the load during the rescue. Foot becoming cold and pale, pulse not found on repeat examination. What had been done, what had changed and what could not be established there.

The driver relayed the new information to the receiving team before they left. The workers and the backup constable helped settle the board and patient onto the ambulance stretcher under her direction. Sitara kept the bag clear, then stepped back when the straps and supports were checked.

The man's hand found her sleeve once more.

"Joota reh gaya."

"Haan. Wahan hai."

She did not offer to go back under the timber for it.

The clinical worker asked him a question. He let go to answer.

---

Before boarding, the woman showed the medical driver her injured knee and told him what she had been able to do. She would stay with the patient, seated and belted where she could continue watching him; her own examination would follow the receiving handover. The injured constable's dressing was checked once more. The bleeding remained controlled. He could wait with the officers for the second medical crew, not take another lifting or fighting task.

While the stretcher was being secured, she also rechecked the two men waiting under police watch. Neither had a new change which displaced the urgent leg patient. The next crew was on its way. She gave the waiting constable the signs which meant another urgent call, not simply waiting longer, and had him repeat them. He could observe and give first aid; he had not learned her work by standing beside her bag.

They checked the ambulance's actual seats.

The backup constable took the belted front passenger place as escort. The driver drove. The clinical worker settled into the rear attendant seat beside the secured stretcher, bag and lamp stowed within reach. The pinned man was no longer pinned; he was still an urgent patient.

Four people left in that ambulance. No one stood wedged beside the door to make the numbers work.

The cheek-wounded man sat up when the rear doors opened. He saw the supported leg, then the face above it. His mouth moved without a sound Sitara could hear.

The constable told him to remain seated. Another medical vehicle had been requested. His condition was still being watched.

The driver waited only for the clinical worker's ready signal.

Then the ambulance pulled away from the bend and took the ordinary road towards the emergency team already expecting it.

Sitara watched until the vehicle's sound began to lose its separate shape among the sounds of the road.

The threat to the foot had gone with it. It had not been solved by getting the man out.

After the ambulance left, Sitara sat on the low roadside stone.

Her hands would not quite uncurl at first. She opened them against her thighs, slowly, and found the red dents the handles had made. No broken skin. A small tremor when she tried to bring finger and thumb together.

The rescue workers sat too.

One of them drank, then passed the bottle only after asking whether the other had his own. The man who had worked the jack stared at the dust on his boot. When Sitara thanked him, he nodded without making the effort to become modest for her.

They had done something difficult. They were tired.

They offered Sitara biscuits from the vehicle. She cleaned her hands, ate and drank, watching a crumb cling to the deep red line across her palm.

The constable with the cut arm remained seated near the vehicles, his partner watching the two injured suspects. The man at the road kept the turn. There were three of the original four officers here now; the fourth had left with the urgent patient.

---

The requested police van arrived while they were resting.

Four officers got out, including the man who had driven it. They stopped at the road turn for the briefing instead of following the lamp's old direction into the opening. The original constable pointed out the vehicles, the two suspects and the route the rescue group had actually used.

The next medical vehicle came behind them with two crew.

Its clinical worker went first to the people still needing examination, not to the story of the place. She checked the cheek wound and the man's current responses, then the swelling wrist and split lip of the restrained attacker. The officers helped adjust and support the hurt arm as her examination required while keeping him under control.

He was stable enough for seated transport to the urgent review she arranged, with police accompanying him. Stable did not mean his wrist had escaped injury.

The original constable's forearm dressing came off for inspection. His hand still moved and felt as expected, but the cut needed further cleaning and review in proper light. It was dressed again. He would go for care, not drive a patrol car with the injured arm tucked out of sight.

Sitara watched him accept the instruction.

Only when the clinical worker came to her did she notice that she had closed her own hands again.

---

"Kahin lagi?"

"Nahi. Pakadne se thak gaye."

She showed the palms, described the tremor and answered the questions about pain and dizziness. Rest, water and food had begun to settle it. The woman asked about the remaining task.

"Bas mod tak dikhana. Unko saamaan waapas lena hai. Main kuchh nahi uthaaoongi."

Not another rescue. The empty space under the baulk was to stay empty while the workers made the abandoned load safe and recovered the jack. Two fresh officers would guard and document the short inspection. The return still had to be checked; nobody was going farther into the street to discover who else might be there.

Sitara got up after the rest and walked the level stretch beside the vehicle and back. The ordinary movement was comfortable. Her grip was steadier. She did not offer it for another board.

The clinical worker found no fresh injury requiring treatment there. She reminded Sitara to stop if that changed and to eat properly when she got back. She did not certify the wall gap safe.

Sitara said yes to showing the short line, and no to a wider search.

The officer taking the briefing wrote down both answers.

---

The workers had eaten from the food kept in their vehicle. They checked their own hands and feet, told the new officers what the load was doing and agreed to retrieve their equipment if the guarded bearing remained as they had left it. A different position, a new person or an unseen movement would stop the entry.

The two relief officers who would stay on the road for the moment were told exactly who was going in. One was the van's driver. The other would accompany the wrist-injured man later. Neither could leave before the people inside had returned and the new posts were handed over.

The two going in checked the wrapping and containers they had brought for the blades. The ordinary equipment looked strangely insufficient beside the opening. It would have looked equally ordinary beside a knife in a house.

Sitara stood at the mouth without taking the first step yet.

"Andar ki awaaz baahar tak nahi aati har jagah," she told them. "Lip tak test hua tha. Uske aage khabar laani padti hai."

They repeated the limit back. No one tried a phone call from the bend and announced a new rule when it failed.

The injured constable gave his own account of where he had left the knives. The workers gave the position of the loaded jack and the split block. Sitara added the width they had just carried through, and the near side which had remained clear.

Then she took the two fresh officers along the old passage for a current look through and back before the workers entered.

The first look found the board gone and the timber where the workers had left it.

The near packing still carried the changed corner. The far end remained blocked at the wall's foot. The knives were visible in the two places described. No one appeared in the strip the officers could inspect from that side of the bend.

They did not go through the wall gap.

Sitara brought the officers all the way back to Earth. The old shaft still leaned against the rock on the return. She included it in the report without giving it a new owner or a message.

The workers heard the current findings before choosing to go in.

Five entered this time: Sitara, the two workers and the two fresh guards. The other officers kept the Earth posts and the patients; the new medical crew continued care while the van waited.

---

One officer watched outward. His partner photographed the near stack, the far bearing, the two ropes and the marks in the grit before the workers changed the load. The pictures stayed with the case record, not a roadside audience.

The near blade was recorded as the weapon the first constable had moved out of reach. Its present place could not honestly be described as where its owner had dropped it. The streetward knife had fallen during the later struggle. That distinction took a few more words and spoiled the neatness of a single caption.

The officer used the words.

Beside the wall, the old loose rope lay apart from the new cut line. The short, fresh ends and the fibres at the cut were kept with the account of who had actually seen the knife cross it. Drag marks led towards the failed stone stack. There was still no doorway above to have shed an innocent lintel into the road.

The second obstruction had been made here after the group's first withdrawal. How each man had helped make it would require their accounts. A photograph could show a dragged timber; it could not overhear an order already given.

---

The workers lowered the near end in controlled stages onto ground from which the patient and board were now absent.

The damaged block was no longer allowed to set the next decision. They transferred the weight through the sound supports, took back only the pieces which were free and kept the far end from turning against its blocked bed. There were pauses in which nothing moved except the worker's head as he checked the other bearing.

Sitara stood out of the working space.

Her hands wanted to offer themselves at every pause. She kept them at her sides. She had said she would show the route, not quietly become the missing third worker because watching was uncomfortable.

At last the timber rested low and stable against the wall foot and ground. The jack came away without taking a load into anyone's hands.

The two broad blocks at the far end stayed as a restraint. The workers recorded what they were leaving. A returned tool kit would be two blocks short; that was preferable to a complete kit and another rolling beam.

The route along the near side remained passable. The street beyond was still a street they had not searched.

---

The documenting officer collected the blades separately when the workers confirmed that he could reach their positions without entering the load's reach. The cut line was retained too. The old rope was photographed and identified as the unused one, not quietly folded into a story about a failed rescue rope breaking under weight.

The boot lay clear when the near end came down to its new rest.

Sitara pointed it out.

The officer asked whether it belonged to the patient just taken away. She described what she had actually seen: the foot coming free, the boot staying under the wood, the man's repeated request. He recorded that source and wrapped the boot separately for the patient's property.

It looked smaller away from the leg.

A split seam ran along its side. Grit filled the heel. There was nothing on it which could identify a hidden ruler, a realm's guilt or the whole purpose of a war. It had kept somebody's foot warm before the wood took it.

The officer put the wrapped bundle beside the equipment to go out.

---

They left without taking a step towards the old gap.

Sitara checked the return in front. The workers carried the recovered kit. One guard came with the load; the other kept the rear in view, stopping where he could look back without losing his footing. No new figure entered the part they could see.

At the Earth mouth, all five were counted back.

The account they gave was small enough to be true. Knives recovered. Cut line kept. Jack and usable packing out. Timber lowered, two blocks deliberately left, near-side route available during their return. No search beyond the bend. No claim to an empty realm.

"Bas yahin tak meri haan thi," Sitara said.

The officer closed the route note.

No one asked her to stretch the word until it covered the dark.

The two guards who had returned from the bend took the Earth posts after a proper handover.

One at the mouth, one at the road turn. They had the current account, the contact numbers and a clear limit: keep people out of an unassessed danger, watch the approach, call for support if the facts changed. They were not to march into an unknown street because a man on a phone called it a border.

The second medical crew checked their loading arrangement before moving anyone.

The cheek-wounded man took the stretcher under continued observation. The constable with the forearm cut had a belted patient seat in the rear, the clinical worker beside them. Another original officer rode in front as escort, next to the medical driver. Five people, with no one asked to sit on the floor.

The wrist-injured attacker went in the police van after the clinical worker confirmed the seated journey arranged for his urgent review. The van's driver drove; the other waiting relief officer sat with the restrained man in the rear. The support under the hurt arm stayed where the clinician had put it.

The two vehicles left separately, each with its own receiving directions. Neither was sent to find the students' rooms.

Three known attackers were now under actual police control and medical care. That was a count of these three men, not of everyone who might have stood beyond the wall.

---

The rescue workers stowed the recovered equipment.

The man who would drive sat behind the utility vehicle's wheel only after food, rest and a check that his hands had settled. His partner said he could take over later if needed; neither had acquired a duty to drive through exhaustion to make the rescue look complete.

They left the missing blocks on the equipment record. The split upper block had been photographed with the load before it came out; the cut line went with the weapons, not into the workers' rope bag.

Sitara thanked them again, now that it no longer interrupted a grip.

One of the men looked at the mark across her palm.

"Aaj aur mat pakadna kuchh bhaari."

"Aaj kaafi pakad liya."

He smiled at that, a small tired movement. Then he shut the vehicle's door.

---

The road-turn constable who had been there from the earlier support would drive Sitara back in one of the existing police vehicles. The other police vehicle stayed at the guarded approach for later collection. The damaged car, the spear through its window and the two empty chairs remained on the scene record; nothing was being returned to an owner before the examination which still needed it.

Sitara asked the relief officer to make sure the vehicle owner's contact stayed attached to that record.

He showed her where it was.

She called Vedant from Earth before leaving.

"Sab baahar aa gaye. Jo log andar kaam kar rahe the, sab."

Then the separate facts. The leg patient had left by ambulance for urgent care. The other two men had been examined and were going under escort. The clinician's knee and the original guard's cut needed their own review. Tools and blades recovered, two support blocks left deliberately, new officers holding the Earth approach. No deeper search. No students required.

She gave no prognosis for the foot.

Vedant asked about her hands.

"Thake hain. Chot nahi. Ab waapas aa rahi hoon."

"Khaana?"

"Biscuit se zyaada chaahiye."

"Mil jaayega. Main banaa nahi raha, darr mat."

For the first time since the rope parted, she laughed without having to stop listening for a second sound.

---

The road journey took the time a road journey took.

Sitara kept the belt on and let the constable drive. She gave him the factual account of what she had seen, leaving space for the workers and the injured officer to give their own. He did not ask her to turn the other men's words into her memory.

At the receiving entrance, she got out slowly. The constable asked when the patients might be able to give statements. He accepted the care team's answer: not by gathering everyone now because he had a pen ready. A short appointment could be arranged for the following morning if the patients' current condition and choices allowed it.

He left after giving the contact for that arrangement. His shift would finish; he would not be spending the night outside a curtain in case an exhausted boy woke usefully talkative.

Sitara went inside.

Dhruv saw her first.

"Haath ka check hua," he said.

She had promised to ask when she came back. He had kept the small ordinary future ready for her.

"Aur?"

"Dressing badli. Pakadna abhi nahi. Tumhaara?"

She showed him the unbroken palms.

"Mera bhi abhi nahi."

He moved the water glass within reach without asking her to open a cap to prove it.

She ate before the fuller telling. The ward staff kept the patients' care moving around the absence of a meeting. Ira did not have to open the irritated eye to count people in a doorway. Aadhya did not have to hold a phone. Veer did not have to stand up to welcome her back.

The unhurt helper came to settle the work times before leaving for his own rest. He had another booking to answer for the morning. Vedant put the ordinary care handover as the end of this duty, not Sitara's much later return, and kept the extra emergency work as a separate payable entry. Tomorrow was not silently booked. The helper checked the entries himself. He could be glad they were alive and still need the next day's wages from somebody else.

The first receiving confirmation came through the escort's actual call that night.

The leg patient had reached emergency care and been taken up by the team needed for the threatened limb. He was alive. They were not stopping his treatment for a full account of the fight. The clinical worker had handed him over and was having her own knee examined.

Vedant asked for no photograph and no copy of a stranger's medical file. He thanked the officer for the limited update and passed on only that much.

Rudra heard it after the next check.

"Bach jaayega?"

"Abhi woh nahi bola. Ilaaj chal raha hai."

He lay quiet. The difference between those answers was larger than the space they occupied.

---

The night did not become a council.

Sitara washed, ate the rest of the meal and went off duty from everyone's small requests. The staff who had taken the girls' care continued it. Dhruv did not need to finish his account of the fight while somebody was standing up to leave. Vedant put the phone away between necessary contacts.

Rudra slept in stretches, waking for the care already planned. When the ankle ached differently against the support, he said so and let the position be checked. The easier breathing did not make the faint at the approach a thing which had never happened. The observation continued.

Once he woke to the sound of a trolley outside and thought of a boot wrapped separately from its owner.

Sitara had told him that part. She had not told him the man had become good when he wanted his shoe back.

He slept again before there was anything useful to add.

---

Day ninety-seven began without a return plan.

The morning examination found Rudra's pressure and breathing settled enough for a short supported conversation. The cause of the earlier collapse was not closed by that improvement. His wrist and legs remained protected, with no new standing task attached to the permission to speak.

The nurse asked whether he wanted the arranged statement now or later.

"Abhi thoda. Rukna hua toh bol doonga."

The road-turn constable came back after his own rest, as arranged through the care contact. He had the scene account. He did not bring the recovered weapons into the bay or ask the patient to demonstrate what had broken a staff.

Vedant stayed only after Rudra said he wanted him there. Sitara could give her account separately. The other three students had their own choices and care times; four friends would not become one convenient mouth because the officer had a single notebook open.

He explained that he would write what Rudra said and read it back. Rudra could correct it, stop or leave a part for later when he could manage it properly.

"Hamaare jawaab mila mat dena," Rudra said.

"Alag hi lenge."

---

He began with the place where the return had become a fight.

The man with the blade. Dhruv between him and the people withdrawing. The staff coming towards the chair. The attendant trying to keep the wheel clear, hurt arm working because the danger had not waited for it to heal.

The constable wrote slowly enough for the words to remain Rudra's.

When they reached the shouted labels, he asked what Rudra could actually place.

Rudra gave the words he had heard and the movements he had seen. A man helped the bleeding staff-holder. Another pressed the return. The names shouted between them did not give him their birthplaces, Kuls or the person who had sent them.

The officer read that portion back.

Rudra corrected the order of one movement. The pen crossed it out with a single line and put the correction beside it. He was allowed to have remembered badly without losing the right to be believed about everything else.

---

The nurse came to the bay opening.

They had time for a little more, if Rudra still wanted it. Then rest.

He looked at the page rather than the officer's face. The account on it was beginning to acquire the clean shape of an injured boy attacked on his way home. It was true. It was not all that had happened.

He remembered opening the good hand before he had decided what shape the answer should take. The staff breaking. The red-black striking stone beyond it. A splinter falling beside Veer's aid, close enough to a friend to make the moment impossible to polish.

The boy who had been attacked was also the person whose force had not stopped where he wanted it to stop.

Vedant shifted the water glass within reach and said nothing. He was leaving the next sentence to the person who would have to live inside it.

Rudra could have waited to be asked. The officer had not been at that wall. An account was beginning to exist which could protect him from a question he had not yet answered for himself.

He kept the good hand still on the sheet.

"Mere haath se jo nikla tha," he said, "usse kya hua, woh bhi likhoge?"
