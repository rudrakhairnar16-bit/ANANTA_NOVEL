# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY — WHAT VICTORY COULD NOT PROMISE

---

> *"We knew whom we had to resist.*
> *The next page began with what we still had to learn."*
> *— Ira's later register, end of the account of the Kul wars*

---

"Pata nahi," Aadhya said.

She answered the question Rudra had put to them, without making the uncertainty sound like a failure of courage.

"Tumhaare Papa se poochhna. Hum mein se kisi ne unse yeh suna hi nahi."

Rudra heard someone shift at the other end, then Aadhya saying she was all right before the nearby worker had to ask again. The line held their separate rooms together. It could not give any one of them an answer which had never crossed it.

"Main poochhoonga," Rudra said.

"Toh abhi apni taraf se haan mat likhna," Ira said. "Na, bhi nahi."

She was not writing. He knew that because she had told them, not because he could see the hands beyond the line.

Veer asked whether not knowing the answer changed what they did about Kaal.

"Usko todne denge toh nahi," Aadhya said. "Par rokna aur theek karna ek hi kaam hai, yeh humein kisne bataaya?"

No one had.

The answer did not make the man Ram had named less dangerous. It made the promise Rudra had almost built around defeating him less easy to give.

---

Sitara said their agreed listening time was nearly over.

Rudra had been preparing to ask another question. He let it wait. Veer had a shoulder to settle, Aadhya had already spent the interval she had offered, and Ira's eyes were not being rested by the good intentions of the people talking near them.

"Ek aur chhoti meeting rakh lete hain," Veer said, "jismein decide karein ki aur meeting kab nahi rakhni."

Aadhya laughed once.

"Usmein tum absent rehna. Kaam jaldi hoga."

They did not make another appointment to prove they were still together.

Rudra asked whether, after he had actually spoken to Ram, they would want the narrow answer in a short voice message. The four agreed, to be heard when their own care allowed. Ira asked him to say whose answer it was. He said he would.

The call ended with ordinary goodbyes, one person leaving and then the next, until the room's ceiling fan was the loudest thing beside his head again.

He had not lost them by letting them go.

---

The nurse returned to find him looking towards the water.

It was close enough for an unwary hand to make an attempt. The elbow was still thick beneath its support. When he imagined reaching, the palm tightened before the rest of the movement had even begun.

"Paani chahiye," he said.

She brought it within the supported position and helped him take the few mouthfuls he wanted. A little caught at the split in his mouth. He told her, and waited while she changed the angle.

Afterwards he asked whether the fold under his back could be moved lower.

The first adjustment pulled through the sore place. His mouth opened on the automatic answer that it was fine; then he said it was worse there.

She stopped.

Nothing dramatic happened in the space his answer made. No alarm, no rushing feet. She put the support back, asked him to describe the pull and called the other worker before trying the position shown in his care plan. Between them they made the small change without asking either injured arm to hold him up.

He could breathe more comfortably in that position. The deeper breath still hurt.

Both facts were written down.

---

For years he had known the pause between a teacher's instruction and the next effort as the place where a boy could be found wanting. The knee shook, the hand opened, the correction arrived. Even now some part of him expected a verdict after saying that the first adjustment hurt.

The nurse asked whether he wanted the sheet left over the foot.

"Haan."

She left it.

He looked at the fold where she had tucked the edge, absurdly moved by its staying exactly where he had asked. An answer could change what happened next. It did not have to earn the right by being shouted through a fight.

The clinician's next check did not turn comfort into permission. Grip, swelling and the pain with breathing were reviewed again; the protected joints stayed protected. Rudra described the night and the morning without inserting a trial of standing which no one had asked him to perform.

There was to be more rest before any further conversation. If the symptoms changed, he was to call the nurse, not wait for the conversation to finish.

He agreed and let her move the phone away from his face.

The room warmed towards noon. A trolley went past with something loose ticking against its metal side. From farther down the corridor came an argument about which bag held a patient's slippers, then the tired laughter of someone who had found them.

Rudra closed his eyes.

He would ask his father a question when both of them could answer a phone again. For the length of this rest, that was the whole task.

When he woke, the water had been changed and the sunlight no longer reached the same square of floor.

He ate with the help the position required. After the next check, he asked whether a short call could fit before the prescribed rest. The nurse agreed to a few minutes if his answers remained first, and put the phone where his head could stay supported.

Ram did not answer at once.

Rudra waited without calling again. When the return call came, his father said he had been with his own clinician. He was free now, for a little while. It was Ram alone on the line; there was no new audience for the question merely because somebody in the house might be awake.

Rudra gave the current findings first. The swelling and grip had been checked again. The back still hurt. There had been no further faint or change in his sight. He was resting between the checks, not trying the new form.

"Aur ek baat poochhni hai," he said.

He asked it exactly as he had asked the others.

His father did not answer with an instruction to become strong enough to find out.

"Mujhe nahi pata, beta. Kaal ko rokne se woh apne aap theek ho jaayegi—iski guarantee main nahi de sakta."

---

Rudra listened for the qualification which sometimes followed an admission: not yet, not until you were older, not until the next drill had been survived.

Ram let the answer stand.

What he had learned six years before Rudra's birth identified the hand and the danger it wanted to bring about. It had not given him a tested account of what would happen to the whole balance after that hand was stopped. He could repeat the threat he knew. He could not make the repair into something he had witnessed.

"Phir aapne jo kaha—"

"Woh waapas nahi le raha. Cycle mein nakaami hai. Kaal use khatam karne par laga hai. Uske baad ka har jawaab mere paas nahi hai."

There was an impatience in Rudra which wanted one of those statements to be false so that the other could be easier. The failing order could be a trick; or the person attacking it could be the whole explanation. Either version would give him a single thing to aim at.

His father had not offered either version.

"Rokna zaroori hai," Rudra said. "Par usi ko poora kaam nahi bol sakte."

"Jitna jaante ho, utna hi bolo. Main bhi."

For once the last two words did not reach Rudra as an order intended only for the person with less authority.

---

At the other end a sound interrupted Ram, and he asked Rudra to wait while he answered someone nearby. When he returned he said his next care was due shortly. They would have to stop.

Rudra had another question ready. He said so, then asked the small permission before the large one.

Could the four friends hear this answer too?

Yes, Ram said. As his answer, with the uncertainty left inside it.

Could Rudra ask Vedant and Dhruv about the account, when their own care allowed? He would need to give them the name and what Ram had said about Adhira, Aegis Meridian and the balance. He did not know what either mentor already knew.

Ram agreed to those parts being shared with those two people. He was not appointing either as an authority on every question by agreeing that they could be asked.

"Unse bhi poochh kar," he said.

"Haan."

Rudra could hear his father preparing to finish. He wanted, suddenly, to keep him there over an ordinary thing: whether he had eaten, whether the room was hot, whether his mother had found somewhere comfortable to rest. There had been years in which a question about the world was easier than a question about the man in front of him.

"Khaana hua?" he asked.

"Hua. Tumhaara?"

"Hua."

Neither asked what was on the other's plate. The two answers were enough for this call.

Ram said he would go for the care he had just mentioned. Rudra said he would put the phone down too. They ended it before either had to be reminded again.

The quiet afterwards did not repair the old courtyard. It contained one present occasion on which his father had said stop and stopped himself.

---

After the nurse had checked his position, Rudra asked for help recording the short reply the others had chosen to receive.

She placed the phone near him and began the recording at his request. He spoke in his own voice, without forwarding the private call.

"Papa se poochha. Unhe pata nahi ki Kaal ko rokne se Divine Cycle apne aap theek hogi ya nahi. Unka pehla bayaan wahi hai: santulan bigad raha hai aur Kaal use khatam karna chahta hai. Yeh unka jawaab hai; koi nayi jaanch nahi."

He listened to the playback once. There was a little room noise behind him, nothing which identified another patient. He asked for it to be sent only to the four agreed contacts. The nurse checked those recipients with him before sending.

The phone went aside.

The next breath pulled at his back. He called the nurse while she was still near enough to hear him, and told her it was the familiar ache with the movement, not a new place of pain.

She checked before leaving him to rest.

Ira heard the message after the interval she had kept for her eye.

She did not bring the screen close to see whether the voice had arrived. The phone was already where she could play it by touch, and the sound came low into the ordinary room. Rudra spoke once, with the slight roughness still in his mouth. There was no recording of Ram behind him, no secret second voice to strain towards.

She let it end before reaching for the notebook.

At her review she had asked about a very short ordinary entry, and been told to keep it brief, stop at discomfort and rest afterwards. The left eye remained covered. She set the page in the light available to the right one and wrote larger than she usually did.

The first line gave the source.

*Ram's account of the fight six years before Rudra's birth, through Rudra, shared with permission.*

Then the few things she had actually been told: Kaal behind Adhira and Aegis Meridian. The Divine Cycle maintaining the balance among seven Lokas, already failing. Kaal intending to destroy it.

Beneath that she put the answer which had arrived today: *Ram does not know whether stopping him would repair it.*

The handwriting spread wider towards the end. She stopped there, before the short entry could become a demand to make the whole page beautiful.

There were facts she had not entered: the man's birth, a face, the identity of the injured attacker. She had no new facts for those spaces. A blank could be left without turning it into a dark shape from a dream.

She closed the notebook.

---

The temptation was not only to invent an answer.

Sometimes it was to keep working until somebody else could look at the record and forget that an injured person had made it. Straight lines, even letters, every old loose paper found and put in order: an appearance of control which cost more than the missing information had cost to admit.

Ira set the pencil down without going back over the final word.

The wall beyond the bed was easier to look at than the page. After a moment she let that eye close too. The few lines remained where she had left them. They did not require a watcher to keep the source from changing.

When she could send a brief reply without using the screen, she told Rudra the limited entry was made and the book closed. She gave no claim to have checked his father's account against a hidden record.

Then she left the phone alone.

---

Aadhya had heard the same voice message before her next hand care.

The worker asked her to describe the feeling during the movements already prescribed. She watched the slow effort, reported where the familiar altered sensation persisted and stopped at the limit they had agreed. There was no second set added because she had managed to sound cheerful on a call.

Afterwards the cup beside her was still too awkward to lift in the position she had been given. She asked for help instead of trying to trap it between hands which had other work to recover.

The tea had gone a little lukewarm by the time she drank it.

That annoyed her more than the lack of a speech about bravery would have done. She said so when Veer called later, after checking by message whether she was free for a minute. It was easier to begin with the tea than with an enemy who wanted to break the order between worlds.

"Phir garam karwa lena," he said.

"Advice ke paise loge?"

"Abhi introductory offer hai."

He had played Rudra's message after his own care. The shoulder was settled for the moment, not cured; he was speaking with the phone propped where the arm did not need to hold it. Aadhya could hear him telling somebody that the position was all right before he returned to her.

They let the small joke finish.

---

"Usko bas bolna tha ki hum taiyaar hain," Veer said at last.

"Kis cheez ke liye?"

"Wahi toh. Bolne se pehle socha nahi."

Aadhya turned her hand on its support within the movement she had been shown. Ready had become a word people could put in front of any pain and expect the pain to move out of the way.

"Sunne ke liye the," she said. "Aaj utna kiya."

"Kal?"

"Kal ka kal poochhna."

He accepted it without making her soften the answer into a promise. They could want the same person stopped without having signed away every later choice about how to stop him.

Veer said he would tell Rudra he had heard the answer. Nothing needed Aadhya to relay it for him. She said she would send her own reply after rest.

They finished before the worker came back.

The cup was taken away to be warmed. Aadhya watched the small circle of damp it had left behind and resisted the impulse to wipe it up with the protected hand.

She could ask for that too.

---

Rudra heard Ira's reply when his next care interval allowed it. Later came the separate acknowledgments from Veer, Aadhya and Sitara, each received without a demand for a new conversation.

Nobody had supplied the missing answer. Nobody had mistaken its absence for an instruction to leave him to ask alone.

He rested with the phone dark beside him. The form stayed gone; the support under the arm still had work to do.

The recovery truck reached the arranged yard that afternoon.

Its driver stopped at the receiving space instead of leaving the load beside the entrance. The operator stayed with him while the yard worker checked the damaged ambulance and the separate pieces against the list which had travelled with it. Torn door. Detached wheel. Folded stretcher. The list was read aloud once more where a crease made one of the pencilled items difficult to read.

The driver waited until the last item had been checked before reaching for the receiving sheet.

The owner arrived after taking the two medical workers back to their base in his car. They had got out there with their own bags; their return ride was over. Neither had been asked to come on to the yard and help unload a vehicle after treating the people who had left it.

The owner showed the keys he had accepted at the road.

The recovery operator checked the unloading position with the receiving worker before loosening anything. The ambulance came off under control. The separate pieces were brought down and set where they could be inventoried without someone having to climb over them later.

The receiving worker signed for the vehicle and the listed pieces in the owner's presence. A copy went with the owner; the recovery driver kept his own delivery acknowledgment. The recovery payment had already been counted and accepted at the road. Nobody presented that same charge a second time because the next person in the chain had arrived.

Only after the load was accepted did the driver and operator take their truck clear and leave.

---

The owner stood beside the empty ambulance for a while.

There was no patient left for it to protect. Without the bed occupied, the damage looked at once less terrible and more expensive: a thing which would have to be inspected, taken apart, priced and either put back into service properly or kept out of it.

He asked when the inspection could begin.

The yard worker gave the next arrangement, not a roadside guess turned into a finished estimate. The owner kept the receiving copy with the damage record. Who would ultimately pay for which loss remained a matter for those records and the account still to be settled. No injured family's address had been written in place of an answer.

He did not throw away the list of pieces because it looked too small beside what had happened.

At the base, the second clinical worker had put his own bag down and given the remaining work report. The driver had handed in the account of the damaged vehicle. Their part at the bank had ended; the return had actually brought them somewhere they could stop.

The bent stretcher did not become usable because its workers were no longer beside it.

---

At the tea counter on the diverted road, somebody had heard that the ambulance had gone away on another truck.

That part was true.

The rest acquired a new ending with every telling. The boy had stood up beneath the vehicle. He had walked away. He had said something to the police which nobody could remember but everybody was prepared to repeat.

A man waiting for change said he had seen a medical vehicle leave the approach.

"Kaunsa ladka?" someone asked him.

He did not know the name. He had seen only the ordinary movement at the outward road, from where he had been kept waiting. He could not give the bank beyond the bend or the words spoken there.

The tea seller put the change into his hand.

The customer checked the coins before pocketing them, a small delay in which nobody added anything. Then a scooter started, its rider complaining about the longer route, and the story began moving again without him.

It carried light and a vehicle and a boy who could always stand in the next person's version. It carried no voice from the private call, no explanation of the balance between worlds, no bed number to which a stranger could bring the legend.

---

The fresh constables at the road gave their actual report through the ordinary duty contact: recovery had cleared the vehicle; the damaged mouth and the strip leading to it had not been opened for public access.

The broken ground was still broken.

One remained at the diversion while his partner checked the barrier from the safe side. Neither entered the passage to see whether an afternoon without another attack had made it sound. Their next relief belonged to the normal duty arrangement, not to a boy's power or an obligation to guard a realm for the rest of their lives.

Beyond the bend the unused route held the ordinary light of late afternoon. No one was going back through it to fetch an image that had ceased to be worth the journey.

In the receiving room, Rudra knew none of the new details at the yard.

He woke when the nurse came for the next care. The ankle had begun to throb in its supported position. He said so before asking for the phone, and waited while she checked what had changed.

The nurse checked the position of the ankle, the dressing and what Rudra could feel beyond it. She asked him to distinguish the new throbbing from the sharper pain with movement. He did, and waited through the examination instead of supplying the answer which would get the phone back sooner.

The clinician reviewed the change before the next rest. No walking trial was added. The support was settled again, and Rudra was told what to report if the pain or feeling changed.

He did not ask the new form to give a second opinion.

Only later, when he could have a brief conversation, did he ask for help sending Sitara a question. Could Vedant listen for a few minutes after his own care? Rudra wanted to ask about an account Ram had permitted him to share. Sitara did not need to carry the account between them, and nobody needed to come to his bed.

Her reply came after she had actually asked.

Vedant could listen at the next agreed interval. His forearm had been reviewed, the changed feeling in the fingers still present. The phone would be set down for him; he was not offering the injured arm for a walk through the building. Dhruv was resting after his own care. A second listener was not required to make the first conversation count.

Rudra waited for that interval.

---

When the call connected, Vedant began with the ordinary question of how he was lying.

Rudra told him. He gave the new elbow problem and the back pain, the grip which still failed him, the reviewed images which had not cleared loading or standing. The account began after the first ambulance had taken Vedant away, not at a point his teacher was supposed to have watched from another vehicle.

The other man had stopped attacking before Rudra left the bank. That was the last condition Rudra himself could report. He had come to receiving care with the returning crew.

"Roop badla tha," he said. "Unbound: Pataal naam rakha hai. Band kar diya tha, unke uthaane se pehle."

He described the decision to end it, not a method Vedant could ask him to reproduce. The swelling had remained inside it. Afterwards other people had still needed to lift the patient and do their work.

Vedant did not request a demonstration to make the name credible.

"Abhi bataana kaafi hai," he said.

Rudra let the elbow stay where it had been put.

---

He asked if he could give Ram's account now.

Vedant agreed to hear it within the few minutes they had set aside.

Rudra named Kaal. He gave the source and the timing: what his father said he had learned while fighting Adhira six years before Rudra's birth. Adhira had been a pawn; Aegis Meridian was under the same hidden hand. Ram had allowed these parts to be told, not his whole private conversation with his son.

Then came the Divine Cycle: the balance among seven Lokas, already failing; the named enemy intent on destroying it.

Vedant listened without supplying a birthplace or a face for the man who had attacked at the passage. Rudra had not offered one. He said plainly that his father had been unable to identify that man from his account.

Finally he gave today's answer. Ram did not know whether defeating Kaal would make the failing order repair itself.

The nurse came near enough to check whether Rudra needed the call stopped. He told her the pain was unchanged and asked to finish the short interval. She checked his position before agreeing. At the other end, Vedant answered a question about his supported arm before returning to the line.

The world had not grown too important for either of them to be interrupted by care.

---

"Agla vaar nahi poochh raha main," Rudra said.

He had meant to say it more quietly. Not softer, exactly, but without the edge which could make a request sound like a challenge to a teacher who had not issued one.

Vedant waited.

Rudra tried again. His father had given him something real. His friends had heard it, with the source kept beside the words. He wanted to learn what followed without making another person's injury the price of proving that the first answer mattered.

"Toh kis baat mein madad chahiye?" Vedant asked.

The question was offered inside the time they actually had, not across an open road which somebody expected Rudra to stand up and take.

---

Rudra looked towards the window, where the afternoon had thinned without asking whether he was ready for evening.

Pataal had been a word his father kept behind his teeth. Then it had been a street under Rudra's wheels, ruined houses which gave him no right to invent the people who had lived in them. Later it had been the place they had come back from hurt, and the name of a shape he had learned to end while the injuries stayed.

None of those things had been nothing.

They had brought him to an enemy's name and to a failing balance which could not be understood by striking a wall harder. He wanted the next part of his life to hold more than a better way of surviving the same unanswered question.

The foot stayed supported. His friends had chosen no journey. The man listening had an injured arm and a few minutes, not an obligation to know everything.

Rudra could ask him where learning began.

For once he did not begin by asking how much force would be enough.

"In saaton ka santulan samajhne ke liye sawaal kis ke paas le jaana hoga?"
