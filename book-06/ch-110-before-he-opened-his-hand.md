# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TEN — BEFORE HE OPENED HIS HAND

---

> *"The first telling remembered the broken weapon.*
> *He asked that the splinter near his friend be remembered too."*
> *— Ira's later register, margin beside the account of the second return*

---

"Haan," the constable said. "Jo tumne kiya aur jo uske baad dekha. Alag-alag bataao."

He turned the page enough to leave room.

Rudra kept the good hand on the sheet. He would not demonstrate. He had opened it towards the staff because the end was coming at the chair again. The thing which came out had caught the wood and gone on towards the wall. He had wanted the staff away. He had not decided what every piece of the broken wood would do.

"Laal-kaala tha," he said. "Ek baar. Lakdi tooti. Peechhe patthar pe laga."

The officer wrote the description without supplying a name for the force.

"Chehre pe jo lagi—"

"Lakdi ka tukda dekha. Uske haath pe khoon dekha. Saara ghaav tab nahi dekha."

The difference stayed on the page.

A splinter had fallen beside Veer's aid. The chair had kicked against the attendant's hold. Rudra's ankle and knee had jarred; the pain under the breastbone and the broken breathing had followed. He could tell those things. He could not turn the later collapse into a diagnosis he had never been given.

The constable read the sentences back.

---

One word was wrong.

"Roka nahi," Rudra said. "Door kiya. Phir apne aap nahi rok paaya jahaan chahta tha."

The pen waited.

"Staff se aage gaya. Yeh rehne do."

The correction made the account uglier. He could feel the old wish to put a clean edge on it: his own hurt body, another man's weapon, a force used only as far as necessary because that was how a person in his position was supposed to have used it.

The piece beside Veer had not asked what shape a good account ought to have.

"Main bach gaya," Rudra said, "isliye sab sahi mat likhna."

The officer looked up.

"Tumhara bachna bhi likhenge. Tumne kya kiya, woh bhi. Faisla is ek bayaan se nahi hoga."

He was not being offered a conviction as punishment for honesty, or a pardon as a prize for being injured. His account would stand beside other people's accounts and the things taken from the scene.

Vedant sat quietly near the water glass.

Rudra had expected a hand on the shoulder, a word which made the difficult line easier to leave behind. The silence was better. It kept the line his.

---

The nurse came in before the next question.

"Ab rest."

The constable stopped writing.

Rudra listened to the last short portion once more and confirmed what was right. The unfinished parts were marked for another time. He did not have to finish being useful before the care could resume.

The officer put the notebook away, gave the contact for corrections and left the bay. There would be separate conversations with the others when each could manage them. No one was to carry a completed answer from bed to bed for the friends to agree with.

When the curtain settled, Rudra discovered he had been holding his jaw tight.

Vedant passed him the water in the way the nurse had allowed.

"Galat likh deta toh?" Rudra asked.

"Phir sudhaarwaate. Abhi tumne kar diya."

It was a smaller answer than a speech about courage. He could use it.

---

The nurse asked about the breathing, then the ache near the knee.

He answered with the same care he had given the page. Easier than yesterday was not normal. More comfortable against the padding was not a leg ready to take him back to the bend. The wrist stayed clear when she adjusted the support.

He wanted to ask what would have happened if the splinter had gone a little farther. The question could build a whole second afternoon in his head without giving Veer an unhurt shoulder in either one.

He told Vedant he would like to speak to his friend later, if Veer wanted it and their care allowed. Not to make Veer promise that nothing had frightened him. Just to hear what the moment had been like from that side of the broken staff.

Vedant said he would ask, not book the visit for him.

Rudra let that be enough for now.

Outside the curtain, the ordinary trolley rattled past. Someone laughed briefly at a joke he could not hear. The room had not become a courtroom or a training ground because he had said the difficult part aloud.

He put the good hand flat again and let the nurse finish.

The other accounts were taken in the intervals each person could give.

Aadhya described the crack above the chair and the fragment falling. She could not tell whether it had been thrown or shaken loose. The officer left the uncertainty in place. Afterwards the care worker checked the supports where sensation could not reliably warn her of pressure. Her skin remained closed. That did not make the hands available for a demonstration of what she might have done instead.

Ira listened to her own short account read back, with the ordinary eye rested. What she had seen on the way out stayed separate from the shouted words the boys had later repeated to her. The left cover stayed untouched. There was no attempt to make sight useful enough to pay for being believed.

Veer gave the moment in which he had used the small curve and the moments when he had been unable to repeat it. The shoulder and ribs were still being treated. He did not describe himself as a wall.

Dhruv stopped when the hand needed its next care. He could return to the unfinished part. A blade cut did not become a duty to keep talking until everyone else's account was complete.

The notebook left each room with less certainty than a legend would have wanted.

It had better facts.

---

The work payments took another kind of listening.

Vedant called the man who had arranged the helpers, with the hours checked the previous evening in front of him. The unhurt helper answered from his own place. He was not summoned back to receive money which could reach him without a road journey.

Ordinary agreed work. The extra emergency work he had actually chosen. The return expenses. Each entry was read out; he corrected one finish time and confirmed the rest.

Vedant checked the remaining travel money before authorising the transfers. It covered the agreed work and these checked extras. It would not replace a damaged car or turn into an unlimited promise about future treatment. He said what was being paid now and kept the other costs on a separate list.

The helper waited for the credit on his phone.

"Aa gaya," he said.

Only then did Vedant mark it paid.

---

The injured attendant wanted the same distinction kept.

His ordinary fee and emergency work were settled, and the immediate treatment and return costs for which he had receipts were reimbursed. The closed wound remained dressed. The restriction on lifting was on the actual treatment note, not a polite suggestion he could ignore if the next employer needed another pair of hands.

He named the work he would miss during the present restriction. Vedant recorded that separately with the work contact and fixed the next conversation after the already-planned review. Neither asked him to sign that everything arising from the injury had been settled forever.

"Kal ka kaam nahi kar paaunga," the man said.

"Woh aaj ki fees mein nahi chhupega," Vedant answered.

The man checked the payment himself. He did not have to say that saving the students had made the lost work worthwhile before the number could be acknowledged.

---

The drivers' accounts were checked separately from the vehicle damage.

The owner had the police contact and the scene reference. The nearer car's tyre, window and seat were not a repair bill to be deducted from the wounded driver's day. The damage claim would follow the scene examination and the owner's actual repair assessment; nobody invented a final amount from a glimpse of the spear.

The driver had his brow and vision reviewed again. He was still not to drive. He could make the ordinary seated journey arranged for him, keep the dressing protected and attend the follow-up; any change in the symptoms named by the clinician would bring him back sooner.

The other driver had rested properly overnight. His own check and account left no new symptom requiring continued treatment there. He had the intact car's keys and agreed on the ordinary return run after the work payment was settled. It was not another unbooked day waiting for the students to decide where they might go.

The attendant could travel seated too, with the arm protected and no unloading task. He chose the offered return with the drivers after confirming his own destination and care arrangements. Nobody presumed that three working men lived at one address because they had arrived in the same emergency.

---

Sitara watched the three prepare to leave.

The second driver took the wheel. The brow-injured driver sat belted in front; the attendant took the rear seat with space for the dressed arm. Their bags were placed so neither hurt man had to hold one away from a wound. The return stops had been agreed with them, not guessed by the person with the keys.

The intact vehicle drove away.

The extra helper had already ended his duty. These three had theirs ended too, without ending the continuing care or the unpaid questions on the separate list. The staff had the patients' necessary assistance; no one found a sudden gap which required a paid man to come back out of gratitude.

Rudra heard only after his rest that the men had left and the checked work had been paid. He asked Vedant to keep the remaining costs where he could return to them later.

A debt did not become smaller because the person who carried it had finally gone home.

At the approach, the following shift arrived before the waiting one left.

The two officers who had taken the wrist-injured man for review came back in the police van after the receiving duty staff had accepted the custody handover and the officers had finished their own rest. The pair at the mouth and road showed them the present positions. Nobody had emerged from the passage during their watch. Nobody had gone in for a speculative search.

The new pair took those two posts. The outgoing pair had the older police vehicle which had been left there, its keys included in the handover.

A shift could end without ending the watch.

---

The scene examination on Earth had reached the point where the damaged car could be released for recovery.

The spear was photographed in place before it was removed. Its point and shaft were secured as a weapon, separately from the vehicle's return. The tyre cut, broken glass, punctured seat and the driver's account stayed attached to the case. Releasing the car did not erase them.

The owner arranged the recovery truck after receiving that permission.

It came by the ordinary road, stopped behind the turn and was brought forward only when the officers had checked the collection details. The car went onto it rather than being driven with a replaced tyre and an unsafe window because an engine could still start. No one called the brow-injured driver back to prove that the vehicle was his responsibility.

The people who had supplied the chairs sent their authorised collector in a small goods vehicle. He arrived while the truck was being secured, folded both empty chairs and checked them against the collection note before loading them. The receiving contact acknowledged their return. They did not remain borrowed furniture beside an opening for the next injured group.

One of the outgoing officers called Sitara with the completed handover and collections before leaving. She kept the source with the update when she passed it on: he had seen the truck and the collector, not merely received a promise that somebody would come.

The repair assessment still belonged to the owner. The road no longer held the students' chairs and a hired car as unpaid extensions of yesterday's journey.

---

By the time the truck passed the nearest tea stall, the spear had acquired a second point in one telling and lost its shaft in another.

A customer said a driver had caught it.

The man who had been kept back on his motorcycle said he had seen the ambulance leave, which was true, and therefore knew exactly how the fight had happened, which was not. He described a door being torn clean off. The door was still on the car passing the stall.

The recovery driver pointed to it without stopping.

Someone laughed. The story shortened itself around the visible hinge, then began growing elsewhere.

One version put nobody hurt. Another filled both sides of the road with bodies. Neither teller had been allowed near the people receiving care. The public facts were smaller: a damaged vehicle, emergency crews, police at the approach, a road kept clear while a patient came out. No patient address was needed to make those facts travel.

The old man pouring tea said the driver would still lose a day's work whether he had caught a spear or hidden behind a door.

For a moment the customers had less to say.

Then somebody asked what the tea cost now, as though this too might have changed during the rescue.

---

At the receiving centre, the account came back through the vehicle owner's short call with his driver, and from him, by permission, to Vedant. It did not come as a leaked photograph of a patient's face or a forward of Ira's private notes.

Vedant passed on the door which had stubbornly remained attached in spite of the local account. Veer laughed, stopped when the ribs objected and asked him not to improve the joke.

The local telling could have the impossible door. He would keep the actual shoulder.

The official update, when the statement constable gave it, contained fewer miracles.

The three men identified in the attack were all in care under police control. The first remained with the team treating the leg. With his permission, a short clinical update had been sent to the rescue crew: urgent treatment had restored blood flow to the foot, but the injury was serious and needed continued observation. That was not a promise about walking or the final recovery. A full interview would wait on the clinical team.

The wrapped boot had reached him too. The constable had the receiving guard's acknowledgment of the property handover, not a photograph of a grateful patient.

The cheek-wounded man had been received and examined. The third man's wrist and lip had been reviewed, with continued care and restraint arranged. None had been released back through the passage because the emergency was inconvenient to hold.

The clinical worker had completed her own knee examination and wound care after handing over the leg patient. She was off carrying work, with rest and follow-up arranged. The cut constable's wound had been cleaned and treated, and he too was off the duties which would load it. The updates about their own injuries were shared with their permission; neither had asked the students to make the hurt worthwhile.

---

No new attacker had appeared during the reported watch. The last checked near-side line was passable; the lowered timber and its two retained blocks were recorded. Beyond the bend remained beyond the account.

The rescue was finished. The weapons were held. The immediate pursuit and the attempt to prevent a departure had been stopped, and the continuing watch had passed to people who had actually arrived to take it.

For this raid, there was finally a past tense.

There was no such tense yet for every person who had wanted the road, or every danger elsewhere in Pataal. The shouted labels still belonged to the mouths which had used them. No one filled the empty place above the three men with a convenient principal's name.

That unanswered history did not send an injured student back out to finish what the professionals had just brought to an end.

Veer agreed to the short conversation after his next care.

They used audio from their separate rooms. The nurse placed Rudra's phone where he did not have to keep a wrist raised. Veer had his own supported position and no audience to entertain.

For a while they spoke about the door.

"Poora nikal gaya tha?" Rudra asked.

"Agli chai tak waapas lag gaya."

He heard the careful breath after his friend's laugh and let the joke end before it became work.

Then he said what he had asked to say.

"Tukda tere paas aaya tha."

"Dekha tha. Aid hataaya."

"Main tujhe—"

"Pata hai. Meri taraf nahi kiya tha."

Rudra looked at the phone.

"Phir bhi aaya."

There was a small silence, with the background sounds of two rooms which could not quite become one through a speaker.

"Haan," Veer said.

---

Rudra did not ask him to say it had been nothing.

Veer described where the splinter had fallen and the little movement he had made to clear it. He did not enlarge the near miss into an injury which had never happened. He did not shrink it into a thing Rudra was foolish to remember.

"Mujhe us waqt wheel dikh raha tha," Rudra said. "Bas staff door karna tha."

"Ab aur bhi dikh raha hai."

"Ab der se dikh raha hai."

"Haan."

The second yes did not shut him out. It was a place where neither had to rescue the other from the facts.

Veer needed to change the shoulder position. They stopped talking while he got the help he needed. Rudra waited through the small sounds on the line, without making the wait into a demand that his friend come back sounding happier.

When Veer returned, he asked whether Rudra had eaten.

They argued briefly about whether what had arrived on the tray could be called enough salt. It was the nearest either came to an ordinary afternoon.

The call ended before the comfortable part had to be spent on a promise about the next battle.

---

The later call to Varanasi was asked for on both sides.

Rudra wanted to tell his parents what had changed since the urgent call the previous day. They were awake after their own care and wanted the short update. He agreed that the care worker could give the present clinical facts first, and that the account of other people's injuries would stay limited to what had been shared for telling.

Pressure and breathing more settled. Continued observation. Wrist and legs protected. No new return to the field arranged.

Ram listened from his supported place. Rukmini's arm rested on a cushion beside the phone. Neither had been asked to get ready for a journey.

Then Rudra spoke for himself.

The rescue people were out. The three men they knew had been taken into care and police control. The road watch had been properly handed over. He did not know the leg patient's final outcome, and he did not send his parents somebody else's treatment notes to fill the space.

"Apna bayaan diya," he said.

Ram's gaze changed a little.

"Jitna yaad tha?"

"Jitna khud dekha. Jo mere haath se hua, woh bhi."

Ram waited.

Rudra told him about the splinter beside Veer, without trying to make the boy's unhurt skin proof of perfect control.

---

His father did not call it a successful exercise.

He did not begin instructing Rudra to repeat the movement over a video call so they could see where it had gone wrong. His own treatment was still in progress; his son's current room was for care.

"Abhi dohraana mat," Ram said.

"Nahi kar raha."

The two sentences were smaller than everything which had once passed between a father's instruction and a son's obedience. They did not repair it all. Today, they left a hurt hand out of another test.

Rukmini asked whether the men who had helped them had eaten and been paid.

Rudra gave the checked answer, including the costs still on the separate list. She nodded at the part which was done and did not turn the unfinished part into his next task before sleep.

The call stopped when the parents' own care was due.

After the screen went quiet, Rukmini held the fingers of her free hand against her wrist for a moment. Her pulse kept its own hurried time. Ram watched without asking her to pretend the update had taken all the fear away.

She released the wrist, settled the supported arm and called for the help she needed with her own evening medicine.

In Mirzapur, Rudra let the phone be moved out of the way and took the next ordinary rest.

By evening, the corridor had changed shifts.

The nurse coming on introduced herself before touching the support. Rudra told her which position had begun to ache. She checked, adjusted and waited for his answer instead of inheriting a comfortable patient from the previous entry on the chart.

The ache eased.

He would remain under the observation still needed. There was no discharge hidden inside the quiet end of the day. He agreed to the plan without asking whether staying would make him late for something nobody had actually arranged.

No return journey was waiting behind the door.

---

Ira did not want to make another record that evening.

When Sitara passed on the choice, Rudra asked her to leave it there. His account did not become more urgent because it was finally an account he wanted to give honestly. Ira could listen another day, or keep only the part she chose with its source attached. Her eye was not a public shelf which had to remain open because people had survived something worth remembering.

Aadhya had sent word through the same agreed contact that she wanted quiet after the day's care. He sent back a good night, not a question which would require her to reassure him. The message was passed on as a message, without adding an invitation to sit beside him until he slept.

He put neither girl's absence on the list of things the attack had taken from him.

They were allowed an evening.

---

Vedant came after his own forearm check and meal, not from a watch he had kept unbroken since the ambulance.

The dressing was clean. The hand stayed out of helping when the nurse made the last adjustment to Rudra's supported leg. Vedant waited until she had finished and the agreed quiet interval remained, then asked whether Rudra wanted company.

"Thodi der."

He took the chair, leaving the way to the bed clear.

For a while they listened to a cart wheel which caught at the same floor joint on every pass. Somewhere a man was bargaining with a relative about one more spoon of food. The quarrel had the weary affection of something which had happened before this day and expected to happen after it.

Rudra wanted that kind of tomorrow very badly.

He had once thought wanting an ordinary thing made a person less ready for the extraordinary one. Now he could see how much extraordinary labour had gone into letting people go home, receive their wages, complain about salt and end a call before they were too tired to want the next one.

He did not want to repay it by making them do it all again because he could not bear the word *wait*.

---

"Us waqt bas staff dikh raha tha," he said.

Vedant turned towards him.

"Abhi baaki jagah bhi dikh rahi hai. Veer kahaan tha. Attendant ka haath. Peechhe wall."

"Haan."

"Ab dekhne se kal waala tukda waapas nahi aayega."

"Nahi."

It was not harsh. Vedant had no use for turning a fact into a sentence Rudra would have to serve forever. The splinter had fallen. It had not struck Veer. Both belonged to what they knew.

"Tumne kaha force staff se aage gayi," he said. "Apne dimaag mein us line ko chhota mat karna."

Rudra nodded.

"Par sirf woh line rakh ke hamla mat mita dena," Vedant added. "Tum kursi mein the. Woh bhi hua tha."

The boy looked down at the protected wrist.

He had been attacked. He had answered dangerously. Neither fact could be used to swallow the other. If he made himself only the injured person, he would learn nothing about the piece of wood beside his friend. If he made himself only the danger, he would turn every hand which had helped him into a hand mistaken to have tried.

That was not an account either.

---

He asked for the water and took it as allowed.

The good hand could hold the small ordinary weight. It could press the call bell. It could lie still while someone moved a leg which needed care. Those things were still available to it, even after the red-black had made everybody look first for what it might break.

He put the glass down before he began to speak again.

The ward's evening light lay along his fingers. No hiding layer had returned while he rested. No new certainty had arrived with the morning's steadier pressure. The force at the bend was still something he had used once in desperation, not a result he could summon safely because the account now contained the word *once*.

Across from him, Vedant's hurt forearm rested where it could stop being useful for a few minutes.

Rudra looked at it, then at his own empty hand.

He did not ask for a larger blow. He did not ask whether wanting to protect someone could be made an excuse which worked in advance. When the time came to consider anything more, it would have to include the people standing beyond the thing he meant to strike.

For now, he had only a question, asked from a bed he was not leaving.

"Haath khulne se pehle," he said, "kya aap mujhe rukna sikhaa sakte hain?"
