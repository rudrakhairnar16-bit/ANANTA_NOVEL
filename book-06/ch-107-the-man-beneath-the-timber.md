# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND SEVEN — THE MAN BENEATH THE TIMBER

---

> *"Needing help did not make the injured man innocent.*
> *Remembering what he had done did not make him lighter to lift."*
> *— Ira's later register, the second account of the return bend*

---

"Use waapas andar mat bhejiye," Vedant said.

"Nahi bhej rahe," the constable answered. "Bithaaya hai. Ilaaj ke liye gaadi aa rahi hai."

The two short sentences separated the things which fear had put together. The man on Earth was being cared for. The man he claimed was pinned inside still had to be found.

Dhruv leaned towards the phone without lifting the dressed hand.

"Kis cheez ke neeche?"

The constable put the question to the person beside him. There was a pause, a reply too far from the phone to distinguish, then the officer's voice again.

"Lakdi ka bada tukda. Patthar waale mod pe. Pair phansa hai, bol raha hai."

"Kisi ne dekha?"

Not from the Earth side. The next bend took the view. They had heard a sound from inside which might have been a voice, but nobody there could identify it. The request for rescue support had gone through with the warning that armed men had used the passage and one might still be beyond the visible line.

The constable did not ask the patients to come and look for him.

---

Sitara asked for the phone.

She gave the officer the order of the known bends, the uneven lip and the broad turning place beyond. If the man meant the rock bend they had used, it was close to the lip, not at the farther outlook. She could not place his body from a sentence relayed over a phone.

The officer asked whether someone who knew that short line could brief the rescue crew at the approach.

"Main aa sakti hoon," she said. "Pehle baahar ka kaam. Andar ka haal dekh kar hi."

No promise to walk an unchecked crew into an occupied street.

One of the four constables would come to collect her. The other three would remain at the approach. The wounded man would not be kept untreated until her arrival or until he had produced a satisfactory story.

She gave the receiving entrance, not the patients' rooms, and agreed to meet the officer there.

---

Dhruv looked at her.

"Main line bataa sakta hoon."

"Bataa do. Chalne ki baat nahi kar rahi."

He gave the small facts which mattered to feet and hands: the place the chair had reversed, the low rock which stopped a direct throw, the shaft he had left diagonally inside. The last one might already have been moved. Two armed men had gone back past it.

Sitara repeated the order back to him.

He corrected one distance, then let the account be enough instead of trying to attach his hurt body to it as a guarantee.

"Haath ka check chhodna mat," she said.

"Tum waapas aake poochh lena."

It sounded like an instruction about a dressing. It gave her an ordinary future task on this side of the trip.

---

She had eaten and been off her feet while the girls were examined. She was tired, not injured. Before leaving, she spoke to the care lead about her own condition and the task she had offered: a road journey, an Earth-side briefing, any further movement dependent on what could actually be checked there.

The woman asked about dizziness, pain, the day's exertion and what Sitara could do without somebody else's support. Sitara answered, walked the corridor and came back without hiding the fatigue. There was no finding which required her to become another inpatient. There was also no clinical opinion which could make a fight safe.

The girls' necessary help was handed over to the ward staff. Ira would not have to follow two voices at once because the familiar one had left; the nurse taking the next movement task introduced herself and gave the same short directions Ira asked for.

Aadhya was awake when Sitara told her the limited plan.

"Humko lene mat bhejna," Aadhya said.

"Nahi bhejoongi."

It was a promise Sitara could make without knowing the shape of the timber.

---

Vedant would keep the contact while he rested between checks. Rudra was asleep under observation; the new report was not a reason to wake him into a task. Veer was told only the part he needed to understand why Sitara would be away: a possible trapped man, a police request for the known approach, no patient going back.

He asked that the crew be told about the ground where a wheel caught.

Sitara said it was already in the account.

At the receiving entrance she found the constable who had come by the ordinary road. His vehicle was waiting. She got into the passenger seat, put on the belt and told him to stop before the rough turn so they could hear the latest report before going farther.

They stopped before the rough turn as agreed.

The constable called the approach and listened. The wounded man was still there, now under the ambulance crew's care. Two rescue workers had arrived in a utility vehicle with lifting equipment. There had been no new visible arrival from the passage.

Sitara asked about the sound from inside.

It had come again. Still too far round the rock to identify a person. The officer did not call it a confirmed answer merely because a second sound resembled the first.

They drove the last stretch and stopped behind the road bend.

The light was going out of the ordinary afternoon. One of the rescue workers was testing a hand lamp; the other had put a jack, packing blocks and a coil of rope on clear ground beside the vehicle. Both had been told about the weapons and the unknown man still beyond the inspected stretch. Neither was being sent in to discover those facts with his body.

---

The man with the cheek wound was sitting under the ambulance's open rear door.

A dressing covered part of the face Sitara had seen beyond the chair. The clothes were the same, and the remaining piece of staff lay apart from him where the constable had put it. She recognized the man who had stood between them and the return bend.

She said that much to the officer, quietly.

It was a witness identification, not a name for his whole life.

The clinical worker had cleaned and dressed the facial wound, checked his responses and begun the care it needed. For the moment he was stable enough to remain under her observation while the report of a potentially more urgent trapped patient was assessed. The ambulance's stretcher was ready. If his condition changed, she would not wait for the scene to become convenient.

Sitara asked whether he could answer a few questions about the timber.

"Thoda," the worker said. "Beech mein rokna pade toh rokenge."

---

He watched Sitara come closer.

His gaze went to the place beside her where the chair had been. Then to her hands. He did not ask where the students were. She did not offer their location.

"Mod pe kya rakha hai?" she asked.

"Lakdi. Lamba."

He held the good part of his mouth away from the dressing as he spoke. One hand made a sloping line in the air, then stopped when the movement pulled the cheek.

"Ek sira neeche aaya. Pair ke upar."

"Kaun sa aadmi?"

"Chhuri waala. Jo peechhe tha."

The constable asked whether the weapon was still in his hand.

The man said he did not know. He had heard him shouting. He had got over the timber and come towards Earth. His own blood had made it hard to see where he put a foot.

He asked for water. The clinical worker checked him before giving the permitted small sip.

---

Sitara asked why the timber had been moved there.

The man looked towards the opening.

"Raasta rokna tha."

"Kiska?"

He made a small impatient movement with the unbandaged side of his face, then winced at its cost.

"Jo aayega. Jo jaayega."

It was his account. There was no reason yet to make every word a fact. But it fitted the tyre cut, the attempts to take the bend and the time spent disabling a vehicle while hurt people were still close enough to reach.

"Chehre diye the tum logon ko?" the constable asked.

"Raasta diya tha."

The answer did not establish that no one had wanted Rudra dead. It did take something from the easy picture in which every hand raised that afternoon had come carrying a complete knowledge of the boy beneath the gold.

The next useful questions were about the wood, the stones beneath it and the space in which a person could still breathe.

---

The man said one end had been put against the low wall; the other had been hauled towards stacked stone near the rock. The work had not held as they expected. He repeated that the blade man's leg was caught. He could not give a clear answer about the third man after that.

The clinical worker ended the questions when his breathing grew hurried.

Sitara moved away at once. The officer kept the account with its source attached, including what the speaker had failed to see. His hands were kept empty; that was not the same thing as pretending the broken staff had been harmless.

The two rescue workers listened to the short version.

One asked whether the injured man could be reached without going out into the wider street. Sitara said they would first check that limited distance through and back. If it was not there to use, an old memory of the line would not make it available.

The constable who would go with them checked the other three officers' positions on Earth. One would cover the mouth from the road side; one would stay with the ambulance and the man being treated; one would hold the road turn.

Nobody gave the passage a promise about what it would find.

Sitara went first with the constable, only as far as the known bend.

She had told him what she could still recognize and what had changed since the morning's inspection. He carried the hand lamp. She used its ordinary light to check where a foot would land, and they stopped before each place where the next piece of ground had to be seen afresh.

The old natural passage admitted them without a word or a gesture asked of either body.

At the uneven lip they paused. Beyond it, the rock bend was the one she had named. The low wall was there. So was something which had not been across the line when she had last walked it.

A heavy baulk lay sloping from the wall towards a broken stack of stone.

Under its lowered end was a man's leg.

---

The man was alive.

He lay turned towards the near side of the bend, with one hand braced uselessly against the timber. The other was beside the blade on the ground. Sitara recognized the face she had seen beyond Dhruv's guard, before the staff man took the return.

"Haath khaali," the constable said.

The fingers opened. The blade stayed where it lay. The officer brought it out of the man's reach with his boot and kept the hands in view.

Sitara did not kneel against the load.

"Saans aa rahi hai?"

"Pair," he said. "Nikaalo."

"Koshish mat karo uthne ki. Log aaye hain. Pehle dekhna hoga."

He pushed against the timber once more and cried out when the movement reached the trapped leg. Then he lay still, panting, the empty hand flat on stone.

Beyond the baulk, the street opened towards the broader place where they had turned the chair. Broken ground and the off-line wall gap remained beyond it. Nobody was visible there in the lamp's short view. Sitara could not turn that into an account of everything hidden by the wall.

---

The timber was not an old lintel which had fallen from above.

There was no doorway over that patch to supply it. Drag marks crossed the grit towards the new stone stack; a loose length of rope lay around the far end. Some of the supporting stones had spilled down beside the pinned man. The shapes were ordinary enough for the constable to describe without needing Sitara's name for the realm.

He asked the injured man whether anyone else was close.

The answer came between breaths.

"Pata nahi."

They did not fill the unknown space with a promise.

Sitara looked at the clear way behind them, the lip, the next turn towards Earth. They had reached the man without going out into the wider street. They would still have to return with more than two unburdened bodies.

She said they were going back to bring the people who could help. The man's empty hand rose a little from the ground.

"Mat jaao."

"Saamaan laana hai."

She could promise the next act. She could not promise the leg beneath the wood would be unchanged when it was free.

---

They walked the return line all the way back to Earth.

The lip had no new obstruction. The narrow turn still needed its angle respected. The recovered shaft Dhruv had left was now against the rock rather than across the floor; who had moved it was not visible in its new position.

Sitara reported what they had actually found, including the live man, the empty hands, the removed blade and the ground beyond which they had not checked. The constable confirmed his own observations.

The short line through and back was available at that moment. The wider street was not cleared. The third attacker was still unaccounted for.

The rescue workers asked about carrying a board around the turn. They took the empty board from their vehicle and tried the limited route with Sitara showing the angle, while the constable watched the outward side. They came back with it before making the next plan.

A remembered width became a width they had actually used.

---

The two workers checked the jack and packing blocks again.

The task was a pinned leg beneath one lowered timber end, with a far support which could move. They would shore before lifting, keep hands out of the unprotected gap and stop if the outward side could not be watched. The officer would guard that side, not help pull a load while an unknown armed person could come behind him.

Sitara would keep the route clear and pass the medical worker's questions back from the lip. They tested the distance with ordinary voices. The officer at the Earth mouth could hear her there and answer. Farther in, someone would have to come back with information.

Each worker said what he could do with the equipment he had brought. One wanted another block carried in first. They brought it.

Then all four went along the checked short line: Sitara, the two workers and the constable. The return remained a physical place behind them, not a promise to be kept by the injured man's gratitude.

The workers did not begin by pulling at the trapped man.

They looked at the ground under the jack, the baulk's sloping weight and the wall carrying its other end. One set a flat base on the uneven stone. The other placed the first packing blocks where they could catch a small change in height without putting fingers beneath unprotected wood.

The old rope was left out of their load.

They passed their own line around the wall end to limit its sideways roll, working from the checked near side while the constable watched the street. The line came back to a sound rock on their side. It was a restraint against movement, not a replacement for the blocks carrying weight.

The injured man tried to pull his leg when the jack first touched the timber.

"Abhi nahi," the worker said. "Hilaao mat."

The man stopped. The wood had not lifted yet.

---

Sitara went back to the lip with the medical questions answered as far as ordinary eyes could answer them.

The clinical worker wanted to assess the compressed leg herself before it was released. She checked the cheek-wounded man's condition again, left him with her colleague and the officer, then took the bag for the short route. Her colleague knew which changes meant calling her straight back for an immediate transfer rather than waiting for the second patient.

She had been told about the unaccounted attacker. Sitara told her what they had found through and back, where the outward view ended and what the constable was watching. The woman asked about getting a stretcher back around the turn. The empty-board trial gave her an answer which was more useful than an assurance.

"Main dekhoongi," she said. "Rokna pade toh pehle rukenge."

Before leading her in, Sitara used her phone on Earth to give Vedant the actual report. A living man pinned at the bend. New timber and piled stone. A clear short return at the time of the check. The wider street still unknown. Medical assessment before lifting.

Vedant repeated it back. No patient was requested.

---

Five people were now inside with the trapped man: the constable, the two rescue workers, Sitara and the clinical worker.

The officer had put the blade on a near rock out of the patient's reach, behind his own guarded position. He kept looking beyond the timber, not at the hands setting the blocks. There was a whole wider street he had not agreed to pronounce empty.

The clinical worker knelt at the man's near side and made the examination she could make with the leg still caught. She asked about the length of time, the pain and the feeling below the timber, checked the accessible limb and prepared for the problems releasing the pressure could bring. She did not declare the injury small because the man could shout.

He reached towards her bag.

The constable told him to leave his hands where they could be seen.

"Haath khaali rakho," Sitara said. "Jagah hum banaayenge."

The reaching hand came back. It lay open against the grit while the medical worker finished. The next movement would belong to the people holding the load, not to a desperate pull from beneath it.

---

In Mirzapur, Rudra woke during the next check.

The breathing was easier than it had been at the approach, still being watched. The nurse checked the supported leg and asked about the pressure before Vedant gave him Sitara's Earth-side message. It was not a live account from beyond the rock. It was what she had found before taking the clinical worker in.

Rudra lay still through the short telling.

"Wahi aadmi?"

"Sitara ne pehchaana. Chhuri waala."

He remembered the shaft striking the rim, the blade which had kept Dhruv behind them, the small awful distances for which everybody had paid. The man under the timber did not become somebody else in his mind simply because other hands were now trying to get him out.

"Mera bayaan bhi lena hoga," he said.

"Aaraam ke baad. Tumhara apna."

Rudra nodded. He could give an account without making another person's untreated leg its price. He could receive care without having to stand at every place where care was being given.

He told the nurse when the ankle support needed a slight change. It was the task in front of him, and he let it be one.

---

At the bend, the lead worker put the next block within reach of his partner.

They had room to raise the lowered end a little, fill the gained space and check before trying more. The clinical worker had named what she needed watched during the release. The board lay along the clear near side, ready for the move which would follow only when the man could be moved.

Sitara kept the lip and the backward line free of bags and loose tools. From there she could see the jack end and the officer's shoulder beyond it, not the whole hidden face of the low wall.

The new rope lay taut around the standing timber end. The loose old length lay beside it, useless to their lifting plan.

A thin scattering of grit came down from the far side of the wall.

The constable turned his lamp towards the sound.

The man who had thrown the spear stepped out of the off-line gap.

He came along the street side of the low wall, using the broken ground which had kept him out of the first view. There was no spear in his hand now. There was a short knife, held down beside the thigh until the constable's lamp found it.

"Chhuri neeche!"

The man stopped beyond the wall end of the timber.

The constable brought the lathi up. He could watch the man from behind the low rock or leave that cover and try to cross the narrow working space. He could not do both while keeping his body out of the trapped man's way.

"Kaam roko," he called to the workers.

The jack stopped where it had just taken the first touch of weight. One worker kept his hand on the control. The other moved away from the timber's unprotected side and checked that the packing still sat beneath it.

---

Sitara called from the lip towards Earth.

"Andar hathiyaar! Madad, lip tak!"

The answer came from the officer at the mouth, an ordinary voice over the distance they had tested. He was coming. The other two constables still had the ambulance and the road turn; nobody could fill every place by shouting a single name.

The clinical worker shifted her knee back behind the packing, preparing to withdraw if the officer could not keep the knife away.

The pinned man had begun trying to lift his shoulders against the wood again. She told him to stop pulling, kept one hand where he could see it and repeated the next small instruction until his gaze came back from the armed figure beyond the wall.

"Pair mat kheencho. Meri taraf dekho."

He turned his head.

There was no room in the working space for gratitude, innocence, surrender and pain to be sorted into clean separate containers. There was room for a bag to be moved out of a possible fall line. Sitara moved it. The board stayed clear for an evacuation which had not happened yet.

---

The spear man looked at the rope around the standing timber end.

"Peechhe hato," he said.

The constable repeated the order about the knife. The man's eyes went from the lathi to the jack, then along the new line running towards the near rock.

The lead worker spoke without taking his hand off the control.

"Rassi ko haath mat lagaana. Neeche aadmi hai."

The man under the baulk said something too broken by pain for Sitara to make out. Then one clear word reached the wall.

"Ruk."

The armed man put a hand against the timber instead.

It did not move far. It moved enough for a little dust to leave the wall beneath it.

The worker at the packing told his partner what he had seen. The jack stayed still. They were holding the space already gained, not trying to win the next fraction while another pair of hands changed the load.

---

At the Earth mouth, the third constable's position became empty for the few steps it took him to reach the lip.

The man at the road turn saw him go and held the public approach. The one beside the ambulance stayed with its driver and the cheek-wounded patient. They had heard Sitara's warning; they had not suddenly seen through the bend or acquired the right to leave a second open route unguarded.

The cheek-wounded man turned towards the sound from inside.

The ambulance driver told him to stay seated. He stayed, the treated face tight, both empty hands against his knees. No one asked him to prove remorse by going under the timber himself.

Inside, the clinical worker said she needed the patient's near side kept clear. The rescue worker moved one loose block which was not bearing weight, leaving the supporting pack untouched.

Sitara heard the arriving officer's foot at the lip and told him where the blade was, where the patient lay and which line of wood must not be crossed.

He came only far enough to see the threatened space before deciding the next step.

---

The pinned man looked past the clinical worker towards the board.

"Nikaalo," he whispered.

"Jagah bante hi," she said.

He was asking the people he had attacked to finish a task which the movement beyond the wall was making harder by the moment. His own lowered hands did not make the other man's knife disappear. The empty board did not become wide enough to take a timber's weight. Help remained a thing people did with the actual room left to them.

Sitara put her hand against the board's near handle and kept it ready. The body on it, if they could get him there, would need a supported leg and the same slow turn through which she had earlier guided her friends. Nothing in the new need had made the angle larger.

At the wall, the spear man drew his knife across the taut line.

---

The first fibres gave with a small, dry sound.

The worker at the packing called out. His partner held the jack still; the blocks were beneath the lowered end, but the line had been keeping the far end from turning sideways on the wall.

The constable moved towards the knife hand.

The man behind the wall put his boot against the loose stone under the timber and drove it forward. Grit fell. The new rope parted before the officer could reach him across the broken edge.

The far end shifted off its seat.

The clinical worker had started to rise from the patient's near side, one hand still steadying the leg they had not yet freed.

The baulk began to roll towards her back.
