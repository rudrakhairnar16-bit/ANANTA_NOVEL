# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND FIFTEEN — BEFORE HE WAS AN ENEMY

---

> *"The missing answer was a relationship, not an alias.*
> *Writing it down did not identify the man on the road."*
> *— Later private statement appendix, shared by consent*

---

The constable threw himself towards the clear Earth strip.

The corner hit where his heel had been. Its lower edge broke on impact, driving chips across his calf; the main piece turned once and settled across the mouth with a grinding finality which did not belong to everything behind it.

He landed on his side. The lathi went out of his hand.

For a moment he could see only the pale underside of the stone above his head and a strip of light beside it. Then he rolled away from that strip, dragging the struck leg after him until the outside shoulder was between his body and the opening.

"Main baahar hoon!" he shouted. "Raasta poora band nahi hai!"

His road partner answered.

The officer found the lathi with his sound foot, brought it close and used the rock, not the injured calf, to begin sitting up. Blood had soaked a small patch through the torn trouser leg. His ankle moved when he tried it. He did not turn that fact into permission to stand under the next falling piece.

---

A hand pushed through the narrow gap beside the fallen corner.

The constable saw it before he moved farther back. Fingers closed on the new edge. The stone shifted slightly, caught against the opposite face and stopped. A second push scraped grit across the officer's abandoned position.

The man behind it was alive and still coming.

The officer called that too. He could give his partner the sight of a hand and a moving obstruction, not a report that a collapsed entrance had imprisoned a whole threat for them.

He got onto the sounder leg with the lathi and the outer rock for support. The short retreat hurt enough to make him stop once. He stayed outside throughout, edging towards the bend until his partner could see his face.

"Pair laga," he said. "Andar mat jaana."

The clinical worker came to him on the protected side.

The patients were already behind the bend. Nobody had to take Rudra back towards the mouth to make space for another injury.

---

A police vehicle reached the outward road while the worker examined the calf.

Two constables got out. One had driven; the other carried an issued firearm with its muzzle lowered. The original road officer met them where the approach could still be closed to new arrivals. He described the attack, the fallen corner and the hand which his partner had just seen moving it.

He did not send them through the opening to confirm it.

The armed constable took a view from the outside cover, without pointing through an unseen bend. His companion went farther along the road to turn approaching traffic back before it reached the vehicles. Their car stayed facing out beyond the medical vehicle, clear of the original driver's exit.

There were four officers here now.

One of them was a patient.

The first worker cleaned the calf's cut, checked the nearby movement and feeling and covered it. The bleeding came under control. The painful leg still needed a proper receiving review, particularly if loading grew harder; the brief outside examination did not turn the chip's path into an image of everything beneath the skin.

The officer was taken off the mouth watch.

His replacement said the handover back to him before leaving: damaged last turn, moving man beyond the fallen piece, no entry, no assumption that the stone would hold.

The injured constable sat with the leg supported.

He let the new man keep the view.

---

Behind the bend, Rudra heard the new vehicle before Sitara told him who had come.

"Do police waale aur," she said. "Pehle waale ko pair mein laga. Check kar rahe hain."

"Nikal aaye?"

"Haan. Mere saamne baithe hain."

He did not raise himself to see. The new collar covering tugged when he tried to turn too far, and he let his head settle back on the folded support.

The clinical worker returned after the constable's dressing. Rudra gave the same answers about breathing and the foot, including the places which still hurt. The worker checked the covering and told him the second medical crew was close enough now to have spoken to the road officer directly.

Sitara looked at the car driver's clock.

The new ten-minute interval was running. She told him help was approaching but did not change his departure limit.

"Waapas ke liye ek police waale ko bhi bitha sakte hain?" she asked. "Check ke baad, agar baith kar jaa sakte hain. Aapko uthaana nahi hai. Wahi receiving entrance."

He looked at the available belted rear seat, then at the open road.

"Baith sakein toh. Main gaadi le kar hi rahoonga."

She accepted the boundary.

The second medical vehicle came into view before the waiting interval ended.

The road officer stopped it on the clear outward side first, spoke to the driver and pointed to the protected widening behind the bend. Only then did it come alongside.

Exactly two people got out: its driver and one clinical worker.

The two clinical workers spoke beside Rudra, not over an empty stretcher.

The first gave the back impact, the new fall and every injured place Rudra had named. Rudra added the earlier faint after exertion. The worker included the reopened collar, the hand's tremor and the tingling which had eased when padding changed. Rudra corrected the order when asked: the mouth had begun bleeding before the foot went through.

The second worker listened to his breathing, checked the current observations and asked about the toes again. The pain was still there. The new tingling was not.

"Khade nahi hona," he said. "Yahin se lete hain."

The second driver brought their stretcher alongside and secured its wheels. With both drivers and both workers available for this one transfer, Sitara could keep her covered hand out of it. Rudra moved with the carry sheet under their direction, the leg and back supported together. They paused once for the knee's position, then settled him onto the new padding.

The first worker did not leave until the second had taken the handover and checked the result.

There was somebody else's qualified attention here now.

Sitara unclenched the unhurt hand from the bag strap.

---

Dhruv and Vedant left in the first ambulance.

The worker got into the seat which had been kept for him. The driver closed the rear, checked the outward road with the original constable and pulled away. Dhruv's leg remained supported in the secured stretcher; Vedant had his own belt and protected forearm. Neither was left behind to help Rudra through another emergency.

Rudra heard the engine recede beyond the next stretch of road.

"Pahunch kar khabar lenge," the second worker said. "Ab aapka check."

He had begun examining the guarded arm when Sitara returned from the car.

She had told its driver that the new crew was here. The clock left them a few minutes in the second extra interval. They would use the return, not buy another wait because she disliked the feeling of leaving.

The injured officer had been assessed for a short supported seated journey. He was alert, the dressed cut was not actively bleeding, and the first worker had given him the findings and the need for receiving review. His partner agreed he was off duty at the mouth and should go for that review. No one called the leg uninjured.

The driver accepted the changed passengers and the same receiving entrance: Sitara and the injured constable, not a stretcher squeezed into a family car. The return price and both paid waiting intervals stayed on the agreed account. No lifting job was added.

---

Sitara put Rudra's own phone beside the new padding after taking it from his trouser pocket at his request.

The screen had a smear on it. She wiped it with the clean corner of a cloth from the bag, without opening the unanswered messages.

"Yeh yahin," she said. "Bag mere paas. Jo chahiye ho, bol dena."

He looked at the zipped compartment as though he had forgotten what ordinary things could be inside one.

"Charger hai?"

"Haan."

"Toh phone band hone se pehle laga dijiyega."

She knew he was not asking for an inventory.

"Receiving par. Aur tum pahunch kar khud poochh lena."

He nodded. The next worker's hand was under his forearm, checking the painful movement without asking him to reproduce the blow. Sitara waited until that check had ended before bending close enough for him to hear her without raising his head.

"Ab main jaa rahi hoon. Inko sab de diya hai."

"Apna haath bhi dikhaaiyega. Wahaan."

"Dikhaaungi."

She did not promise to stop hurting before he next saw her.

---

The constable used his sounder leg and the help actually offered by the second driver to reach the car's rear seat.

The driver of the car stayed at the wheel. Sitara fastened her own front passenger belt with the unhurt hand, then checked that the officer had the room and support agreed for the dressed calf. The assisting medical driver helped him settle and clip his belt before returning to Rudra's vehicle.

The waiting clock stopped before the new limit.

The car moved out along the clear road, carrying its three actual occupants back towards Mirzapur. Payment would follow the completed return, with the extra waiting included, not vanish because the passenger beside the driver had become quiet.

Sitara looked back only until the bend hid Rudra's ambulance.

Then she faced the road she was on.

---

Rudra asked the clinical worker whether a short call would interfere with what he was doing.

"Abhi kar sakte ho. Sawal poochhoon toh pehle uska jawab."

He agreed.

The driver was preparing the vehicle for the protected loading. The worker was recording the present findings after the transfer. The armed officer still watched the obstruction, and the third officer kept ordinary traffic away from the approach.

Rudra rested the phone in the soft fold beside his ear rather than holding the shaking arm up.

Ram answered on the third ring.

"Papa. Maa paas hain? Dono sun sakte hain abhi?"

He waited for an answer before trying to be brave for either of them.

Ram asked Rukmini first.

Rudra heard the ordinary question carried away from the phone, then her answer, a little farther from the microphone. Their own afternoon care had finished. They could listen for a few minutes, not indefinitely.

"Bolo," she said.

He told them why they had gone to the road: a statement about the old Earth-side positions, not another attempt at a form. He told them the photographs had stopped when a new man attacked the mentors inside. Dhruv had come out hurt. Vedant had been cut off. Rudra had chosen to go with Sitara after her current report.

He did not call that last part the doctors' plan.

"Purana roop aaya tha," he said. "Unko nikaalte waqt. Phir band ho gaya. Pair ke neeche ka hissa toota. Sitara laayi hain. Ab doosre medical waale ke paas hoon."

Rukmini asked about his breathing.

He answered. Then the collar, the mouth, the leg: he gave her the findings he had been given, including what still needed a fuller examination. No broken bone had been ruled out. He had not fainted during this encounter.

"Maa, main baat kar raha hoon. Dard bhi hai. Dono sach hain."

She let out the breath he could hear at the receiver.

"Dono bataate rehna. Sirf pehla nahi."

---

The worker asked Rudra whether the support was pressing at the heel.

He answered before returning to the phone. Ram waited through the interruption without demanding that a frightened father's question become the most urgent job beside the stretcher.

"Woh aadmi kaun tha?" Ram asked when Rudra came back.

"Naam nahi pata. Dhruv ne taaqat ka andaaza diya. Ohda nahi."

He described the wall being moved by hand and the scraped arm he had seen. He did not send a photograph; none had been taken of this man for him to offer.

"Isse pehchaan nahi sakta," Ram said.

The refusal to identify sounded almost sharp. Then his voice softened, not into reassurance, but into a correction of what he had meant.

"Main nahi jaanta woh kaun hai. Jo nahi jaanta, uska naam abhi tere darr mein nahi daaloonga."

Rudra watched a fly settle on the vehicle's metal step. It rubbed its forelegs together with a patient attention he envied.

"Lekin kisi aur ko aap pehchaante the," he said. "Aegis Meridian ke neta ko. Bargad par jo aapse lada tha."

"Haan."

"Kaun tha woh aapka?"

---

The line stayed open.

Ram did not ask for another day before answering. Rudra could hear a small scrape at the other end, perhaps the phone being held differently. He could not know more from the sound, and for once he did not try.

"Mera guru tha," Ram said.

The sentence was plain enough that Rudra heard it without understanding its full size.

"Aapko sikhaaya tha?"

"Haan. Aur baad mein main unke saath lada hoon. Ek hi taraf. Sirf teacher nahi the. Mere ladne ke saathi bhi the."

Not an enemy who happened to know where Ram would put a foot. Someone who had once stood on the side towards which Ram turned when he needed another man there.

Rudra's good fingers began closing on the edge of the blanket.

He noticed and let them loosen.

Nothing in the answer belonged to the man breaking stone beyond the road bend. It supplied no new insignia, no shared master, no reason to call every attacker a piece of one familiar betrayal. It changed one relationship Rudra had actually asked about.

That was enough to change the old fight in his memory.

---

"Aapko unko rokna pada," he said.

"Haan."

"Apne guru ko."

"Jo kar rahe the, usko. Mera guru hona uska haq nahi ban gaya."

Ram did not sound proud of having found a hard sentence. Rudra heard weariness in it, and something he could not separate cleanly from grief. He did not need to separate it before believing the answer.

Rukmini spoke from beside him.

"Abhi itna sun sakte ho?"

She was asking Rudra, not deciding that he had become too fragile to hear his own father's life.

He checked himself before saying yes to more.

The worker was ready to change the stretcher's height. His back hurt when he tried to lift his head, and the phone had slipped from the fold towards the collar covering.

"Abhi itna hi," Rudra said. "Mujhe move karenge."

"Theek," Ram answered at once.

There was no demand to finish the history in payment for its first honest line. No instruction to stay by the opening until his father could arrive. Ram asked him to let the crew do its work and to send the next actual news when he could.

"Aap dono apna bhi," Rudra said. He left the sentence unfinished because they knew which medicines, which rests, which stubborn small departures from their own instructions he meant.

Rukmini said they would.

They ended the call before the stretcher moved.

The worker put the phone safely beside Rudra's belongings on the padding and asked whether he was ready for the lift.

This time the question concerned a height somebody else would change.

The stretcher rose under the crew's hands.

Rudra kept the recovering wrist on the padding. When the height changed, the supported ankle throbbed more sharply; he said so, and the worker checked it before releasing the brake for the short roll towards the rear doors.

The doors were opened fully and held clear of the loading path.

The driver inspected the fastening which would secure the stretcher inside. Rudra watched him test it with an ordinary pull. The click sounded too small to deserve the relief it gave him.

It would be good to have something hold because somebody had checked it.

He let the thought stay about the vehicle.

The answer on the phone had not made the back less tender or the leg easier to move. He would have to think about his father's teacher later, when the next question did not compete with the movement of a stretcher wheel.

---

In the car, Sitara kept the bag on the floor where it would not slide onto the injured officer's leg.

He had reported his departure and the handover to his station by phone. Now he watched the road through the rear window, the lathi secured down beside the seat rather than laid across the passengers.

"Aaj ka statement nahi hua," he said.

"Nahi."

"Gaadi ki jagah waala hissa—"

"Woh baad mein theek karenge."

The driver slowed for ordinary traffic joining from a side lane. Sitara watched the back of his head, the small irritation in the way he moved his shoulders, and felt an unreasonable gratitude for the irritation. There was still a world in which another vehicle could be merely badly driven.

The officer shifted his calf and winced.

"Rukiye?" the driver asked, seeing him in the mirror.

"Nahi. Bas position."

Sitara asked him to tell her if it changed again. She did not reach back with the covered hand to adjust a leg she could not see properly. He settled the support himself within the position shown to him.

They continued.

---

The driver confirmed the receiving entrance as they reached the turn for the return route.

Sitara repeated it and sent the arrival desk a short ordinary update: two people needing review, travelling with their driver: one officer with a dressed calf, her own hand needing attention. Dhruv and Vedant were ahead in the first medical vehicle. Rudra had been handed to the second crew and had not left when she did.

She did not report him already following.

Ira's reply asked whether the bag needed to be collected. Sitara said she could bring it to the receiving point herself and would ask for help if that changed. Ira had her own eye-rest interval. No one needed her to spend it staring at a screen to keep the others alive.

Sitara put her phone down.

The injured officer looked at the bandage on her knuckles.

"Aapne andar se unko laaya?"

"Haan."

"Akeli?"

"Woh bhi madad kar rahe the. Jitna ho sakta tha."

The man nodded. After a while he said that he had been able to move his own leg too, after the stone fell, but the few steps out had been longer than they looked from the road.

Neither of them corrected the other's distance.

The car went on towards the actual receiving team.

---

At the mouth, the armed constable saw the fallen corner move farther than it had moved before.

He stepped back behind the outer cover and called the change to the original road officer. The second backup officer kept the farther road closed to ordinary traffic; there was still no crowd behind the vehicles, no curious man being permitted to come and see whether a photograph would explain the noise.

The stone's lower point had found a groove in the Earth grit.

The hand at its side pushed again. This time it slid into that groove instead of wedging higher against the opposite rock. A shoulder appeared in the opening behind it, then a bent head.

The constable gave a clear warning.

"Rukiye. Haath khaali rakhiye. Aage mat badhiye."

The man worked his second foot past the fallen edge.

His scraped arm was bloodier now. He shook one hand briefly before putting it back on the stone, as though his fingers had paid for the work of getting through. The constable could see his chest moving hard.

The man shoved the corner aside far enough to straighten.

He was fully on Earth.

There was no flash across the approach and no change in the shape of the passage. He had used the same damaged turns the others had used, at the pace his own obstruction had forced on him.

The constable retreated towards a view which would not put the patients behind the man's body.

His next warning was for the medical crew.

"Baahar aa gaya! Abhi nikaalo!"

The driver stopped the stretcher before its loading wheels entered the vehicle.

He looked towards the warning, then at the clinical worker. The original road constable was already coming back to the bend, waving them away from the exposed side.

"Andar lock nahi hua," the driver said.

"Toh peechhe. Dono saath."

They drew the stretcher clear of the rear step. Rudra felt the direction change beneath him, the supported ankle travelling with the bed instead of being left for him to gather up. His recovering hand stayed on the padding. He had no useful part in hauling a loaded stretcher with one shaking arm.

The gunshot came before they finished the turn.

---

The armed constable had fired from the outside shoulder at the first clear view with bare rock beyond the man.

The man's other upper arm jerked. A new dark patch spread there, separate from the long scrape on the opposite arm. He stumbled against the displaced stone, clamped a hand over the fresh wound and drew a breath which the constable could hear.

The shot had hurt him.

He did not fall and remain down.

He moved along the low outside edge, putting the projecting rock between himself and the firearm. His wounded arm stayed close until he needed it for balance. When he came into view again, he was farther round the bend, below the officer's first line.

The constable moved to change that line.

The medical vehicle and the patient were now beyond the man's shoulder. He could not put another shot through the view merely because the first had failed to finish the emergency. The weapon stayed ready while he sought an angle which did not offer somebody else's body as the stopping place.

The third officer, farther out, sent the last approaching motorcycle back.

There was no one on it to recruit.

---

"Gaadi se door," the road constable told the crew.

The second driver and worker took Rudra towards the firm outer strip beside the widening. The bed's wheels struck a shallow rut and stopped. The worker kept the leg supported while the driver changed the angle rather than jerking harder at the frame.

The sound of running feet shortened the interval they had.

Rudra could see the new man now, coming from the bend on his own two feet, blood on both arms and dust stuck to the wet places. The man looked at the stretcher, then at the vehicle beside it. He was breathing through his mouth.

The road constable put himself off the bed's immediate line.

"Ruko!" he shouted. "Patient hai!"

He kept the lathi in both hands but did not charge in to trade it for the strength which had moved the entrance. He backed towards the crew as the man crossed the last open part of the approach.

The worker spoke to Rudra without looking away from that movement.

"Neeche rehna. Hum hataa rahe hain."

He did not ask for a defence, a demonstration or a favour with a name.

---

The man struck the open rear door.

Metal boomed. The door bent against its check and swung partway back, narrowing the space the driver had just left. Rudra flinched at the sound; a pull went through his collar, and he told the worker before trying to raise the good arm.

The worker kept one hand at the stretcher and glanced at the covering.

"Haath neeche. Door le jaa rahe hain."

They got the wheels out of the rut. The bed rolled another short distance onto the firmer strip, fully outside the vehicle. Its brakes were set when the driver needed both hands to bring the raised frame lower without tipping the protected leg.

Rudra lay low enough now to see beneath the door's bent edge.

The armed constable had reached the outside of the bend. The original road officer called the crew's position to him, not an instruction to fire through it. The second backup officer remained on the outward road, keeping the approach beyond the police vehicle empty and calling for more help with the changed threat.

Nobody had abandoned the patient and the two people moving him.

Nobody had arrived who could make it move without effort.

---

The man put a hand beneath the ambulance's rear frame.

The driver saw it and stepped away from the door. He had the keys; he had not yet had a chance to take the driving seat after bringing Rudra to the loading point. A running engine would not have secured a stretcher which was still outside.

"Idhar se hato," he told the worker.

The worker came towards the head end of the bed, keeping Rudra between the raised sides and his own eyes on the man's hands. They needed to release the brakes and roll farther out, not stay beside the vehicle because its first position had been sensible.

The second hand went under the frame.

The man's freshly wounded arm shook. He changed that grip, brought the sounder side closer and bent his knees. Blood spotted the gravel beneath his elbow. His mouth opened with the effort.

Rudra heard the suspension creak.

The vehicle's rear body rose a little without the tyres leaving the ground. The driver moved farther from its corner and reached towards the bed's brake. The armed officer was still changing position beyond the bend; from here the body of the ambulance filled the place where a clear view of the man's chest would have been.

The worker told Rudra to keep the leg on its support.

Rudra looked at the dark gap opening above the gravel.

The man straightened farther.

Both rear tyres lifted clear of the ground.
