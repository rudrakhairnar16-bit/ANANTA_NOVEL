# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN — THE GROUND BEYOND THE TARGET

---

> *"They drew the first line where he wanted the force to end.*
> *The useful line was the one drawn beyond it."*
> *— Ira's later register, correction to the first practice account*

---

"Haan," Vedant said. "Lekin aaj is bistar par koi zor aazmaayenge nahi."

Rudra looked at the good hand on the sheet.

"Main abhi karne ko nahi keh raha."

"Pata hai. Main apni taraf se saaf kar raha hoon."

The answer settled between them without asking his body to prove how seriously he had meant the question. The wrist remained protected. The ankle rested where the nurse had just made it comfortable. There was no test hidden under the last few minutes of company.

Vedant glanced at the place where the glass stood.

"Tumne khulne se pehle kaha. Us baat ko badalna mat jab jaldi lage."

Rudra could hear the difference. He had not asked how to pull a splinter back from the air. He had asked about the moment before a hand became the beginning of something other people would have to survive.

He wanted that moment to be longer.

---

"Agar us waqt samajh hi na aaye?" he asked.

"Toh hum pehle samajhne waala hissa dekhenge. Jahaan kuchh chala hi nahi rahe."

"Bas baat?"

"Baat bhi. Dekhna bhi. Aur jab mujhe nahi pata hoga, main bolunga."

He did not make a large promise out of it. He had heard Rudra's account; he had not acquired a map of everything happening under the boy's skin. They could look for the point at which intention narrowed, for the people and ground which disappeared from his attention when one weapon filled it. That was work they could eventually begin without pretending a new instruction would make an injured chest safe.

Rudra wanted to ask how long.

The question reached his mouth, then changed.

"Kab poochhna hai, yeh bhi doctor se?"

"Jis kaam ke liye badan chaahiye, uske liye jo abhi kar sakta hai woh poochhenge. Mere kehne se haddi tayyaar nahi hoti."

The old impatience stirred. He could already imagine a permission arriving in pieces while the thing he wanted remained whole and far away. He let the feeling stay a feeling instead of turning it into an argument about whether anybody understood the danger.

Vedant understood enough to leave the next question alone.

---

"Veer ko phir hatna pada toh—"

"Uska hatna hamaari taiyaari nahi hogi."

Rudra looked up.

"Agar kabhi practice hui, toh uske peechhe koi dost khadaa nahi hoga. Koi bhi nahi. Jo tum rok na paao, uske liye pehle se khaali jagah chaahiye."

He pictured the staff, then tried to picture what lay past it without allowing memory to put Veer conveniently somewhere else. There had been rock. An aid. His friend's foot. The attendant's working arm close to the chair. He had known each thing separately and had not held them together while the end of the staff came down.

"Mujhe laga tha bas saamne waala hataana hai."

"Isliye saamne ke baad bhi dekhenge."

The room stayed quiet for several breaths. Neither counted them into an answer.

---

The nurse came to the opening and asked whether the conversation could end now.

"Haan," Rudra said before Vedant had to say it for him.

There was something disappointing about how little it resembled a beginning. No raised hand. No heat. No word which made a form arrive. He had asked to learn, and the first thing he had chosen was to let the visitor go.

Vedant stood without leaning on the hurt forearm.

"Kal ka waqt abhi pakka nahi kar raha," he said. "Care ke baad dekh lenge."

Rudra nodded.

Ira would not be asked for a record of the conversation tonight. Aadhya's good night remained a good night. There was no message to his parents saying that a solution had been found and they could stop being frightened.

Vedant left. The nurse checked the support and the things the next hours required, then put the call bell where the good hand could reach it.

Rudra pressed it later when the ankle needed help.

He had waited until the ache began changing, not until it became severe enough to make asking respectable. The nurse came, listened and changed the position again. He thanked her without apologising for interrupting a night in which answering this call was part of the work.

Afterwards he slept.

The hand stayed empty. In the morning, there would still be work to do.

Day ninety-eight began with the observations already owed to the body.

The doctor compared the pressure readings, asked again about the faint and the breathlessness and listened to what had changed during the night. The first reassuring results had not supplied a complete explanation. Further review concerned the event Rudra had actually had, not a guess about what colour of force might have caused it.

He described the ache at the ankle and the smaller discomfort near the knee. He did not exchange one for the other because two complaints sounded less recoverable than one.

The doctor examined both.

There was no new power trial on the ninety-eighth day, or the ninety-ninth. There were meals, sleep, ordinary investigations and the tedious accuracy of answering the same question when the answer had only changed a little.

By the hundredth morning, there had been no further faint. The observations had stayed settled through the ordinary care and supported movements they had allowed. The senior review agreed that he no longer needed the monitored bay, with further follow-up and clear instructions about what changes required urgent help.

"Iska matlab wajah mil gayi?" Rudra asked.

"Nahi. Abhi ki zaroorat badli hai. Wajah ka poora jawaab nahi mila."

He repeated the warning symptoms back instead of nodding through them.

---

The move to the ordinary room happened after the receiving staff were ready.

They checked the bed, transferred the supports and helped him across without letting the protected wrist take his weight. His phone and charger came with his clothes. The nurse showed him the bell before anyone left him to discover whether it worked.

There was a window with an ordinary patch of sky above the next building.

Rudra stared at it for a long time.

He had not realised how much effort he spent listening to the monitored bay, sorting each footfall and equipment sound into something which concerned him or someone else. Here, a pigeon stood on a sill and pecked at a flake of old paint with complete indifference to whether he was about to become well.

He liked it at once.

The next care still came. The window had not discharged him.

---

On the hundred-and-third day, the therapist asked him to describe the proposed movement before trying it.

The chair. The permitted help. The good hand's support. The ankle's limited load and the place where he would sit again. The other wrist was not available to catch a mistake.

He gave the order. She moved the chair a little and asked him to look again.

He was annoyed for a second, then saw the longer turn she had made impossible by bringing the seat nearer. He had been preparing to prove he could manage the old arrangement instead of noticing that a better one had been offered.

The first rise was slow.

The therapist's assistance stayed with him. He reported the knee's pull, waited while the position changed and sat when she said the trial was enough. The ankle trembled afterwards. He wanted to get up once more so the last thing would not be a tremor.

He told her that too.

"Last cheez report bhi ho sakti hai," she said.

So it was.

---

The next afternoon went badly in a smaller way.

The ankle felt warmer after the permitted work. He said it before the second interval. The therapist checked the swelling and the way he had loaded the foot, then reduced the work rather than asking him to repeat the same effort more bravely. The treating team reviewed the change. Rest and the adjusted support belonged to the plan for the following day.

Rudra spent that day furious with his own sock.

It had turned slightly on the foot. He could feel the thick edge of its seam, but reaching to correct it would put the leg and wrist into the wrong work. He called for help and had to explain a problem which, from a distance, looked as though it ought to belong entirely to him.

When Veer called later, Rudra told him about the sock rather than giving a heroic summary of rehabilitation.

"Usne bhi raasta roka?" Veer asked.

"Silai tedhi thi."

"Case alag likhwaana."

Rudra laughed, then let the joke finish while it was still comfortable.

He did not have to turn a bad afternoon into a good lesson before it could end.

---

By day one hundred and eight, the changed work was helping.

With the therapist, the ankle support and the permitted aid, he went a short way along the corridor and came back. There was a seat where the return needed it. He used it instead of passing it to make the distance look uninterrupted.

His breathing remained settled. The knee tired. The good hand did not have to become a fist around the aid. Afterwards they examined the joints and took the account before considering another interval on another day.

Rudra looked back down the corridor from the room.

He had returned along the same floor he had just crossed. Nothing in the scene looked like a battle. Sweat cooled under his shirt, and the small success was large enough that he no longer needed it to look like one.

The weeks were not the same week repeated.

Veer's shoulder began accepting more ordinary movement under the therapist's care, then objected when he tried to make that improvement carry the next task too. He reported it. The exercise changed. His ribs hurt less during a quiet laugh; the knee still decided how far a corridor went.

Aadhya could manage a little more with supported hands. The altered feeling in the right thumb and index remained unreliable. She wanted to turn a page herself, not have a leaf placed in her palm as a suggestion that recovery ought to look green. Sitara helped arrange the book where the permitted movement would work.

Ira's ordinary right eye had its own review. The surface irritation from the grit had settled; comfortable use could increase in short intervals, with rest when it changed. The left stayed covered. A better right-eye morning did not become depth she could safely borrow from the other side.

She chose to hear part of a story rather than read all of it.

Nobody counted which friend was recovering fastest.

---

On day one hundred and twelve, Rudra asked Vedant for the part which used no force.

They sat where he had been allowed a short interval outside the room, with the legs supported and a chair for each. Vedant put an empty cardboard box on a clear patch of ground. A second box stood farther back, against the plain wall.

"Pehla dekho," he said. "Phir uske baad waali jagah bataao."

Rudra described the second box.

"Uske baad."

The wall.

"Uske baad?"

He could not see it.

For a moment he wanted to make that a fault in the question. He had given the things in view. Then he remembered how easily an unknown place became an empty place when he was in a hurry.

"Pata nahi," he said.

"Woh bhi jawaab hai. Abhi haath se kuchh nahi karna. Bas isko dekhna."

They looked from another permitted position later, after rest. Beyond the wall was a narrow working strip, empty then, which a shut gate had hidden from the first chair. There was nothing mysterious about it. It had been there while he named the box.

He was glad they had found it with their feet and eyes instead of a piece of broken wall.

---

Vedant kept his own forearm review.

The light uses allowed to it increased gradually. He could hold an ordinary bottle more comfortably; heavy gripping still needed limits. He told Rudra rather than arranging the class so the student never saw a teacher stop.

Dhruv's closed cut healed through the scheduled dressing and skin reviews. His shoulder movement improved. The hand had slower work to do. He used the sound one for the ordinary tasks it could manage and kept the other out of the old habit of pretending to grip.

Sitara took time away from the rooms. Her tired palms recovered without becoming the reason she was assigned every future handle. When she came back, she had eaten somewhere else and had a complaint about tea which was entirely her own.

The young people were not responsible for keeping all three mentors usefully occupied.

---

Rudra's own hundred-and-sixteenth day ended early.

The short uneven-ground trial had been planned with the therapist, after the flatter work. Halfway back, the knee began pulling differently. He stopped before the next cue.

The therapist checked him where he stood, helped him to the nearer seat and heard exactly where the movement changed. The review reduced the next day's load. He could still do the ordinary things which had been comfortable; this particular progression had been too much today.

There was no red-black to blame for it.

When Vedant came, Rudra gave him the result before asking about practice.

"Aaj nahi," he said.

"Theek."

He waited for the second part: disappointment, a smaller task which would secretly test the same thing, reassurance so large it would require him to act grateful.

Vedant asked whether he wanted company for a few minutes or quiet.

Rudra chose quiet.

His mentor left it with him.

---

The attendant's work review came during those days too.

Through the agreed work contact, he gave the current lifting restriction and the missed work they had recorded, not a promise to stay available for the next trip. The verified missed-work payment was made from the remaining money set aside for the outstanding costs; he confirmed receiving it. Longer-term costs remained on the separate list. He had begun arranging work he could actually do.

Rudra heard the update with relief which did not need the man back in the doorway.

---

By the hundred-and-twenty-first day, the ordinary functions could be considered again.

The wrist review allowed light use within its limits, still no catching the body on it. The ankle and knee had made the shorter out-and-back work comfortably on separate days. Breathing and pressure had remained settled through the graded exertion they had actually tried.

The treating people examined him and discussed the very small effort proposed next, including the earlier costs and the symptoms which would stop it. They could assess those functions. They could not promise what an unfamiliar force would do to the chest which had frightened everyone at the approach.

Rudra heard that part without trying to bargain it away.

Any attempt would need a place in which a mistaken answer could run into empty ground.

The place was on the Mirzapur side of the ordinary road, a short drive from the centre.

There had been earth stored there for construction. The work was elsewhere now. Sitara obtained the occupier's permission for a closed morning, explained what might strike the ground and did not accept a permission which depended on pretending nothing could go wrong. A high, thick bank of compacted earth closed the far side. Behind it lay more unused ground inside the same boundary, not a house they had neglected to ask about.

She and Vedant inspected it on foot before Rudra was offered a journey.

They looked over the bank, walked both flanks and checked the ground beyond. No worker remained in the strip. No open path joined it from the other side. The return to the vehicle did not require climbing over the target area. There was a firm, level place from which Rudra could stand briefly, and a seat outside the line of work.

The bank was not a promise that any imaginable force would stop there. Its depth and the empty ground beyond it were reasons to consider one very limited attempt, not reasons to make it larger.

---

On the hundred-and-twenty-second morning, they asked again.

The treating team had examined Rudra after the previous day's ordinary work. Today there was no new warmth or swelling, no dizziness, chest pain or changed breathing. They discussed the seated journey, the short level stance and the earlier faint. The ordinary activity was within the present plan; a force trial still carried an uncertainty no examination could remove.

He could decline it without losing the walking work he had earned.

Rudra asked to hear the proposed stop arrangements before answering.

There would be no exercise to exhaustion. Any return of the earlier symptoms, any change at a joint, any unexpected force or failure of the site to remain empty ended the attempt. Sitara or Vedant could stop it, and so could he. No retry would be owed merely because a morning and a vehicle had been paid for.

He said yes to that small proposal, not to whatever he might feel capable of once he arrived.

---

The car was newly booked through the work contact.

It carried four people including its driver: Rudra, Vedant and Sitara occupied the other belted seats. The agreed round trip and waiting time fitted the checked money remaining after the recent payment. It was not one of the old drivers returning to a duty nobody had ended aloud.

The new driver heard that there might be a dangerous impact in a closed yard. He would stay outside its marked working space; he was not booked to hold a target or lift an injured passenger. He accepted the limited run and the right to end his own part if the arrangement changed.

They took an ordinary first-aid bag and Rudra's current care instructions. Sitara checked the receiving contact by phone, described the location and agreed how to call if the old symptoms returned. The phone had signal there. The nearby care was a destination on an actual open road, not a healing power waiting in somebody's palm.

Dhruv kept his own appointment instead of supplying a fourth observer.

The others knew where Rudra had chosen to go. None had been asked to witness something which would make their own recovery day harder to refuse. Ira wished him a dull morning. He replied that he would try not to improve the description.

---

He got out after they parked on the firm approach.

Vedant asked whether help would be useful. Rudra used the door support as planned, kept the recovering wrist out of the movement and took his time before the short walk. The ankle remained comfortable. He sat while Sitara made the last circuit.

It was strange to see a place arranged for a mistake rather than for an audience.

The driver waited beyond the closed entrance, on the vehicle side of the earth flank. Vedant and Sitara would stand behind Rudra and well off the forward line, with the bank continuing around their side. Nobody waited beside the mark he was meant to strike. The way to the seat, phone and car remained clear without crossing in front of him.

Sitara came back by that way.

"Abhi bhi khaali," she said. "Peechhe bhi."

Vedant asked Rudra to describe what he could see.

He named the level strip, the low lump of dry clay set in loose sand, the sand's far edge, the bank beyond it. Then he named what the inspection had established rather than letting his eyes pretend they reached through earth: unused ground, closed boundary, nobody on the other side at the last check.

"Aur ab koi aaye toh?"

"Khatam."

"Haan."

---

The first raising of his good hand produced nothing.

Vedant had not told him to call anything. Rudra was only looking along the line while the hand found a position which did not pull at the wrist or turn the recovering knee. He lowered it when asked. They changed the stance slightly, then rested.

The second time, he found that he was watching the clay and forgetting the sand behind it.

He lowered the hand himself.

"Peechhe se nazar hat gayi."

They did not reward the report with an immediate chance to do the dangerous part. He sat, drank a little plain water and looked at the whole strip again from the side.

The clay was unimportant. They could leave it there all morning.

For the first time, Rudra believed that leaving it untouched would not make the journey a wasted one.

When he stood again, a little dust had settled on the clay.

It looked less like a target than something somebody had dropped while doing another job. Rudra preferred it that way. There was no painted enemy on it, no face he could use to make striking seem easier than deciding not to.

He checked the knee before finding the hand's position.

Vedant remained where the bank's side gave him cover without putting him anywhere beyond the clay. Sitara could see the entrance and the working strip. Her ordinary stop signal was an open raised hand as well as her voice, so one sound could not be mistaken for the only available warning.

They checked that Rudra could see it before beginning.

"Kitna aayega, mujhe nahi pata," he said.

"Isliye chhota chaahne ko chhota aane ka saboot nahi maan rahe."

He looked at the earth which stood behind the thing he wanted to touch.

No one offered to make up the difference with their body.

---

The first effort was an effort of wanting the old sensation back.

His good palm grew warm. He could feel the grain of air against the fingers, or thought he could. Nothing crossed the empty ground. He waited long enough to notice that he had begun squeezing the other hand inside its allowed position.

He stopped.

The recovering wrist had not taken a load, but wanting it to join the effort was information too. He said what had happened. Vedant had him sit while they checked that there was no pain or altered feeling. There was not. They changed the position so the hand could rest openly where both Rudra and Sitara could see it.

He was embarrassed by how nearly the small failure had become a secret.

"Aapko dikha?" he asked.

"Ungliyaan badli thi. Tumne kya mehsoos kiya, woh tum bataaoge."

Not omniscience. An observer, watching what he could actually see.

Rudra let his shoulders settle.

He could have passed the whole interval as careful concentration. Everybody wanted the morning to be useful. The wanting was not wicked; it was simply another thing which could put pressure on a hand.

---

"Aaj yahin chhodna hai?" Vedant asked after the rest.

Rudra considered the question without looking first at the clay.

The wrist was comfortable in the changed support. The ankle and knee felt as they had before standing. His breath was ordinary. There had been no force, no sudden symptom and no change to the empty site. He wanted one further attempt at the limited thing they had discussed, not another chance to hide what happened during it.

He gave that answer.

Sitara checked the approach and the ground behind the bank once more. They waited for her return; nothing began while an observer was on the other side. When she was back behind the working position, Vedant asked Rudra to name the way out.

He pointed it out with his eyes.

He would not have to cross the strip to sit down. Neither observer would have to cross it to reach him. The car remained beyond the closed entrance, not in the flight of whatever might leave the palm.

He stood for the last short interval of the morning.

---

There was gold somewhere under the sternum, familiar and not obedient.

It did not cover him. The old whole concealing layer had not grown back during the weeks in which his ankle learned a corridor. No part of the warmth could be used to tell him that his chest would tolerate what he meant to try.

The red-black gathered closer to the good hand.

He could see the difference between the two even while both belonged to him. He did not try to press them into one colour. He did not ask a remembered count to decide whether anybody was safe.

The clay lay low in the sand.

Rudra opened the hand towards it, with the far earth still in view.

A narrow red-black fold left the palm.

---

It struck lower than he had expected.

Sand jumped before the clay moved. The lump broke at the bottom and rolled instead of bursting apart. Rudra saw the dark streak come through the space it had occupied, still travelling towards the farther mark.

"Bas," he said, and closed the hand.

The gathering at the fingers went out. He felt the effort cease there, as abruptly as a held weight being set down.

The streak beyond the clay did not go out with it.

For the smallest instant he waited for the empty palm to become an answer. He had stopped doing the thing. He had meant it. The body could report an ending before the ground agreed.

Nothing was standing between the moving force and the bank. No friend had to step into that interval and make his intention true. The way back to the seat lay clear behind him, and the mark he had chosen was only a mark in sand, not a person who had trusted him.

Even so, he had chosen it.

He had wanted to stop there.

The red-black crossed the line.
