# CHAPTER NINETY-EIGHT — BEFORE ANOTHER ATTEMPT

---

> *"We asked about the water he learned to hold.*
> *He told us what he put down before trying again."*
> *— Ira's later register, after Vedant's account*

---

"Sabse pehle main," Vedant said.

Rudra kept looking at him.

The mentor let the answer remain where it was before adding the part which prevented it from becoming an easy promise.

"Aur tum roko, toh sunna hoga. Par meri har galti pakadna tumhaara homework nahi hai."

Rudra smiled a little, then asked what happened if he could not find the words quickly enough. He did not mean only the cup in the story. Some instructions arrived inside a voice one had spent years learning to obey; understanding an objection and saying it were not always the same movement.

Vedant did not ask him to identify every voice he meant.

"Tumhara chup rehna meri jaanch khatam nahi karta," he said. "Mujhe dekhna hai ki kya maang raha hoon. Aur jiska kaam ilaaj hai, uski baat main apni class ke neeche nahi rakh sakta."

There would still be mistakes. He did not promise a room in which every person always knew enough to prevent them. He was saying that a teacher could not hand the work of remaining responsible to the person least free to interrupt him.

Rudra heard the difference.

"Aap bura maan gaye toh?"

"Maan bhi gaya toh pehle kaam rok sakta hoon. Bura lagna koi hukm nahi hai."

The answer was less comforting than a claim that Vedant would never feel offended. It was also harder to mistake for a claim of perfection. He could imagine a person having the feeling and still being required to choose what happened next.

He could imagine himself having to do the same.

"Sirf yeh boloon ki samajh nahi aa raha? Behtar tareeka na pata ho?"

"Toh pehle wahi sunenge. Sawaal poochhne ke liye mujhse zyaada jaanna zaroori nahi hai."

Rudra let the words settle. The student did not need to arrive with a complete replacement for the lesson before he could say something inside it was wrong for him. Not knowing yet could be the beginning of asking, rather than the reason he must remain quiet.

---

Vedant asked if the support remained comfortable and whether the call for the next care was within reach. Then he left when it was time to leave.

The boy did not test the promise by keeping him there.

He lay with the two finished accounts put aside, one about the help needed for the outing, one about the view and the people whose lives had existed before it broke. The questions which came after them did not have to reopen the first visit as unfinished business.

There were new things he wanted to ask the teachers. There were old things about his father which did not become simple because one teacher could tell a useful story about him. They could stay different things.

During the night the ankle began pressing against a fold which had been comfortable earlier. Rudra called before deciding how long he could tolerate it. A member of staff helped change the position, checked what needed checking and left him settled again.

He slept afterwards.

The answer to the evening's question did not heal the joint. It made asking for the ordinary adjustment a little less crowded by the idea that he ought to have managed alone.

---

On day eighty-four, the separate care came before any gathering.

Rudra's wrist and leg protections remained. Current examination and function allowed the supported movement already being used, not a longer walk awarded for a conversation well heard. Veer had his own shoulder, ribs and knee reviewed and his own limit on the move to another room. Aadhya's hands still needed their treatment and protection; Ira's left eye remained covered and the ordinary eye's use stayed a matter of comfort and the treating plan, not how interesting the next story might be.

The proposed listening could be short and could end early. There would be no task to perform while listening, no water moved by a student and no demonstration to make pain worth mentioning.

Each was asked again whether the arrangement still worked.

Ira wanted the shaded place and no expectation that she look at the teller throughout. Aadhya wanted the hands supported rather than a notebook placed in them. Veer wanted the aid and a place to sit before anybody began. Rudra needed help with the chair and the transfer.

The arrangements were made as arrangements, not interpreted as four apologies for coming.

---

Before the appointed interval, Vedant checked that the room was free for the agreed time and that nobody else's care had been moved aside to provide him an audience.

He had his own forearm comfortably supported. He did not bring a basin. The drinking water already in the room remained drinking water, available to anyone who wanted it without having to become material for a lesson first.

Sitara helped with the settling and would remain within call rather than occupy every place needed by the people listening. Hospital staff did the assistance which required them. Veer was not recruited to lift the chair. The attendant from the passage had not been summoned back merely because the four were together again.

Rudra looked at the ordinary room when he arrived: chairs, a table with a faint ring where a wet glass had been, light cut into a softer shape by the curtain. There was nothing in it which required a power to begin.

For once, knowing that did not make him feel they had come to the lesser part of the training.

Veer was sitting before the story began.

It ought not to have felt like a contribution. The seat was there because the knee needed it; the aid was close because getting up later would require it. Still, he caught himself watching the other arrivals for some work he could take from them before he noticed that the necessary work had already been given to people able to do it.

He kept the place he had been helped to reach.

Rudra told them the question he had chosen to ask the evening before. Not only when Vedant learned to stop, but who would stop the mentor when the mentor was wrong.

"Meri taraf se jawaab abhi bhi wahi hai," Vedant said. "Apni zimmedaari tum par nahi daal sakta."

Then he told the story from the beginning, so nobody had to borrow a friend's partial memory to follow it.

---

There had been no flood.

Veer found himself almost disappointed by that, then amused by the disappointment. Vedant was describing a basin after a meal. Ram wanted water to drink. The younger man wanted his already-practised skill to be noticed.

The curve was elegant. The cup moved because a person reached for it. The water continued towards a position which had become empty.

The simplicity made the error more difficult to put away as something which belonged only to an inexperienced fool. Veer knew the urge to solve a problem before the person inside it had finished moving. He knew how certain a body could feel about a response which another body had not asked for.

Vedant spoke about the attempted correction, the growing wet place, the stop he did not want and the ache he had hoped another attempt would let him forget to mention.

He did not add danger to make the memory worth their time. The wet ground was enough. So was the person who had asked for a drink and still needed one while the display was being repaired.

"Unhone jaan-boojhke gilaas hataaya tha?" Aadhya asked.

"Peene ke liye uthaaya tha. Usko meri pareeksha banaane ka mann mera tha."

She nodded. The distinction seemed to matter to her.

Ira asked whether the ground had been wet before he began.

Part had. The washing had made it so. His spill had carried water to a place previously dry, and he had first looked only at the obvious splash, not at the thin run near his own foot. The cloth had made him pay attention to what had actually changed.

Veer looked at the faint ring on the table. No lesson was concealed in it for him to discover. Someone had put a damp glass there. He could let that be the whole fact instead of turning every ordinary trace into an examiner's trick.

---

Vedant told them that Ram had filled the tumbler by hand.

Veer laughed softly. It had not occurred to him either, though the ordinary answer had been available from the beginning of the story.

"Pehle hi kar dete," he said.

"Main? Kar sakta tha. Par phir kaun dekhta ki paani chalaa sakta hoon."

There was no self-disgust in the older man's voice. He had not made the younger one ridiculous in order to prove how far above him he now stood. He described a desire which still sounded human, including in a room where four people had reasons to want their own bodies to become impressive again.

Veer shifted to ease the shoulder and did not make the movement discreet enough to become painful.

He had wanted to hear where the mentor's assurance came from. He was hearing about a time before it could protect the mentor from the embarrassment of wanting praise.

That was not the same as hearing that assurance was false.

---

When the first part was finished, Ira asked if the second attempt had succeeded.

Vedant said he had asked himself the same question before the next day began. He had imagined the better arc of water. He had made the receiving hand still in his imagination, the cup exactly where it should be, the teacher looking at the right moment.

"Is baar sabko aapki tasveer mein rehna tha," she said.

"Haan. Sabse pehle apne aapko. Haath kaisa hai, woh bhi tay kar liya tha."

Rudra was quiet beside the window. Veer did not know every part of what his friend was thinking, and the account did not demand that he guess. He had enough of his own response to attend to.

"Phir gaye?" he asked.

"Gaya."

"Paani uthaaya?"

"Usse pehle kehna pada ki haath abhi bhi dukh raha hai."

The sentence altered the imagined next day. Veer had been waiting for a performance repaired. The person who went back had taken the same arm with him.

He wanted to hear what became possible once that fact was allowed to arrive before the water.

The younger Vedant had gone back with the improvement already arranged in his head.

He knew where he would stand. He knew the point at which the curve would narrow, the shorter path it would take, the quicker end which would prevent the cup from moving beyond it. The imagined attempt had no ache in it. He had placed that fact outside the picture as carefully as he had placed the tumbler inside.

Then Ram asked how the arm was.

For a moment he considered saying it was better. It was, a little. Better could be made to sound like enough if he spoke quickly and began before the next question.

He remembered the wet ground near his own foot.

"Abhi dukh raha hai," he said.

Ram put aside what he had been doing and asked what he had noticed overnight. Vedant told him. There was no new trial with the water that day. The arm could be rested and attended to; an exercise was not the proper means of finding out how much hurt could be concealed inside a successful result.

Vedant had returned ready to repair a demonstration. Instead he found himself sitting with the fact which the demonstration had been meant to cover.

---

"Aap naraaz hue?" Aadhya asked.

"Apne aap se. Unse bhi. Us waqt farq nahi kar raha tha."

"Bola?"

"Pehle nahi. Phir keh diya ki agar har baar rukenge toh seekhenge kab."

Veer felt the question find a familiar place. He had asked versions of it without always using words. If the body could not be trusted today, when did trusting it begin? If another person could do the work more safely, when did a person become useful enough not to be asked to sit?

Vedant said Ram had asked what he thought there was to learn from the previous day.

"Sahi jagah paani giraana," the younger man had answered.

It had been the obvious thing. He had missed. The mistake needed better aim. He wanted to be allowed to work on the skill rather than be congratulated for talking about the ache which prevented work.

Ram asked whether the person receiving the water had become less thirsty because the curve was beautiful.

The question annoyed him. Of course not. He knew that. He was not a child who needed the use of a cup explained.

What he had not wanted to know was how completely the receiver had become scenery inside an attempt made for the receiver.

He had put the person's hand where it needed to be for him to succeed. He had made the ground dry by forgetting the spill. He had made the arm ready by giving the imagined arm no answer of its own.

There was no better curve which could correct all that before he looked at it.

---

"Toh us din baat hi hui?" Ira asked.

"Hui. Aur paani haath se diya gaya. Jise peena tha usne piya."

"Aapne kya kiya?"

"Pehle poochha ki baith sakta hoon ya nahi. Khada rehke zyaada yogya dikhne ki koshish kar raha tha."

This time the four laughed together.

Vedant let them. The joke belonged to the younger man's stubbornness, not to an arm which had hurt. He had been allowed to sit. The disappointment did not vanish as soon as the correct words were spoken. He had stayed disappointed and listened anyway.

There had been no grand declaration that praise no longer mattered to him. He still wanted to make the water do what he intended. He still wanted the person teaching him to see and be pleased. Those desires could remain without being permitted to decide whether the arm was ready.

"Agli baar kab hua?" Veer asked.

Vedant said the next practice came after rest and after the arm was fit for the effort again. He did not supply a miraculous interval or turn the return to practice into a new name for a power.

Before beginning, he had asked whether the other person was ready to receive. That had felt embarrassingly small beside the skill he had hoped to display.

It had been useful.

---

Veer looked at his own aid.

He had heard a story with no dead friend inside it, no last command from a battlefield, no hidden inheritance which made the teacher's present ability inevitable. The person telling it had learned something before disaster made learning dramatic.

That did not make the learning less available to those who had already been hurt.

"Aapko ab bhi prabhaavit karna hota hai?" he asked.

"Hota hai," Vedant said. "Kabhi tum logon ko bhi."

Veer had not expected the last part. The room grew quieter around it.

"Isliye pehle waala sawaal zaroori tha," the mentor added. "Main achha dikhna chaahoon, uska kharcha tumhare haath-pair kyun dein?"

Rudra was looking at him now. Aadhya's hands remained on their supports. Ira listened without reaching for a notebook or opening the eye to reward the confession with attention she had not offered.

The story had brought them to a man who could still want the wrong kind of success.

It had not left them alone with the responsibility for giving it to him.

Veer asked for water.

It was not a way of extending the story. His mouth was dry. Vedant reached for the glass with the unhurt hand and began to put it where Veer could see it clearly on the table.

Then he stopped.

Veer had started to lean. The glass was visible, but the comfortable reach was on the other side, closer to the hand he could use without drawing the shoulder into the movement. Vedant had noticed the body preparing to accommodate his placement.

He moved the glass nearer the right place before Veer had to finish the reach. Then he asked whether that position worked or whether help was needed.

Veer gave the answer. The water reached him without becoming a task in which he had to prove the injured shoulder was less troublesome than expected.

"Yeh bhi plan tha?" Ira asked.

"Nahi," Vedant said. "Jahaan mujhe seedha laga, wahaan rakh raha tha. Uske haath se nahi dekha."

He did not make the small correction into a second story about his wisdom. Nor did he call it too small to admit because the first account had just made him sound careful. He had put a glass down badly and changed it when he saw.

Veer drank.

The useful part was not that somebody had nearly made a mistake for him to catch. It was that he had not been required to pay for the mistake with a painful reach while the older person finished looking right.

---

Aadhya asked whether, when they were ready to practise again, asking for a pause would end the lesson.

"Kabhi us din ke liye khatam ho sakti hai," Vedant said. "Jitna sach hai, utna hi."

He would not promise that the body always got to stop for one minute and return unchanged to everything it wanted. Sometimes stopping meant treatment, reassessment, another day. That was not a punishment the teacher could remove by agreeing to call it a break instead.

"Par phir mauka milega ya nahi, woh dard chhupaane ka inaam nahi hoga," he added.

Aadhya looked at her protected hands. She did not offer them for examination by the story. Their treating people already had that work, and the next permitted effort would need their actual answer.

Veer understood why she had asked. He had heard the question; he did not have to decide what every past silence of hers had meant.

Ira asked if they could keep their own account of what they had heard, without trying to take down every word before the interval ended.

Vedant agreed. He would rather answer a later question about what he meant than let an inaccurate sentence remain because somebody had written it neatly. Their records could name him as the teller of his memory, not turn a remembered cup into independent proof of every part of his life.

The story gave them something to ask about. It did not give them access to the things he had not told.

---

When the staff member came for the care which was due, Vedant stopped speaking.

He did not say there was only one more point. Veer had already been collecting a question about the next practice in his head; he let it go back to being a question, not a reason for Aadhya to wait beyond the time arranged for her hands.

The others had their own limits. Ira wanted the eye's next quiet interval. Rudra needed the position changed before returning to his room. Veer's knee had begun asking for the movement which the chair allowed but prolonged sitting did not.

He reported it before standing.

There was help where needed. The staff member checked the transfer. The aid went to the place chosen for it rather than being left on the table because conversation had made the table seem more important than the way out.

Veer rose within the effort permitted that day. He did not offer the shoulder to Rudra on his way past. Rudra had assistance already; the friendship did not need an extra injured person inserted between the helper and the chair to make it visible.

"Baad mein," Veer told him.

"Haan."

It was an ordinary goodbye between people who expected to remain part of one another's day without occupying every interval of it.

---

After his own care, Veer lay with the aid in reach and thought about the receiving hand.

He had spent a long time imagining protection from the side of the person giving it. The body which rose, held, caught, stayed. He still wanted to be that person when he could. The story had not asked him to stop wanting.

But the receiver could move. The receiver could need the cup elsewhere, the help later, the voice quieter. Being helped was not the empty place into which another person's idea of usefulness could be poured.

He knew that now from the side of a shoulder which had not been asked to hurt for the sake of accepting a drink.

When the nurse asked whether the support needed changing, he said yes and showed her where.

It was not the opposite of learning to protect someone.

The afternoon went on without becoming a continuation of the class.

Veer had food brought when he was ready for it, asked for the thing which was awkward to be moved closer and ate before it went cold. There were parts of the day which had begun to feel available only after he had made them into proof of improvement. He tried letting the meal be a meal.

Later he sent Rudra a short message asking how he was.

The answer came after a while: settled, tired, no new revelation from the ceiling. Veer replied that he would report if his own ceiling became more forthcoming. Neither boy treated the delay as evidence that a friend had gone somewhere dangerous without him.

Ira was resting. Aadhya had finished the treatment which had ended their gathering and wanted her own quiet. The four had heard one story. They did not have to keep discussing it until every private response was agreed.

There would be time for questions when attention did not have to be borrowed from care.

---

Near evening, Dhruv came to ask whether Veer wanted company for a little while.

He had two bananas in a small bag. He offered one, did not put it into a hand already occupied with getting comfortable and accepted that Veer wanted it later. The bag went where it could be reached with help if needed. Nothing had to be peeled immediately to prove hospitality had been received.

"Kya dekh rahe the?" Dhruv asked.

Veer had been looking at the aid.

"Yahi. Abhi tak taare nahi uge isme."

Dhruv sat down with the tender hand in an easy position.

"Achha hai. Raat ko dhoondhna mushkil ho jaata."

Veer laughed. The humour took no strength away from the man. That still surprised him sometimes, how little an older person had to lose by not requiring every sentence in the room to protect his dignity.

They sat through the sound of a trolley passing outside. A staff member checked something at the doorway, found that no help was being requested just then and went on. The room did not close around teacher and student as though the rest of the day had been dismissed.

---

Veer said he had been thinking about being helped.

Not because he had discovered he liked needing it. There were still moments when another person's approach made the body prepare to refuse before it knew what was being offered. He disliked how much care had to be organized around a shoulder, a knee, the space in which he could sit without either becoming an argument.

Dhruv did not tell him the feeling was ungrateful.

"Aur?" he asked.

"Kabhi lagta hai jiske liye kuchh karna hai, usse poochhna bhool jaata hoon."

He could say that much about himself without giving away another person's memory in order to explain it. The story he had heard was available for thought, not a borrowed authority which had to accompany every sentence he made.

Dhruv looked towards the aid and back.

"Aaj kisi ko poochha?"

Veer thought of the nurse adjusting the support, of telling her where, of not offering the damaged shoulder to a boy whose transfer already had help.

"Apne liye bola."

"Woh bhi shuruat hai."

It did not sound like congratulations for becoming harmless. Veer still wanted the part of himself which could rise when someone needed protecting. He did not want recovery to mean learning a polished language in which every dangerous choice was refused before the people inside it could ask for help.

But protecting a person and occupying the person's choices were different things. He had been allowed to notice the difference from the chair, while there was time to think before action narrowed the room.

He would not always have that time.

That made using it now more important, not less.

---

Dhruv asked if he should leave before Veer became tired of company.

Veer checked the actual answer. The knee was settled. The shoulder needed no new position yet. He wanted a few more minutes, not a long lesson.

He said so.

Dhruv remained without taking the invitation as permission to begin training. The surveyor's ring lay still on its cord. There was no star-light answering it in the room, no new reading laid over the injured body.

Veer remembered arriving at the akhada beyond Machli-ghat, expecting to be measured by whether he could win. He remembered the labour of getting up, the attention given to returning, the strange insult of discovering that his most certain idea of usefulness had not been the whole lesson.

Those were things he had actually lived. He could remember them without knowing all the life which had made the man beside him choose to teach that way.

He had once wanted the rest of the answer to concern a stronger stance.

Now he wondered about the person who had decided that a student needed a way back before he needed another victory.

The question did not ask the tender hand to demonstrate anything. It asked its owner for a beginning he could choose to give.

"Aapne humein jeetne se pehle lautna kyun sikhaaya tha?"
