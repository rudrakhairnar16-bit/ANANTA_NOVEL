# CHAPTER EIGHTY-FOUR — THE HANDS ABOVE THE KULS

---

> *"We had mistaken height for innocence.*
> *The order had come from above us."*
>
> *— Ira's later account of the first family disclosures*

---

"Lokas ki satta sambhaalne waalon ne," Ram answered. "Jo insaani Kulon ke upar the. Unka faisla tha. Unhone hi Asuran Kul ko mitaaya."

Rudra kept looking at the phone.

He had been making room for an army from another bloodline, a larger version of the people who could stop a road because they believed their ancestors had owned the land beside it. His father was describing the authority above those claims. Not another family asking for its share. The hands with the power to decide which families would be allowed to remain.

"Kisi doosre Kul ne nahi?"

"Nahi. Yeh unse upar ki satta ka kiya tha."

The answer did not supply one convenient face. It supplied responsibility, placed somewhere Rudra had not understood responsibility could reach.

At the far end, Ram waited through the next breath before continuing.

"Asuranon ki badhti taaqat se khatra tha. Unhone use rokne ke liye logon ko khatam karna chuna."

Chuna.

A choice, then. Not an illness which had moved through Pataal. Not the earth giving way beneath a house. Somebody who could choose had looked at the people inside and decided that their future was the thing to destroy.

---

"Toh unke liye sahi tha?" Rudra asked.

Ram heard the older argument behind it. Good and bad, right and wrong: words he had given a child with so much certainty that the child had sometimes been left wondering whether pain was proof that the lesson was being done correctly.

"Unhone kiya," he said. "Isse sahi nahi ho gaya."

The sentence was short. He did not dress the dead in a philosophy which could make killing them sound more difficult to condemn.

Rudra's good fingers had begun pressing into the blanket. He noticed the effort and let them ease before it reached the wrist inside the brace.

"Aap chahte hain main unse—"

"Abhi tumhara ilaaj ho raha hai."

The old anger flashed: another question turned into an instruction.

"Main poochh raha hoon aap kya chahte hain."

Ram stopped.

The promise from the morning was no longer an easy thing said after an admission. His son had put a hand against it and found whether it would hold.

"Main tumhe badla lene ka kaam nahi de raha," he said. "Yeh tumhara poochha hua sach hai. Hukum nahi."

Rudra listened until the two things separated.

He could ask about the dead without agreeing to become the weapon they had supposedly been waiting for.

---

The nurse beside him said they needed to end the call.

This time it did not feel as though the question had been carried out of the room unfinished so that a new day could disguise it. Ram had answered who bore responsibility. Rudra would still want to know more about those beings and what authority could possibly mean after an act like that. The remaining questions did not make the answer already given disappear.

"Kal aur baat karenge," his father said, then corrected the certainty. "Jab dono taraf bolne denge."

"Haan."

"Thak gaye ho?"

"Haan."

The ordinary answer took effort. He gave it anyway.

In Varanasi, Ram told his nurse he needed to stop too. Neither man had to keep breathing into the phone to prove which one could bear more of the truth.

The call ended. Their separate care continued.

---

Rudra slept badly at first.

In the dark between checks, he could hear the word above trying to become a direction. He would catch himself looking at the ceiling as though the plaster marked a border between the people being judged and the people who had never been made to answer.

He knew better than that. Pataal was not a room under Varanasi. Whatever governed another Loka was not waiting on the next floor of this hospital.

Still, the mind wanted a simple shape when the world became difficult. Above and below. Pure and dangerous. A family high enough to give an order; a child low enough to be included in it.

He lay still until the nurse came for the next check and asked for help with the position of the blanket. The ceiling returned to being plaster, stained near one corner, separating this bay from the work of people who would also be tired before the shift ended.

He could leave the impossible distance unmeasured tonight.

---

Day forty arrived through ordinary sounds: the handover, a wheeled stand being moved, somebody apologizing for knocking a cup against the bedside table.

Rudra answered the review honestly. He had slept in pieces. The pressure was less than on the night the kavach broke; the wrist and ankle still needed their supports, and the team had not cleared him to load them. Observation continued. There was no journey hidden inside the word better.

After the check and a period of rest, Vedant asked whom he wanted contacted first.

"Ma ko," Rudra said.

He had heard his father speak about the house.

His mother had said she would speak for herself.

---

Rukmini took the call after breakfast and her own prescribed care, with the shoulder supported and the phone placed so she did not have to keep one arm raised. Ram was not beside her. Dadi waited within reach, far enough from the speaker to give them the privacy they had asked for.

Her son said, "Aapne kal bola tha. Ghar ki baat."

"Haan."

He heard a small adjustment at her end and waited for it to finish.

"Main tumhe rote dekhti thi," she said. "Subah. Aangan mein."

There was no new place in the sentence. No secret country. Only the doorway whose position he could still have found with his eyes closed.

"Maine kai baar roka. Kai baar tumhare paas aayi. Par har baar nahi roka. Jo nahi rok paayi, use sirf tumhare papa ka faisla bolke apne se alag nahi karoongi."

Rudra shut his eyes.

He had been prepared for her to say that his father had been frightened. She had begun with the child she had seen.

---

"Aapko bhi lagta tha zaroori hai?"

Rukmini looked at the window, where the light had reached the lower frame.

"Mujhe tumhari jaan ka darr tha," she said. "Aur bahut baar us darr ke aage maine apni baat chhoti kar di."

She could have put the fear into an older language, given it the distance of extraordinary dangers. Her son would still have been left with the mornings when the kitchen smelled of breakfast and the stones under his feet had hurt.

"Tumhara dard chhota nahi tha," she said.

He opened his eyes again.

A child's crying had once seemed to him an embarrassing piece of evidence, something he needed to outgrow before he could be believed about how hard the training had been. Hearing his mother say she had seen it made the old shame move without quite leaving.

"Main kamzor tha?"

"Tum bachche the."

The answer came immediately.

Not a promise that he had secretly been the strongest thing in the courtyard. Not a claim that he had never needed to learn. A child could need teaching without having to deserve the right to cry.

---

For a while neither knew what to do with the quiet.

Rudra had always been able to ask his mother for something to eat. He had not known how to ask whether she remembered the person who had sat at the table with split skin and tried to act as though hunger was the only thing wrong.

Now she had answered a question he had never managed to make complete.

He wanted to comfort her. The wish came automatically when her voice thinned, a small domestic reflex as old as fetching the glass before she could reach for it.

He did not tell her she had done nothing wrong.

"Aapki awaaz theek nahi lag rahi," he said instead.

"Rona aa raha hai. Saans theek hai."

The precision nearly undid him.

She was letting him care about her without making that care disprove what he had said.

"Paani paas hai?"

"Haan. Abhi liya."

They waited. A nurse passed at Rudra's end and checked that the phone was not making him turn against the support. He let her move it nearer. The small interruption gave both of them time without requiring either to pretend the conversation had become easy.

---

"Tumhare papa tumse pyaar karte hain," Rukmini said later.

Rudra's face tightened.

"Mujhe pata hai."

She heard what he had been expecting after the sentence and did not give it to him.

"Uske baad lekin nahi bol rahi," she said. "Pyaar tha. Jo chot pahunchi, woh bhi thi. Tumse dono mein se ek chunne ko nahi keh rahi."

He looked at the strip of daylight on his blanket.

All his life, the demand had felt like a choice even when nobody stated it: either understand the father or speak for the child. If he did one honestly enough, the other would have to become a mistake.

His mother had not made the old mornings harmless.

She had stopped asking love to perform that work.

"Mujhe abhi maaf karna nahi aata," he said.

"Abhi maang bhi nahi rahi."

She would have to live with what he did not yet know how to give. So would he. Neither could finish the years in the small allowance of one call.

---

Before they ended, she asked about the shirt from the bag.

"Saamne se khulne waali pehni?"

"Haan. Madad se."

"Akele haath upar karke mat badalna."

"Pata hai, Ma."

The irritation was ordinary enough to make her smile, though he could not see it.

"Pata hona aur madad lena alag kaam hain."

He almost told her she could say that to Papa too.

Then he did tell her.

She gave a short laugh and stopped before it pulled at the shoulder. "Unse bhi wahi bolti hoon."

They said goodbye because both needed rest, not because either had absolved the other. After the line ended, Rukmini called Dadi to help her settle more comfortably.

In Prayagraj, Rudra asked for the stand to be lowered.

For the first time in years, remembering himself crying in the courtyard did not make him want to leave the child there alone.

---

In Mirzapur, Aadhya had managed half a paragraph of the college work before the rest period.

She had dictated it. The helper read it back, including a word which made no sense, and Aadhya corrected that word twice before realizing she had been the one saying it wrong. Ira laughed softly from the other bed.

"Bahut achha paragraph," she said. "Ab bas matlab aa jaaye."

Aadhya looked at her. "Tum likh lo."

The old answer was ready on Ira's tongue: give it here. She let the answer pass. The covered left eye, the limited work the right could comfortably do, the hand which still complained after too much use were not going to become somebody else's restrictions because the joke had been good.

"Kal bhi college rahega," she said.

"Dhamki mat do."

Sitara came in after speaking to the receiving contact. Rudra had asked for a short call later, after his rest. Did they want it?

They did. Sitara checked the interval with their team before confirming it. The paragraph stayed unfinished without either girl making an apology to the page.

---

When the call came, Rudra began by asking whether they had the time for something difficult.

"Haan," Aadhya said. "Bol."

He told them about his father's parents.

The words sounded different when he was responsible for giving them to somebody else. He could not use the word sent without explaining that the parents sending the baby were dying. He could not make massacre mean the loss of fighters and leave out the children his father had expressly included.

He took the time to say those things plainly.

Then he told them who Ram had held responsible: those governing the Lokas, the authority above human Kuls, choosing to destroy the lineage because its growing strength threatened them.

Nobody on the line hurried him through the account so they could tell him how to feel about it.

Aadhya rested her injured hand more comfortably with help. The phone stayed on its support. Ira listened with her eyes closed, not searching for another sight inside what a friend had actually said.

---

"Unhone tujhe kuchh karne ko kaha?" Aadhya asked.

"Nahi. Maine poochha. Bola, badla lene ka hukum nahi de rahe."

She let out a breath.

Rudra heard it and, for a moment, was angry. He had just told her about people being killed. Relief seemed like the wrong thing to have brought out of it.

"Tu khush hai?" he asked too sharply.

"Nahi."

She waited. He could hear a footstep at her end, then the quiet again.

"Mujhe darr laga ki ab tu apna ilaaj bhi unka karza samajhke karega. Jaldi theek hona hai kyunki kisi se badla lena hai."

Rudra looked towards Vedant's empty chair. The man was taking the rest arranged at the last handover. He had not appointed somebody to make sure the boy arrived at the right interpretation while he was away.

"Main bhi wahi sochne laga tha," Rudra admitted. "Seedha nahi. Par jaise abhi sabko bataana padega main kis taraf hoon."

"Humein abhi mat saabit kar," Aadhya said.

The softness of it hurt more than a demand might have.

---

Ira asked whether he wanted them to do anything with the account.

"Bas abhi apne paas rakho," he said. "Veer ko bhejne ki zaroorat nahi. Usse main baat kar loonga."

"Theek."

No one began making a list of people who would be entitled to hear it next. The fact that the story concerned a lineage did not make it the property of every person with a question about his blood.

Ira had not opened the private reminder from the earlier call. Nothing needed to be written while he was still deciding what he wanted to keep.

"Aapko kya laga?" Rudra asked suddenly.

Both girls were quiet.

"Aapko?" Aadhya repeated.

He shut his eyes. The politeness had escaped from another part of his life, the voice in which he asked someone older whether his answer was acceptable.

"Tum dono ko," he said, irritated with himself.

Aadhya did not laugh at the mistake.

"Abhi sun rahe hain," she said. "Ek minute de."

So he gave her one, though a minute on a hospital phone could feel longer than whole afternoons had once felt at the ghats.

---

"Yeh kisi bachche ki galti nahi thi," Ira said at last. "Aur tu us waqt tha hi nahi."

He knew. He had said something like it to himself through the night, and still his mind kept trying to volunteer him as the part of the story which could be changed.

Hearing it in her voice did not cure the thought.

It made him less alone while he refused it.

He asked about the paragraph because there was a little time left. Aadhya told him how far she had got, and about the word she had lost an argument with.

"Mujhe mat bhejna," Rudra said. "Main hospital mein hoon. Itni sazaa kaafi hai."

Her laugh was small and real. They ended soon afterwards, before the joke could become an excuse to overrun what either team had allowed.

The paragraph remained in Mirzapur.

The boy remained in Prayagraj, tired, with something terrible newly known and the sound of his friends still able to be themselves around it.

---

Ram woke from the afternoon rest when somebody said his Earth name.

"Ram-ji?"

He answered before he had quite opened his eyes. The nurse waited until he was properly awake before asking about the pain, and he gave her the answer from the body he was in, not the answer he wished could be true.

The site needed care. He still tired. The plan for support had not become unnecessary because he could speak to his son for longer than before. She checked what needed checking and helped him with the position which had begun to pull.

When Rukmini came back, she asked how he had slept.

"Thoda," he said.

"Maine bhi."

Dadi brought the small bag in with her and put it where neither patient would have to reach across an injured side to find anything. She asked the nurse about the next care interval, listened to the answer and went to sit down herself.

The room was quieter than the courtyard Ram kept hearing inside it.

---

"Baat hui?" he asked Rukmini.

"Haan."

He looked at her face.

She had cried. He could see it in the skin around her eyes, and the sight began collecting all the old impulses in him: ask what had happened, determine where the pain was coming from, decide what could be done to end it.

"Usne kya—"

He stopped.

Rukmini waited. She did not answer the question he had not yet chosen whether to ask.

"Tum dono ki baat thi," he said at last.

"Haan."

It would have been easier if she had offered to tell him everything anyway. He could have called the telling closeness and never had to name the relief he was trying to obtain from it.

She asked for help with her support before sitting. He waited for the movement to finish.

"Usse kya chahiye, woh usse poochhna," she said afterwards. "Mujhse uski taraf se jawaab mat lena."

Ram nodded.

It was an uncomfortable kindness, returning him to the person whose answer he was frightened of hearing.

---

He had survived long enough to become a father.

For years, that survival had looked to him like something borrowed in a place where it could be withdrawn without warning. A meal eaten, a gate shut, a boy asleep in another room: he had loved those things and listened through them for the sound which might end them.

He had not imagined every threat. He had fought. He had won against danger which would not have ceased to exist merely because he became gentler at home.

That fact had been useful to him in too many arguments with himself.

It could not tell him which morning should have stopped earlier. It could not make his son's frightened voice, described to him without shouting, into the price the world had demanded rather than the price he had chosen to exact.

Ram looked at the cup beside his bed.

He wanted water. The reach was wrong from this position. He called the nurse instead of testing whether he could manage it quickly enough that nobody would notice.

She helped, waited and took the cup back.

There had been no enemy in the room to overcome by refusing her.

---

Dhruv arrived during the period he had agreed to cover.

He asked after both of them, heard the answer and took the chair Dadi had left free. He did not bring a declaration about what the exposed colours must mean for the future. The people in the room already had enough future to be afraid of.

Ram asked whether Rudra had wanted another call.

"Poochein," Dhruv said, and the receiving contact was asked to check.

Nothing in the arrangement made a son answer because a father had become ready. In Prayagraj, Rudra was resting after speaking to the girls. The reply came back that he wanted the evening call if both teams allowed it.

Ram listened to that small condition.

He had once made readiness something another person was required to achieve when he said so. Now he had to be one of the people waiting for a time which did not belong to him alone.

---

Rukmini stayed until she needed the next rest.

She told him before leaving, instead of remaining in the chair until pain made the decision for her. Dhruv knew the receiving arrangement. Dadi would be nearby. The team was not leaving him unattended.

"Aaj har baat khatam mat karna," she said.

For a moment he thought she meant the history.

Then she touched the edge of the water tray with her good fingers, making sure it sat where the nurse had put it.

She meant the strength with which he would try to tell it.

"Jitna bol sakoon," he said.

"Jitna bolne dein."

He gave her a tired look, and this time the small smile belonged to both of them.

After she had gone, Ram lay still. The history would not become less true if he accepted the time his body needed before he spoke again.

---

By evening, Rudra no longer wanted to look at the ceiling.

He asked for the phone to be set where it had been in the morning and waited while the nurse made sure he was comfortable. Vedant had returned after rest, his arm supported, but did not sit in the place where Rudra's eyes would have to pass over him with every question.

At the far end, Ram had finished care. Rukmini was resting nearby. The nurse placed the phone within hearing and stayed available.

"Unke paas itni taaqat thi," Rudra said. "Phir bhi darr tha?"

"Haan."

"Toh main kitna bhi seekh loon, kisi ko phir bhi khatra lag sakta hoon."

Ram heard the boy find a terrible way to become responsible for somebody else's choice.

"Lag sakta hai," he said. "Usse tum galat nahi ho jaaoge."

The answer was less comforting than a promise that enough discipline would make him safe. It was also the first one Rudra could imagine remaining true after he left this bed.

---

"Phir main kya badal sakta hoon?"

Ram looked down at the hand which had once corrected the position of a child's foot with no patience for how much the correction hurt.

"Apna kiya," he said. "Kisi ko tumse khatra lagta hai, isliye tumhe us jaisa hona zaroori nahi. Aur darr lagta hai, isliye mere har kiye ko tum sahi mat maan lena."

Rudra stayed quiet.

He had not expected the admission to travel into an answer about power. His father had usually described a world hard enough to make hardness sound like the only sensible preparation for it.

There were still hard things in the world. The dead in Pataal had not been saved by someone refusing to become cruel in imitation of their killers.

Yet the man telling the story had a son who could ask a question. He had been given that much of a future. What he did with it could not be determined entirely by the people who had tried to end his own.

"Mujhe achha nahi lag raha aapse yeh poochhna," Rudra said.

"Poochho."

The word arrived without again behind it.

---

"Aapne kabhi socha, agar main kabhi ladna hi nahi chaahoon?"

The question had come larger than he intended. He was not promising never to fight. He could remember Veer falling and the people at the ghat, could not imagine standing safely aside while those things happened again.

He was asking whether the possibility belonged to him at all.

Ram took time.

"Maine tumhare bachne ke baare mein socha," he said. "Bahut. Tum kaise jeena chaahoge, uske baare mein utna nahi poochha."

Rudra looked towards the curtain opening. Nobody was listening there on behalf of a lineage, waiting to hear what the boy would offer.

"Ab poochh sakte hain."

"Haan."

The exchange did not settle what he would choose. It changed who might be allowed into the choosing.

There had been afternoons when going to the ghats instead of straight home had felt like a rebellion serious enough to require an explanation. He had not known how to ask for a life larger than that detour. Now the question had reached his father without either of them turning it into a test of how much he loved the family.

---

He asked whether his father thought somebody was coming to this hospital.

It was the practical fear beneath the ceiling, the one he had been putting off because the old history was easier to give a shape than an unknown person walking through the duty station.

"Abhi kaun kya jaanta hai, main uska daava nahi karoonga," Ram said. "Jitna pakka pata hoga, tumse chhupaane ko jawaab nahi banaaoonga."

He gave no invented assurance that every danger had ended, no certainty about a person or an arrival for which he had no evidence. Rudra would still tell the team about anything new he felt or saw. The people caring for him would not be made to work around a silence his family called protection.

The nurse checked his fatigue. He told her he could manage a little longer, and she allowed only a little.

"Aap bade ho gaye the," Rudra said. "Yahaan reh rahe the. Phir bhi aisa laga ki kabhi nahi ruk sakte?"

Ram's answer was quiet.

"Khatra sirf bachpan mein nahi tha. Baad mein bhi saamna hua. Main jeeta bhi."

---

For years, Rudra had imagined his father's victories as the places where fear must have ended. A man who could do what Ram did should surely have found a day on which enough became enough.

"Phir?"

"Tumhaare janam se chhe saal pehle bhi ek ladaai jeeti thi," Ram said. "Uske baad bhi ghar aake darr khatam nahi hua."

Rudra lay still.

That fight belonged to a time before his own life. Six years before it, Ram confirmed when he asked. Rudra could not search his childhood for the day his father had returned; there had been no child waiting for him then.

"Pehle se darr tha. Us saal aur badha."

Rudra could hear the distinction without yet knowing the event inside it.

His father had gone out into a world Rudra had not yet entered, fought somebody and come home. Years later, that fear had still occupied the courtyard where Rudra trained. The boy had understood his father's silence as another judgement on whether the morning's work had been good enough.

He looked at the phone.

"Mere janam se chhe saal pehle kya hua tha, Papa?"
