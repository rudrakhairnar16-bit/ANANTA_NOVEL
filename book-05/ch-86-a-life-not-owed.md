# CHAPTER EIGHTY-SIX — A LIFE NOT OWED

---

> *"They had sent a child away so that he could live.*
> *We were not required to turn every life after his into a repayment."*
>
> *— Ira's later notes on inherited debts*

---

"Kisi ki ladaai tumhare naam karne ka haq nahi tha mujhe," Ram said. "Main tumhe zinda rakhna chahta tha."

The answer reached the smaller child in the question before it reached the young man asking it.

Rudra looked at the phone.

"Ek saal ki umar se?"

"Haan. Main chahta tha tumhaara shareer taiyaar ho, jab samajh paao usse pehle bhi. Mujhe lagta tha, der ki toh bachne ka waqt nahi milega."

There it was: the reason, given without asking him to call the reason enough.

"Maine tumhe apne maa-baap ka badla lene ko paida nahi kiya," Ram said. "Na tumne kisi ko aisa waada kiya tha."

He did not name an enemy for a baby to inherit. He did not make the boy's survival a gift which would be taken back if he chose the wrong future.

Rudra breathed out slowly.

For years, the earliest training had seemed like evidence that the plan for his life had been completed before he could enter the room where it was kept. His father was saying the intention had been survival, not a vow. The methods had still occupied years of the life they were meant to preserve.

Both things remained in the answer.

---

"Mujhe zinda rakhna tha," he said. "Par zinda rehne ke alawa bhi kuchh tha na?"

Ram shut his eyes for a moment.

A small boy waking without pain. A morning which did not have to be won before breakfast. Ordinary things, made almost impossible to think about when a parent had taught himself that attending to them could be the lapse in which danger arrived.

"Tha," he said. "Maine uske liye jagah kam rakhi."

The old impulse was close: say what would change, offer a better timetable, make even this admission useful enough to become another instruction.

He let the impulse pass.

His son did not need an improved childhood assigned from a hospital bed.

"Ab tum jo chaahte ho, use sunna padega mujhe," he said.

"Agar achha na lage toh?"

"Tab bhi pehle sunoonga."

It was not a promise never to be frightened or disagree. It was a promise about what the man would do before he let either feeling become an order.

---

Rudra could remember accepting a hand after a fall only when he had become too tired to refuse it.

He did not have to decide this morning what every such memory meant. Nor did he have to turn one better answer into evidence that nothing bad had happened.

"Main abhi aapse gussa hoon," he said.

"Pata hai."

"Baat bhi karni hai."

"Karo."

For a little while that was all the repair either could manage: not going away, not pretending the anger had been a misunderstanding, leaving room for another sentence.

The nurse asked Rudra whether he needed the call ended. He did. The question had cost more than he had expected, even without a raised voice.

He told his father plainly.

"Aaraam karo," Ram said.

This time the words followed an answer instead of replacing one.

---

After the phone was lowered, Rudra watched the nurse move the stand clear.

His father had wanted him alive. He had known that, somewhere, before he knew Pataal or Daavansh. The knowledge had never quite been enough to explain why being loved could feel so much like being prepared for a death somebody else had already pictured.

Now he could ask the man who had done the picturing.

Not every answer would be good. Not every good answer would come soon enough to undo what it explained. He could still ask, and decide what to do with the answer without offering the whole rest of his life as proof that he had understood.

Vedant returned after speaking to the team about the next care interval. Rudra asked whether he could try sitting somewhere other than the bed.

The man did not turn the request into a drill.

"Team se poochhenge," he said.

Rudra nodded.

A question about an ordinary chair could have an ordinary answer, smaller than all the things the exposed colours had made other people fear. He wanted to find out what that answer would be, not discover whether he could force his body to ignore it.

---

While they waited for the assessment, he rested with his eyes closed.

No battlefield arrived. No lost parent spoke through the space behind his ribs. He had heard enough of real voices this morning to recognize that another person's history did not have to appear inside him before it counted as his family's.

The wrist lay still in its brace.

When the nurse came back with the next member of the team, he opened his eyes and listened.

---

The answer was a short trial, with help.

The clinician and therapist checked the morning's findings and what happened when his position changed. The chest symptoms had stayed quieter. That allowed a small step in the plan, not a declaration that the night of the shattering had left nothing behind.

He could be moved into the supported chair within the bay. He was not to stand, push through the wrist or put the injured ankle down to take his weight. The team would do the transfer with the equipment, keeping the supports and monitoring in place. They would decide together when he needed to go back.

Rudra listened to the whole answer.

Then he asked whether wanting to stop would count as the trial failing.

The therapist looked at him.

"Jab thako toh bataana hai. Wahi karna hai."

He nodded before the old shame could turn that into another instruction he had to endure silently.

---

The chair had to be made ready first.

The nurse checked the space, the supports and the things which would have to move with him. Vedant stepped out of the working area instead of offering the injured forearm as one more place for a patient to lean. The team explained what they needed Rudra to do and, more importantly, what they did not want him trying to do.

He wanted to help.

The wanting was almost physical: take some of his own weight, stop being a heavy piece of work, show that he understood how much trouble everybody was taking.

"Haath se mat dhakelna," the therapist reminded him.

He let the hand remain where it had been placed.

They moved him with the transfer equipment, the injured wrist protected and the leg supported. The leads and support went with him. Nobody asked him to prove he could stand between the bed and chair before they would let him sit.

When they had finished adjusting him, the nurse asked what he felt.

He told her.

She listened to the ordinary answer instead of searching the colours at his collar for a more important one.

---

The room looked different from the chair.

Not transformed. The same curtain had a faint stain lower down which he had not noticed from the bed. The shelf on the opposite side seemed nearer the floor. He could see the line where a fresh coat of paint had stopped short of a bracket and left a little island of the older colour behind.

Somebody had painted this room badly and people had continued being treated in it.

The thought pleased him for reasons he could not have explained without making it sound foolish.

"Kaisa lag raha hai?" Vedant asked from beyond the working space.

Rudra looked at the empty bed.

"Kisi aur ka lag raha hai."

The man followed his gaze.

"Wapas tumhara hi hoga."

"Jaanta hoon. Itni jaldi kabzaa chhodne waala nahi."

The joke took no force from the wrist, no bright line from the chest. It cost only enough breath to make him aware that he should not keep adding to it.

He rested.

---

A short while later he said he was getting tired.

He almost improved the statement with a promise that he could manage longer if necessary. The promise reached his mouth and stayed there, unused.

The therapist checked him. They did not call the fatigue betrayal, and they did not decide that admission required him to remain sitting until he had overcome it. The team prepared the return.

He let them help again.

Back in the bed, the familiar support beneath his ankle felt less like the edge of a world he could never leave. He had left it by a few feet and returned without having to pretend that the feet did not matter.

His wrist ached with the tiredness of the day, not because he had secretly pushed through it. He answered the nurse's question about it and let her check the position rather than treating silence as the price of keeping the chair permission.

The next attempt would depend on the next assessment.

No one had cleared a walk, a journey or a fight.

---

After he had rested, Vedant asked what could be included in the update to the agreed contacts.

Rudra wanted them to know about the chair. He also wanted the limits included, so that nobody waiting in another city would hear sat up and begin planning a homecoming the team had not approved.

The receiving contact passed on that small, accurate account: a short fully assisted trial in the bay, return for fatigue, supports and observation still needed, no loading or travel clearance.

No family history went with it.

In Varanasi, Ram heard the update after care and asked the contact to thank the team. Rukmini heard it in the nearby room, asked whether the return had been planned rather than another emergency and was told it had.

In Mirzapur, Sitara checked that the girls were awake before giving them the same permitted care news. Aadhya smiled at the idea of Rudra pretending not to like an ordinary chair. Ira asked no one to make the news into proof that he was ready for more.

At the Prayagraj post, Veer heard it after his own rest.

The chair had travelled nowhere.

The truth about it had reached the people Rudra wanted told.

---

The call to Veer came after another rest, when the team at the post confirmed he could speak.

"Chair tak pahunch gaye," his friend said.

"Haan. Baahar ghoomne mat aa jaana. Team ko uthaa ke waapas rakhna pada."

"Main khud kaun sa darwaaze tak daud raha hoon."

The humour stayed between the two bodies they actually had. Veer shifted with help before the phone was placed closer; the shoulder had settled after the night's check but was not going to hold the device for him. Rudra waited until he said he was ready.

Then he gave his friend the missing parts of the account.

Ram had held the authority above human Kuls, those governing the Lokas, responsible for choosing the massacre. Not another local rival family. The father had also named the opponent he defeated in 2020: Adhira. That later encounter had made an existing fear worse; it had not begun the training which started when Rudra was one.

He did not supply the fight his father had remembered in private. He knew only the parts Ram had actually told him.

Veer listened.

"Aur tu?" he asked.

---

Rudra was ready to explain what he thought about the authority, the killing and the way power could make a child into a threat before the child could act.

That was not what Veer had asked.

"Mera kya?"

"Itni baat sunke. Abhi kaisa hai?"

He looked at the brace, then away from it. The question was not a request for another medical report either.

"Thoda halka," he said. "Phir uske liye bhi bura lagta hai. Unke saath itna hua, aur mujhe—"

He stopped before making relief into another offence which the dead would supposedly want punished.

Veer did not finish the accusation for him.

"Halka kyun?" he asked.

"Papa ne bola, mujhe unka badla lene ke liye nahi paida kiya."

The words sounded younger than the history surrounding them. He had needed them more than he wanted his friend to hear.

"Achha hai ki bola," Veer said.

He did not add that this made the rest acceptable.

---

"Main kuchh nahi karoonga, aisa bhi nahi hai," Rudra said quickly.

"Maine kab kaha?"

"Bas. Aisa na lage—"

"Tu mujhse bhi number le raha hai?"

Rudra shut his eyes.

There it was, caught without being made into a joke: the instinct to demonstrate that he had taken the history seriously by offering something difficult in return.

"Shayad."

At the post, Veer looked towards the passage while somebody waited for a nurse to finish with another patient. Nobody outside the call was keeping a mark for how well a boy had heard his family's dead.

"Mujhe bataana hai toh bata," he said. "Mere liye apna iraada bada mat kar."

Rudra stayed quiet long enough to hear the difference between a friend who would stand beside a choice and a friend demanding a choice impressive enough to stand beside.

He wanted the first kind.

He wanted to learn how to offer it back.

---

"Tera kal shoulder zyada dard kar raha tha?" he asked.

Veer gave him the actual answer. It had been checked; the changed position helped, and he still needed the support. The next movements were the ones the team permitted, not whatever the young man might decide was tolerable once the phone went away.

"Mujhe bol sakta hai," Rudra said.

"Bol diya."

He almost laughed.

"Haan."

There had been a time when he would have taken the short answer as dismissal. Now he could hear that no brave ending had been added for his benefit. His friend's shoulder hurt. Somebody qualified had checked it. A reply did not have to become a rescue before it counted as care.

They talked a little about the college page and the way both of them had begun wanting ordinary unfinished work merely because it belonged to a future less immediate than the next injury.

Veer made no promise that catching up would be easy.

Rudra did not ask for one.

---

Before ending, he asked that the new history stay within the call for now.

"Haan," Veer said. "Tu khud bataana jise bataana hai."

"Girls ko 2020 waali poori baat nahi boli abhi."

"Meri taraf se nahi jaayegi."

There was no grand secrecy oath in it. Just a destination the message did not have permission to reach.

The nurse at Rudra's end asked whether he was ready to stop. He said yes without waiting for the voice to change from request to instruction.

"Shaam ko Papa se phir baat karni hai," he told Veer. "Agar dono taraf bolne dein."

"Theek."

His friend did not ask him to report the whole conversation afterwards.

The line ended. In the quiet which followed, Rudra found that the thing he wanted to ask his father had survived being left unspoken for an afternoon.

It was not a boast or a promise to hurt somebody.

For once, he did not need to make it sound like either before he could take it seriously.

---

Rukmini's shoulder was reviewed that afternoon.

She answered for herself: what hurt, what helped, which movement still pulled. Dadi carried the small bag and let her speak. The prescribed support and limits remained; having managed the previous day did not make the next one free of care.

When she was settled again, she asked after Ram. The receiving team told her he was resting before the evening interval. She left him to it.

There were years in which she might have taken worry from one bedside to another, believing that keeping every fear in circulation was the same as keeping everyone safe. Today she had told the clinician what was happening in her own body. She could let that be work enough for a little while.

She ate when the food came.

The son she wanted to protect had been helped into a chair and back to bed. She did not need to become unwell herself to prove how much the news meant.

---

Ram woke wanting to tell the boy not to try again too soon.

He could already hear the message: rest first, do not push, we cannot afford another night like that. Sensible words, until he listened to whom the we was making responsible for his fear.

The team had supervised the trial. The son had reported fatigue instead of hiding it. They had returned him to bed before the attempt became an emergency. Ram had heard all those things in the update and still begun planning an instruction as though nobody had thought to keep the boy safe until he did.

He asked the nurse to confirm the plan he himself was meant to follow that evening.

She did. He listened.

Then he asked whether the family call was still possible after care. The receiving contact checked the other end, and the answer came back with the same condition as before: short, if both could manage it.

He did not send the warning ahead of the call.

He would ask what the chair had been like.

---

When the line connected, Rudra expected his father to begin with a correction.

It was almost funny how faithfully the body prepared for an old conversation. His shoulders had tightened before Ram said a word, though he had done precisely what the clinicians asked.

"Suna, chair pe baithe the," his father said.

"Madad se."

"Pata hai. Kaisa laga?"

Rudra waited for the rest of the sentence.

None came.

"Achha," he said. "Phir thak gaya. Waapas rakh diya."

Ram's breath made a small sound against the phone. The nurse helped him settle; Rudra waited until the movement was finished instead of taking the sound for a judgement.

"Ab dard badha hai?" Ram asked.

"Check hua. Position theek ki. Abhi rest hi."

"Theek."

His father did not grant the permission a second time, as though the team's first permission had been provisional until he agreed. He did not promise a bigger movement tomorrow either.

Rudra felt his shoulders ease by a little.

---

"Aapko laga mujhe rokna chahiye tha?" he asked.

The words had come before he could make them polite.

Ram considered hiding the impulse. He had not acted on it; perhaps there was no need to give the boy another reason to distrust an answer which had been better this time.

"Pehle laga," he said. "Update poori suni. Tumne thakaan bataayi. Team thi. Phir apni taraf se hukum bhejne ka kya matlab?"

Rudra kept his eyes on the stand.

This was not his father becoming a different man in three days. The fear was still there. The thing which had changed was smaller and more difficult to trust: Ram had felt it and had not immediately made it somebody else's task.

"Kal phir aisa lage toh?"

"Poore haal poochhoonga. Sirf darr se faisla nahi bhejoonga."

Rudra did not tell him that this made everything all right.

"Theek," he said.

He could hear the promise without awarding it more years of proof than it had yet earned.

---

His mother was resting after her own care. Ram told him she had eaten and that Dadi was nearby; he did not turn her absence from the call into a worry the son needed to solve.

Rudra said he would speak to her at another permitted time.

"Unse jo baat ki, woh aapse chhupaane ke liye nahi thi," he added. "Par meri thi. Unki thi."

"Pata hai," Ram said. "Unse tumhara jawaab nahi maanga."

The son could not see the earlier half-question his father had stopped himself asking. He did not receive a claim that there had never been an impulse to intrude. He received the boundary which had survived it.

For a moment neither spoke.

Beyond the curtain in Prayagraj, somebody asked whether an ordinary bag could be moved off the spare chair. It could. The nurse moved it and returned to the work in front of her.

Rudra listened to the small answer.

He wanted the next question he asked to belong to him in the same plain way.

---

Elsewhere, the sheet carried away from the brass bowl lay open under a lamp.

A hand set a small weight beside the copied trace. The page flattened. Red-black and gold remained distinct in the marks which had been made when the concealment failed; folding the paper had not turned the two into one colour.

Another hand moved the lamp closer.

No hospital name appeared in the light. No ward, bed or road arrived on the page. It was a record of a signature, not a window into the life from which the signature came.

The sheet was covered with a second, blank piece of paper. Both were put aside together. The lamp stayed burning after the hands withdrew.

In Prayagraj, Rudra saw none of it.

The faint colours visible at his collar had not become a joined hiding edge. The room in which he spoke to his father remained an ordinary room around a body which could no longer be concealed in the old way.

---

"Jitna suna, usmein sab kuchh marne ke aas-paas aa jaata hai," he said.

Ram listened.

"Woh log mar rahe the. Aapko maar dete. Mujhe bachna hai."

He did not say any of those things was false. He could not make them less terrible by asking the account to become more pleasant.

"Par log the na. Sirf kisi ke khilaaf taaqat nahi."

"Haan," his father said.

Rudra looked at the place where the page had lain beside his bag. A few lines of college work had been enough to remind him that other people had entire afternoons outside the history he was hearing. Surely there had been ordinary hours in the lives being destroyed too, whether anybody could now tell him exactly what had happened in them or not.

He would not furnish those hours with invented voices. His father had refused to do that for him, and he understood the refusal better now.

Wanting something was not evidence that it could be remembered.

It was still a reason to ask what could be known.

---

"Mujhe unke baare mein jaanne ka mann hai," he said. "Isliye nahi ki phir main kuchh saabit karoon."

The last part came more firmly than the first.

Ram had begun the account because his son asked what the kavach had hidden. The boy had not asked to take charge of all the history which came out with the name.

He did not try to finish the desire for him.

"Sunoonga," he said.

Rudra waited through a small noise from the other ward. His father's nurse was making an adjustment. There was nothing hidden in the interruption; when Ram answered the check, the boy could hear that he was still there.

He had spent years learning to read a pause as preparation for the next demand.

This one could remain time given to another person's injured body.

---

He thought about Varanasi.

The approach to the ghats at an hour when the stalls were only beginning to open. The college table. The broken stones of the courtyard, which he could remember hurting and still remember as the place where evening light reached the neem first.

He did not want those memories removed so a grander origin could take their place. He had friends there. Work left unfinished. A mother whose laughter could catch on pain now, a father who had once wasted five furious minutes arguing with a screw.

Daavansh had not erased that man.

Pataal did not have to erase the city in which the man had become his father.

But keeping Varanasi could not require him to make everything before it into a name which appeared only when somebody needed him frightened enough to train.

There had been a place from which a child was sent. There were lives connected to his own which he could not reach by looking harder at the inside of his eyelids.

The distance was real.

He wanted to stop treating it as something shameful to want to cross.

---

"Ma ko laga toh nahi ki main ghar ki baat bhool jaaoonga?" he asked.

"Unse poochhna," Ram said gently. "Unki taraf se nahi boloonga. Main apni keh sakta hoon."

"Kahiye."

"Jinhone mujhe paala, unke saath mera jeena sach tha. Usse pehle jo tha, woh jaanne se jhooth nahi ho gaya. Tum bhi dono ko jaan sakte ho."

Rudra let the answer stay within what it could honestly give. His father's permission to want did not answer for his mother, approve an expedition or make an injured ankle ready for a doorway.

It did not have to do all those things to matter.

"Main aaj kahin jaane ki baat nahi kar raha," he said.

"Pata hai."

At the far end, Ram did not ask him to prove the sentence by withdrawing whatever came after it.

---

Rudra could feel the tiredness coming back.

The phone was still supported. He did not tighten his hand, hold a breath or ask the colours for the strength to finish. The nurses in both places were near enough to hear if either patient needed help. His mother was allowed to remain resting. His friends were not waiting on the line to turn his next words into a pledge.

For once he did not need an audience to make the question brave.

He had thought, before the first name, that the story would tell him which person to become. It had given him a father who could be loved and questioned, two real belongings in that father's life and dead people whose existence did not require him to promise another death.

What he wanted now was not an answer hidden in his own strength.

It was to stand, one day and only when he could, in a place whose existence he had finally been allowed to know.

He kept his foot supported and his voice plain.

"Papa," he said. "Aap mujhe Pataal dikhaayenge?"
