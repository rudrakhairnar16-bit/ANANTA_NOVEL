# CHAPTER NINETY-SIX — WHAT WAS HIS

---

> *"The picture reached them before the story was finished.*
> *It did not finish it for them."*
> *— Ira's later register, on the first photographs brought from Pataal*

---

"Apna laga," Rudra said. "Sirf mera nahi."

He had not known that was the answer until he heard himself give it.

On the screen, his father waited. His mother looked towards Ram and back, leaving the small silence alone. There was no ruin behind either of them. There was a wall he knew, the edge of a pillow, the particular concentration with which she kept the phone from leaning while trying not to seem occupied by it.

Rudra could belong to that view too.

"Pehle ghar dhoondh raha tha," he said. "Aapka. Dada-Dadi ka. Kaunsa hoga. Phir laga, paas waale mein bhi toh koi rehta hoga."

He meant the people he could not identify, not the beginning of an account in which every opening acquired a name because he needed one. He told them about the worn threshold. The broken part had first held his eye; then the smooth place had become harder to look at. Someone had crossed it often enough for stone to carry the difference.

He had looked for the grandness of loss and found a place where people had put their feet.

"Woh pehchaana nahi," he said. "Bas dekha."

Ram's face moved a little, not towards recognition. Rudra could not know everything inside the movement. He had spent enough of the journey resisting the wish to make stone answer him; he could resist making his father's expression answer too quickly.

"Aur?" Ram asked.

"Dukh hua. Par sirf dukh nahi."

He stopped because the rest sounded wrong before he said it. He had been glad to get there. He had sat looking at destruction and felt something loosen, the long uncertainty of whether the place existed for him only as a father's account. Gladness beside those ruins seemed an ugly thing to offer the man who had lost people there.

His mother asked whether the ankle needed changing.

It did not. He told her so.

The interruption gave him somewhere to begin again.

"Pahunchke achha bhi laga," he said. "Yeh bolna ajeeb lag raha hai."

Ram took a breath before answering.

"Pahunchna aur jo wahaan hua, dono ek baat nahi hain."

There was no triumph in the distinction. It did not ask him to be grateful for the massacre because it had eventually produced this journey. It made room for the son who had wanted to see and the people whose lives had been taken without wanting anything of the kind.

Rudra let the relief be small enough to bear.

---

His mother asked whether he still wanted to send the photographs.

"Aap dekhna chaahte ho?" he asked them both.

She said yes. Ram looked away from the screen for a moment, found the position which eased his side and answered for himself.

"Ek-ek karke."

The nurse helped Rudra change from the video to the image while their voices remained on the call. He did not try to keep the wrist hovering over the screen to prove how little assistance the task needed. His mother settled their phone where both could see it without her holding it up.

The first photograph went.

It was strange to watch the little sign that the file had arrived. Something made in another realm had become a small rectangle inside a familiar conversation. The machine had done nothing to distinguish it from a picture of a meal or a crooked shelf. He was grateful that the phone did not know enough to turn the sending into a ceremony.

"Khul gayi," his mother said.

They were quiet.

He could no longer see their faces while looking at the image on his own screen. The absence made him want to explain everything at once: the roof, the missing wall, the way the light had entered from above rather than from some farther room.

He said only the first necessary thing.

"Iske peechhe chhat nahi hai. Photo mein lag sakta hai ki andar aur kamra hai."

His mother made the small sound she used when she had understood a direction and was looking again. Ram asked which side the road had been on. Rudra described his position in the chair and where Sitara had stood to take the picture. The answer stayed on that little stretch; it did not become a route for someone who had never walked it.

"Darwaaze ke neeche jo halka hai," Ram said after a while. "Wahi ghisa hua hissa?"

"Haan."

There was a slight grain to his father's voice which the connection had not put there. Rudra kept the rest of his explanation behind his teeth until Ram had finished looking.

He had brought the view back. He could not decide how quickly another person ought to receive it.

Ram did not say that he knew the doorway.

He asked Rudra to leave the picture where it was for a little longer. A sound came through the call: cloth moving, his mother asking quietly if the position was comfortable, his father's answer. Then there was the silence of two people looking at something which neither could put back into its former shape.

Rudra had expected a question he could not answer. Whose house, how old, how much farther. He had prepared his honest limits as though they would be the difficult part.

It was harder to let the image be looked at without offering an explanation which could make looking hurt less.

"Bas us taraf ka thoda sa dekha," he said eventually. "Poora nahi."

"Samajh gaya," Ram answered.

Not a dismissal. A boundary accepted without being bargained down.

"Doosri bhejo."

Rudra asked for the next file to be sent. This one held more of the way between the broken fronts. His mother said that she could see why he had changed his description from one building to a street. There were edges beyond edges. Even the larger picture stopped before the place did.

"Iske aage hum nahi gaye," Rudra said.

His father asked where they had turned back. Rudra told him about the inspected outlook, the rest and the return before the care interval, not a farther promise which he would now have to make true. He said that Veer had wanted a pause, that Ira had used descriptions when looking became uncomfortable, that Aadhya had kept her hands protected. Those were the functional parts they had agreed could travel with his account.

He did not give their private sentences away.

"Saath the," his mother said.

"Haan. Par sab alag-alag cheez dekh rahe the."

It had not felt like a disagreement while they were there. Only now, telling it, did he understand how much the visit had depended on not making four bodies and four kinds of attention into one witness.

---

Ram said he was glad Rudra had come back when he needed to.

The words landed beside the road in the photograph. Not on top of it. They did not claim the road had become unimportant because a boy's ankle tired on it.

"Aapko kaisa laga?" Rudra asked.

His father took time.

"Dekhke dukh hua. Aur tumne dekha, yeh jaankar kuchh aur bhi laga. Abhi uska shabd nahi hai."

Rudra had wanted a clearer answer. He heard his own unfinishedness inside the one he received and stopped wishing to have it cleared away for him alone.

They could be related without arriving at the same sentence together.

"Mujhe bhi nahi mil raha," he said.

"Toh abhi rehne do."

His mother's voice came closer to the phone. She asked whether there had been anything he had wanted to bring besides pictures.

He told her about the loose grit in the ankle cloth, the moment in his good palm and the choice to leave it clear of the wheels. He did not make the leaving sound wiser than the wanting had been. It had been easier to imagine placing something in his father's hand than saying he had seen a place he still could not properly explain.

"Yeh bhi keh sakte ho," she said.

"Abhi kaha."

"Haan. Sun liya."

He smiled. She had given the sentence somewhere to arrive without turning it into advice about how he should feel next.

---

The nurse came to check whether they were ready for the next part of the morning's care. Rudra noticed his good hand had begun curling against the sheet as though it still had to hold the conversation upright.

He let it rest.

"Abhi thoda thak gaya," he told his parents. "Baad mein baat karenge."

There was more he could say. That did not make the tiredness an inconvenient lie.

They agreed to stop. He asked that the photographs stay with them for now. His mother could ask Dadi later whether she wanted to see; no one needed to pass the files farther merely because they could be passed. Ram said he would look again after his own rest, not keep the son on the call as a guide beside every broken edge.

Before ending, his mother asked if he had wanted them to see something of him in the pictures.

He knew what she meant. Neither showed the chair, his face or the people around him. He had chosen the view rather than proof of a boy beside it.

"Main bhi tha," he said. "Photo mein nahi hoon, par tha."

"Pata hai."

The call ended without a new family secret.

Rudra looked at the darkened screen until it reflected only a piece of the room. He was still angry about things which had happened long before the journey. He was still glad his father had looked. Neither feeling had to be removed to keep the other honest.

When the nurse asked about the support, he turned towards her and answered the present question.

The morning review was not interested in whether the photographs had been received well.

It was interested in the ankle, the knee, the wrist and the work the outing had asked of the rest of him. Rudra described the ache after waking, the easier part after breakfast and the tiredness during the call. When asked what he wanted to try that day, he said he had been thinking of the few ward steps he had managed before the journey.

The clinician examined him before answering. The therapist asked about the transfers and how the support had sat on the return. There would be no increase in walking that morning. They could do the necessary assisted movement, settle him again and see how the day went; tomorrow's review would consider tomorrow's function.

For a moment the answer made the trip feel like something he had spent which he should have saved.

"Kal nahi jaata toh aaj kar paata?" he asked.

The clinician would not invent the other day for him. Yesterday's effort might be part of the tiredness. So might the rest of his recovery. They had today's body to examine, not a second Rudra who had stayed behind for comparison.

The visit had carried a cost. That did not make every later ache proof that choosing it had been wrong.

He asked what they needed him to report if it changed. They told him. He repeated the part he was unsure about instead of nodding as though understanding were a favour he owed them.

During the assisted movement, he let the good hand take only the work intended for it. The ankle was protected. The injured wrist remained clear. When the knee pulled, he said so before the position was held too long.

They changed it.

Afterwards there was no mark entered against Pataal for the ordinary fact that he still needed help.

---

Vedant brought the practical account of the outing only after the care was finished.

The receiving people had what they needed. The drivers and attendant had completed the agreed return work; they were not still waiting somewhere for a command to take the four out again. The route sketch remained an account of the inspected line, not permission to add another turn to it because the first journey had succeeded.

Rudra asked if he could keep a copy of the few practical points concerning his own movement. Vedant said they could set that down in plain words, with the current limits, not draw a bold line through every difficulty now that they were on this side.

"Agli baar ka plan?" Rudra asked.

"Kal ke kaam ka hisaab. Agli baar abhi tay nahi hai."

He accepted the distinction more slowly than the sentence deserved. Part of him had wanted a next visit waiting at once, so leaving could not be mistaken for the end of his connection. He looked at the chair beside the bed. He had not mistaken sleeping for ceasing to be a son. Perhaps a place could remain part of his life through an interval in which he was not travelling towards it.

Vedant asked whether he needed anything within the few minutes before his own forearm review.

Rudra wanted to add the words which belonged beside the photographs.

"Baad mein kar loonga," he said, seeing the time.

Vedant put the phone within the permitted reach and went when he needed to go. He had not become less useful by having a body whose care could not be postponed around every other person's request.

---

After lunch and rest, Sitara had time to help with a short note.

Rudra wanted his parents to have it with the two images, not a label left loose in a conversation which might later be remembered differently. He dictated where he had been sitting, who had taken the pictures, which edges the first frame concealed and that the second stopped before the whole view did. He did not number the unknown buildings as if numbering them had established what they were.

At the end he added that no household had been identified.

Then he asked her to read it back.

The note sounded careful and strangely absent of him. It could have accompanied images handed to a stranger by someone paid to describe a route.

"Ek line aur," he said.

Sitara waited.

He wanted to write that he had felt at home, but it had not been that simple. He had felt lost, glad, ashamed of the gladness, connected, tired. The neat phrase would have asked his father to receive an ease which the son had not actually experienced.

"Likho…Wahaan baithke laga ki main in logon ko jaanta nahi. Par anjaan rehne se unka mujhse rishta khatam nahi ho jaata."

When Sitara read it back, he let the imperfect words stay.

She helped send the note to his parents with the images already in their conversation. Nothing from Ira's phone went with it. He thanked Sitara, put the screen aside and asked for the water to be moved a little nearer before she left.

The message received a brief answer: they would keep it with the pictures.

He did not have to watch the phone until another response arrived.

Outside, a delivery worker argued mildly with someone about the right entrance for a carton. A trolley rattled over a join in the floor. The room remained a place in which other people's days crossed his without belonging to his story.

He closed his eyes and let them go on.

The short time together that afternoon was allowed to begin without the phones.

Veer had a complaint about a pillow which seemed determined to put his shoulder in the one position it disliked. Aadhya said a pillow was a poor opponent to accuse of strategy. Ira asked whether anyone had tried turning it before deciding it had chosen a side.

They had. The pillow was cleared of intention, though not of being uncomfortable.

Rudra laughed, then shifted because laughing had changed the way he was resting. No one made a lesson of that either.

The staff had helped him into the chair for the move. Veer had come with his own aid and a seat arranged. Aadhya kept the protected hands supported. Ira had chosen the shaded side, using the ordinary eye briefly before letting it rest. The time would end when care or tiredness required it, not when all four had produced a useful conclusion.

Rudra told them he had sent the photographs to his parents and added his own note. He described what he had chosen to tell about himself. He did not repeat his father's pauses or offer the family's half-formed reactions for the others to interpret.

"Theek laga?" Aadhya asked.

"Bolna? Haan. Khatam nahi hua."

"Khatam karna tha?"

He looked at her. She was turning the edge of a cloth into a shape which did not require a hard pinch. She left it unfinished when the fingers began to tire.

"Pata nahi," he said. "Shayad."

---

Veer said the road had surprised him most.

Rudra had expected him to say the size of the damage. Veer meant the ordinary possibility of going from one opening to the next. A place which could be fought over was one sort of picture. A place where someone might simply have walked out to find another person was different.

"Har jagah khada hone ki jagah nahi hoti," he said. "Kabhi bas guzarna hota hai."

He adjusted the aid beside his chair so it would be where he needed it when he rose. The sentence did not turn him into a man who had stopped wanting to protect anyone. It made the wish less likely to occupy every bit of ground before him.

Ira said that she had been angry when the ordinary eye began watering.

"Tumse nahi," she added before anyone could apologize for the air.

She had wanted to keep looking. Asking for rest had felt, for a moment, like accepting that the visit belonged more fully to whoever could see through all of it.

"Phir jo tum log bataa rahe the, usse alag cheez mili," she said. "Zyaada nahi. Alag."

She had not acquired a hidden view beyond the closed lid. She had heard where each voice slowed, which detail was difficult to describe, when someone corrected the shape of something first called a room. Her account contained those voices as sources, not replacements for eyesight or proof she could dispense with care.

Rudra had watched her rest. He had not known she was also giving up the particular picture of the visit she had wanted.

She had told them now. That was how he knew.

---

Aadhya said she had wanted something green in the hollow where the wind made its noise.

She looked at her hands rather than the little plant near the window in the room. It had no connection with Pataal, no duty to become an emblem because it happened to be within reach of the thought.

"Ek patta bhi," she said. "Bas itna."

"Kiya nahi," Rudra said.

"Nahi."

She let him hear both the wanting and the decision. No one had asked the place what it needed. No one had examined the cost to her hands and declared it insignificant because the act could be described as small. She had gone as a person allowed to look, not a promise that living things would spring up to repair whatever the four could bear to notice.

"Wahaan hona bhi kuchh tha," she said.

Rudra understood that she was not offering him a prize for sitting in a chair. She was resisting the same bargain on her own behalf: the idea that being present counted only if the body produced something to leave behind.

"Tha," he answered.

She glanced at him and smiled a little. It did not require either of them to reach across the space.

---

There was no single sentence which could contain all four visits.

They had been together in one place. It was still possible for Veer to remember a road, Ira the work of receiving other people's descriptions, Aadhya the leaf she had chosen not to grow, and Rudra the worn edge of an unknown threshold. One account did not have to defeat the others to deserve being kept.

When Sitara came to see whether they needed anything before separating for care, Rudra asked if they could leave the conversations there for now.

They could.

Ira wanted quiet. Veer needed to change the knee's position. Aadhya had her own treatment waiting. The staff helped Rudra back without requiring his friends to become the necessary lifting team.

His phone stayed dark during the move.

There was nothing on it which needed him more urgently than the ankle did.

That evening, after food and the next checks, Rudra read the answer his parents had sent to the note.

His father had added one sentence beneath the acknowledgement: he would keep the words with the pictures, not turn them into names the son had not found. His mother wanted him to sleep before trying to write anything more for them.

He did not answer with another paragraph. He sent that he had eaten and was following the rest already agreed.

The phone was put aside.

Near the collar, the visible colour remained part of the body the journey had brought back. There was no new layer between him and whatever had already been exposed beyond the people who loved him. Nor had the visit supplied a set of instructions for the colours. He had looked at a threshold; the threshold had not explained his blood.

He pulled the sheet into the comfortable place with help and let that be the last useful thing done that evening.

The night contained waking, a position changed because it had begun to pull, water, then more sleep. Nobody had to call the view back in order to make the darkness significant.

---

On day eighty-three, the therapist asked about the night before asking him to stand.

The examination and review came first. A brief assisted stand for the necessary transfer was manageable again; more walking was not added simply because he wanted it. The support would stay, the protected load remain limited, and the chair would take him where standing was not the task.

Rudra heard the difference between an invitation to try the allowed movement and a challenge to make it look easy.

He made the transfer with help. The good hand found the support, the wrist remained clear and the ankle accepted only what had been agreed. When the knee needed an adjustment before sitting, he asked for it. The therapist gave him time instead of making the next instruction louder.

Afterwards he was tired, but not in the same way as the morning before. The distinction went into the account with the ache, not into a private verdict that he had recovered everything which mattered.

The chair took him to the place near the window.

There were people on the road outside whose destinations he did not know. One boy was holding a second helmet while the rider searched a pocket. A woman waited beside a sack with a tear in one corner, keeping her foot near the escaping grain until somebody brought a bag. They were inconveniences small enough to remain inconveniences because other people had stopped beside them.

Rudra watched until the sack was moved.

He had wanted the whole view of a lost place. Here too, even with a window and a road he could see, he knew almost none of the lives crossing it.

That did not leave the road empty.

---

Vedant came after his own review with the plain account Rudra had asked for.

It was no new chart of the passage. It set down the limited movement the boy had actually needed help with, the places where they had rested and the symptoms to carry back to the treating people. Yesterday's successful return was not written as next week's clearance.

Vedant read it aloud. Rudra corrected one point: the pressure at the ankle had begun before the final outlook, not during the return. Vedant changed the line without defending the version he had brought.

"Aur?" he asked.

Rudra wanted to add where he had kept turning to look back. The words became tangled. He began again, trying to find the exact place in the turn, then noticed that the good hand had tightened on the edge of the sheet.

Vedant asked whether they should leave the remaining sentence until later.

"Bas ek line hai."

"Toh baad mein bhi ek hi rahegi."

He had not raised his voice. He was not taking the page away as a punishment for wanting to finish. He set it where Rudra could hear the next reading when there was room for one, then asked if the support needed changing before he went.

Rudra said it was comfortable. He was simply tired.

Vedant accepted the answer.

---

The boy looked at the unfinished account.

A few weeks earlier, he might have thought stopping at one line short was a particularly quiet sort of defeat. He still disliked it. That part had not been educated out of him by a single journey.

But he had seen the wheel freed because nobody forced it. He had seen a road by turning the chair instead of straining the body. He had come back from a place he had wanted badly to stay in, and the leaving had not erased his arrival.

Vedant was putting the pen away. There was no secret diagram on the page, no technique withheld until the student proved he could endure the pain of receiving it. There was one incomplete sentence and a man willing to let it remain incomplete.

Rudra asked before the mentor reached the door.

"Aapne kab seekha ki rokna bhi sikhaana hota hai?"
