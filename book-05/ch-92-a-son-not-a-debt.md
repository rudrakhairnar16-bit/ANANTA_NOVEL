# CHAPTER NINETY-TWO — A SON, NOT A DEBT

---

> *"The account ended with neither pardon nor oath.*
> *A son kept his father. He did not take his place among the dead."*
> *— Ira's later register, closing note on the first family account*

---

"Sach," Rudra said. "Aur mere faisle ki jagah. Dono chaahiye aapse."

Ram listened.

The answer had come without the list Rudra had once imagined he would need: every morning, every question cut short, every occasion on which a child had mistaken being frightened for being wrong. Those things remained. He did not have to recite them all before he could ask for something now.

"Jo poochhoon, usse bas meri galti theek karne ki baat mat banaaiye," he said. "Kabhi aapki baat se bhi mujhe galat lag sakta hai. Bol paaun."

"Bol sakte ho."

"Abhi kehna aasaan hai. Phir jab mujhe kuchh karna ho jo aap nahi chaahte—"

He stopped for breath. The nurse was close; he answered her check, let the position be adjusted and returned to the sentence without forcing it through the body.

"Tab bhi meri baat sunna. Sirf itna nahi ki mujhe kaise roken."

Ram looked towards the good hand lying open on his own blanket.

"Darr lagega," he said. "Phir bhi poora sunna mera kaam rahega. Sirf darr ko tumhaara jawaab nahi banaaoonga."

It was smaller than a promise that he would never fail again. The son could hear what would have to happen the next time for it to become more than a sentence.

He kept going.

---

"Mujhe aapka beta rehna hai," Rudra said. "Aapki har baat maan kar nahi."

At the other end, Ram's face changed. He had been listening for a departure, a refusal, the place where his son would decide that too much of the family had been taken away for the old word to remain.

The boy had kept the word and changed the obedience inside it.

"Mera beta hona tumhe meri haan se nahi mila," Ram said. "Meri na se chhin bhi nahi jaayega."

Rudra swallowed.

There had been times when a stumble, an unfinished exercise, a hand which could not hold its position had felt like something being removed from him. He had heard his father admit that before. Today he could say what he wanted kept without pretending he no longer remembered the removals.

"Pataal dekhna chahta hoon," he said. "Unke saath jo hua, usse bachke nahi nikalna. Par unki zindagi ke baad jo meri hai, woh poori karz nahi ban sakti."

"Nahi banegi."

"Sirf aapke kehne se nahi hoga. Mujhe bhi yaad rakhna padega."

"Haan."

The answer did not enlist him and did not forbid him every future act of courage. He could choose to help, choose to fight when something real required it, choose a life which contained the dead without making every living hour an instalment paid to them.

He had not come into the world having signed for a massacre he could not prevent.

---

"Aur jo aapse naraaz hoon," he said.

Ram waited.

"Woh abhi hai. Aapki kahaani sunke khatam nahi hui. Par sirf wahi bhi nahi hai."

The older man took his next breath slowly.

"Dono sun raha hoon."

He did not ask which feeling would win by evening. He did not ask for a gentler version to be sent to Rukmini, or for the son to let his friends know that a father had finally answered well.

Rudra could feel the tiredness arriving. He told the nurse. She checked him and said the call needed to end soon.

He looked at the phone, afraid for one irrational moment that ending here would make the unfinished anger the last thing left between them.

"Phir baat hogi," he said.

"Hogi," Ram answered. "Dono ko rest ke baad."

The qualification made the promise more solid, not less. Another conversation would happen through the ordinary arrangements by which their bodies were being kept alive. Neither had to purchase it by pretending to be well enough now.

They said goodbye.

---

Afterwards, Ram let the nurse help him settle.

There was no great movement available to him, no journey across the room which could carry the distance between Varanasi and Prayagraj. He accepted the adjustment, answered about the injured side and rested when asked.

The son had asked him to listen.

He could begin by not using the end of the call to send one more instruction through somebody else's phone.

In Prayagraj, Rudra closed his eyes after the check. The body was tired. The wrist and ankle remained injured; the raw colours were still part of a signature the old kavach no longer hid. He had not gained a new means of surviving the world by saying what he wanted from his father.

He had gained a sentence he had been allowed to finish.

Night carried them from day fifty-seven into fifty-eight.

There were checks and sleep, the sound of shifts changing, a cup moved quietly so that the person waking for care would not have to reach too far. In the morning Rudra told the clinician how tired he had been after the conversation. The chest had settled with rest. The wrist and ankle were reviewed in their own right, not treated as parts of an emotional recovery which could be declared complete because somebody had cried or spoken honestly.

The ward plan allowed a short staff-propelled chair visit to the corridor window after care and rest. The same full assistance would be needed for the transfer, the same supports and equipment would travel with the chair. No standing, no push through the injured wrist, no attempt to let the ankle discover its own limit.

He chose the window instead of another conversation in that interval.

The nurse checked the position before they moved. At the end of the corridor, the chair stopped where the light reached the floor but not directly into his eyes.

Outside, someone had hung washing on a rooftop. One sleeve had caught over the line, turning a shirt into a ridiculous thing with one arm raised and the other hanging as though it regretted the gesture. Farther down, a man was trying to move a bicycle past a parked scooter. The bell rang once, unnecessarily. Nobody in the way appeared impressed.

Prayagraj continued to contain people who did not know what he had said to his father.

He was grateful for them.

---

The air near the window smelled faintly of food from somewhere below.

He could not identify it. For a while he tried anyway, turning the possibilities over with more concentration than any of them deserved. The helper guessed something different. Rudra disagreed. Neither could prove the matter from this distance, and the bicycle had already gone before either noticed it leaving.

When the nurse asked about fatigue, he was tempted to give the answer which would buy a longer view.

He wanted to remain where the morning was happening outside him.

"Thoda," he said instead.

They checked him and began the return. He felt the disappointment of the chair moving away, sharper than he wanted it to be after all the fine things he had been saying about ordinary needs. He let it be sharp without making the nurse wrong for hearing the answer he had actually given.

The transfer back was fully helped. He did not lend a bad wrist to the movement because he was impatient to be settled. Afterwards he reported where the ankle support had become uncomfortable, waited for the adjustment and rested.

The view had been real. So had the limit.

There could be another day with different permissions. There did not have to be a secret attempt later that morning to take the rest of the view for himself.

---

Veer's call came after the next care and rest interval, when the post and ward could both manage it.

Rudra told him the answer about disclosure: no date had been set. The father had kept waiting for readiness which fear could always move farther away. He told the limited doorway memory too, identifying it as something Ram had related on the later call, not something the boys had learned from the account of the infant's arrival.

Veer listened.

"Tumne poochh liya," he said.

"Haan."

"Jawaab pasand aaya?"

Rudra considered it.

"Achha nahi laga. Par jawaab tha."

The friend at the post was quiet for a while. The helper moved the phone a little closer when Veer asked, and he spoke without holding the shoulder rigid to reach it.

"Ab?"

"Unhe bola, meri baat bhi sunni hai. Har baat meri samajh ki galti nahi."

"Theek bola."

"Darr laga."

"Woh bhi theek."

Rudra looked at the place where the chair had stood before the transfer.

He told Veer that wanting to keep his father did not mean he had become comfortable with everything the man had done. It sounded clumsy spoken aloud to a friend. Veer let it be clumsy.

"Mujhe kisi ek taraf khadaa karna hai kya?" he asked at last.

"Nahi. Apni taraf raho. Mere dost ho. Unke wakeel nahi. Mere gusse ke bhi nahi."

Veer breathed out with something like a laugh.

"Achha hai. Itna padhna padta dono mein."

Rudra smiled. The nurse glanced across; he answered that he was comfortable, then let the moment settle before continuing.

---

They spoke for a little about the corridor window.

Veer disagreed with both guesses about the food, despite having neither seen nor smelled anything from it. Rudra accused him of conducting a very confident investigation from the wrong bed. Veer said confidence was the only part of the investigation not currently restricted by the care team.

The joke left the shoulder, knee and ribs where they were. When Veer needed the position changed, he said so. Rudra waited, listening to the ordinary work at the other end rather than asking the colours in his own body to do anything about the distance.

Before they ended, Veer said, "Sirf kuchh tootne pe phone mat karna."

"Khaane ki pehchaan pe bhi kar diya."

"Haan. Ab lag raha hai is shart pe phir baat karni padegi."

Rudra was still smiling when the call finished.

He had not needed his friend to become a witness for the defence or the prosecution. He had told him something difficult, then argued about a breakfast neither of them could identify. The wanting to see Pataal remained between them as a possibility to examine, not a summons which made this ordinary morning less worthy of being kept.

The night between days fifty-eight and fifty-nine contained nothing the patients had to turn into a sign.

There was sleep, not enough in one stretch and useful in the stretches which came. There were checks. In the morning, Rudra reported the tiredness after the window, the ankle discomfort which had eased with adjustment, the conversation which had followed after rest. The team reviewed him before continuing the limited ward activity. Better breathing and an easier meal still did not permit weight through the ankle, a push through the wrist or a departure from care.

In Varanasi, Rukmini finished her own review before choosing a call with him.

Her shoulder still needed protection. She accepted help with the support, ate and rested first. When the helper settled the phone, she asked for it to be moved slightly so she would not have to turn towards the injured side. The small change made her voice sound easier when the line connected.

Rudra noticed.

"Ab theek jagah hai?" he asked.

"Haan. Ab tum bataao."

He told her about the window and the shirt caught on the line outside it. She said he had no right to criticize other people's collars after the description he had given of his own.

"Mera jaan-boojhke tedha nahi hota."

"Usne tumhe bataaya kya? Shirt ne?"

He smiled, and for a while that was the conversation: an argument without a cost attached, his mother being funny because something was funny and not because somebody needed reassuring that the family had survived a revelation.

---

Later he told her he had said what he wanted from his father.

Not the whole private conversation. The part which belonged to his own choice: he wanted to remain a son without being required to make obedience the proof, wanted to hear the history without having his life written as repayment for it.

Rukmini listened.

"Bolne ke baad kaisa laga?"

"Thak gaya. Phir laga abhi bhi ghar jaana hai. Aapse milna hai. Unse bhi."

The last two words came carefully.

"Naraaz hoon toh bhi."

"Haan," she said.

He waited for the claim which might once have followed: that wanting to come home proved the anger had never been serious, that love would settle the rest when they were together again.

She made none.

"Mujhe bhi tumse milna hai," she said instead. "Jab sach mein ho sake. Abhi phone pe tum bol rahe ho, woh bhi sun rahi hoon."

There was relief in being missed without being hurried out of the bed. He could want the familiar door while remaining in Prayagraj, could want to see Pataal without making his mother's city a place he had outgrown.

"Aapko darr lagta hai," he said.

"Haan."

"Mujhe bhi."

The admissions stayed beside each other. Neither became the other's instruction.

Rukmini asked him to keep reporting what his body was actually doing, even when it made a hoped-for thing take longer. Not for her to know every private thought; for the people caring for him to know when they needed to help. He said he would. It was a promise about truth he could practise, not a guarantee that wanting to live would make every later place safe.

---

Before they ended, he asked whether Dadi had found the right shirt.

"Abhi kaam chal raha hai," Rukmini said. "Tumhaari itni badi pehchaan di hui hai."

"Rang bhi bataaya tha."

"Do alag rang bataaye the."

He tried again. The helper at her end laughed. Rukmini repeated the new description so somebody could remember it later, when the ordinary work allowed; nobody was being sent away from a patient to prove that a familiar shirt could be found on command.

For the moment, the one he had was comfortable with help. That would do.

They said goodbye before tiredness made it difficult.

---

Rukmini lay quietly after the phone was taken away.

Her free hand rested palm upwards on the sheet. Near the wrist, a small pulse moved beneath the skin, almost lost whenever the light changed across it. She watched until she no longer had to look for each movement.

Her son was far away. He had asked hard questions and still wanted to come home. He had spoken of wanting to see another place without asking her to pretend she was not frightened. There was no photograph which could make all of that simple enough to send to someone else.

The pulse went on.

She let her fingers uncurl. The shoulder stayed supported. When the nurse came, she answered about the body in the bed, not only the boy whose voice had just left the room. The help offered was for her too.

Dadi returned a little later and saw the quiet face.

She did not ask for the son's exact words. She moved the water where Rukmini could reach it safely and sat for the interval she had available before her own rest.

Rukmini turned the good hand over on the sheet.

Neither woman needed to make the quiet into a message for Ram. He would have his own conversation with the boy when both could manage it. This one had belonged to a mother and a son, and the space after it was allowed to remain hers.

Day sixty began after another staffed night, in the three cities still separate.

Rudra's morning review used the actual outcomes of the recent meals and window visit. The quieter breathing had continued. His wrist and ankle still required protection, help and further review; a conversation about a journey could not supply the missing permissions. Veer's team had their own shoulder, rib and knee questions. In Mirzapur, Aadhya's hand and Ira's eyes remained individual matters, with no shared recovery date for friends who wanted the same thing.

After rest, a brief call linking the four became possible. Each had been asked whether they wanted it. Each team knew the interval; the phones were supported where they would not have to be held.

Rudra could hear the two girls at one end and Veer at the other. The familiar arrangement made him want to turn and find them in the room.

He stayed in the bed and listened.

"Paragraph ka mazaak baad mein," Aadhya said before he could begin.

"Main toh poochhne waala tha kaisi ho."

"Phir poochho."

He did.

They gave the actual answers: some things easier, some still tiring, the same needs which did not disappear because everyone could hear everyone else at once. Veer complained that a conversation requiring no travel still involved more arrangements than their old college plans. Ira pointed out that those plans had mostly consisted of arriving late and asking someone else what had been decided.

"Bahut galat yaad hai tumhe," Veer said.

"Abhi bhi poochh rahe ho kya tay hua."

For a little they were allowed to enjoy the familiar unfairness of being accurately described by a friend.

---

Rudra told them he wanted to begin asking for the practical information, not collect another round of promises.

What each could currently do, what would need to change before a later journey could be considered, what care could not be interrupted. They could ask their own teams first. If someone did not want to share a private detail with the whole group, they could choose what the people helping with future arrangements actually needed to know.

"Meri abhi waali baat wahi hai," Ira said. "Pehle samajhna hai."

"Haan," Rudra answered. "Usse badalne ko nahi keh raha."

Aadhya wanted the return questions kept beside the questions about arriving. Veer asked that nobody describe him to anyone as already fit to look after the other three. He would give his own account of the body he was in.

"Aur kisi ne rukna bola toh usko sabki himmat ka hisaab na dena pade," Aadhya added.

Rudra nodded, then remembered the others could not see it.

"Theek."

They had not made the unknown place safe. They had made it harder to hide a known difficulty inside excitement. It was a modest beginning and, unlike a declaration that friendship would handle everything, something they could actually do in the cities where they were.

The call ended within the permitted interval. There was no oath at the end, no agreement to practise a form in secret so the next review would find them more useful.

Later Rudra asked Vedant to help offer the practical questions to the appropriate care teams, with the others' agreement and only the necessary information shared. Vedant accepted the limited task. No departure booking or route had been arranged; the questions would have to reach the people who could honestly answer them before a plan could pretend to exist.

---

In Varanasi, the district had already found a less patient version.

Near the tournament ground, somebody said the boy had walked away after the light. By the next retelling, all four had stood together and agreed to fight again. A third voice supplied an audience which nobody who had actually been there remembered seeing.

"Tum the?" a man at the tea stall asked.

The speaker said someone he knew had been.

Beyond the stall, the boundary rope still kept people off the damaged part of the ground. A worker checked the edge from the permitted side, then put his tool down. The repair hours already done had been paid. Payment had not made the surface safe to open, and the rope had not lowered itself because a better story was circulating beside it.

Somebody repeated that the boy had walked.

"Yahaan toh nahi dekha," the worker said.

It was not enough to end the rumour. It was enough to leave one ordinary objection inside it.

The tea was poured. A customer asked for less sugar and received an argument about when he ought to have asked. The cups needed washing again.

No one at the stall knew the private words spoken between a father and son in two distant beds. No old name or ruined birthplace entered the talk. The district had public damage, remembered light and the confidence with which people added a body rising whenever a story seemed to need one.

In Prayagraj, the body was resting after care.

Rudra did not hear the tale. He had not stood, had not healed by learning his lineage, had not used his friends' interest to make himself the appointed leader of an already departing company.

The bed held him. The next question could wait until he had slept.

The evening family call was arranged after that rest, not instead of it.

Rudra had answered the next check and eaten with the help he still needed. In Varanasi, Ram's care was finished for the moment; the phone was settled where speaking would not make him hold an uncomfortable position. Rukmini was resting. Nobody brought her in to make a family tableau out of two people finally managing to speak for themselves.

"Doston se baat hui," Rudra said.

"Haan?"

He gave the practical part: they wanted accurate information from their own teams before anyone tried to make a journey plan. Their separate answers still belonged to them. Vedant would help put the questions to the right people, not announce that four patients were ready to leave.

"Achha," Ram said.

Rudra heard no disappointment that other people had been asked to help with something his father had offered to help prepare. The offer did not have to mean that every answer would come through one man's hands.

"Aapse ek baat aur," he said.

"Kaho."

---

"Aap wahaan se bachche the jab aaye. Apne maa-baap ko chhodne ka faisla nahi kiya tha."

Ram kept looking at the phone.

"Haan."

"Aur yahaan jo zindagi bani, woh unki jagah chori karke nahi bani. Jinhone paala, woh bhi apne the."

The son was not repeating an examination answer. His voice had the slow care of someone placing things beside each other because he had stopped believing that one had to be thrown away to make room for the next.

"Main Pataal ko aapki paidaish ki jagah keh sakta hoon," he said. "Aur Varanasi ko ghar. Ismein kisi ko kam nahi karna."

Ram's good hand moved once on the blanket.

"Nahi karna."

"Jo unke saath hua, galat tha. Aap bach gaye, woh galat nahi tha."

The older man shut his eyes for a moment.

He had told the account as a father, trying to let the son hear what had been kept. Now the son had given something back without offering himself as payment. The infant's life could remain a life saved, rather than the first entry in an obligation which had to pass from one child to another until it was settled in blood.

Rudra waited through the next breath.

"Main aapke liye wahaan ka bachpan laane nahi jaa sakta," he said. "Apna pehla dekhna hoga. Jab jaa sakein."

"Pata hai."

"Bas aapko bhi yaad rahe. Mujhe bhi."

Ram opened his eyes.

"Rahega."

---

They spoke about the corridor window for a while, then the shirt which had become a more difficult identification problem than either boy at the Prayagraj beds had expected. Rudra explained the muddle of colours he had given his mother when trying to describe it. Ram admitted he was unlikely to help distinguish two crooked collars by a colour Rudra had already described differently twice.

"Aapko toh pata hona chahiye," the son said.

"Maine pehna nahi hai."

"Phir bhi ghar mein tha."

"Screw bhi ghar mein tha. Usne bhi meri nahi suni."

Rudra laughed softly, stopped when he needed to and answered the nurse's glance. His father waited without using the interruption to make the laugh into evidence that everything had become easy.

When the line was quiet again, Rudra said, "Pyaar karta hoon aapse."

The words came plainly. He did not add a withdrawal of the anger to make them acceptable.

"Main bhi, beta," Ram said.

Rudra let himself hear it. He had always known something of that love, even when he had not known how to live beside its fear. Naming what had hurt him did not require him to pretend that no love had ever reached him. Accepting the love did not make the hurt disappear.

His father did not ask for the missing pardon.

---

The boy rested back into the support while the nurse checked him once more. The conversation was nearing its end. He said he was tired, that the position was comfortable now, that he needed only a little longer if the team allowed it.

A little was possible. Not an unlimited evening.

The wrist stayed in the brace. The ankle stayed protected. Beyond the window, Prayagraj's lights belonged to streets he could not yet walk. His friends had their own bodies and their own answers; his mother had her rest; the father at the other end was still an injured man, not a doorway which love could force open.

Rudra knew his father's birth name now. He knew why an infant had been sent away, where he had been found, that there had been years of being raised rather than merely years of preparing to fight. He knew the fear had not begun in 2020 and that a real victory then had not taught his father how to stop being afraid.

Those truths no longer waited outside his life for permission to enter.

He did not need to become the lost child in order to remain this man's son.

He looked at the phone, at the ordinary means by which they could still hear each other across the distance, and asked for what came next.

"Papa…Ab Pataal tak pahunchne ka raasta kaise tay karenge?"
