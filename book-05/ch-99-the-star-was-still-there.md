# CHAPTER NINETY-NINE — THE STAR WAS STILL THERE

---

> *"The sky in his account was clear.*
> *For a long time we had mistaken that for a clear way back."*
> *— Ira's later register, beside Dhruv's first account*

---

"Kyunki jeet tumhe uthaakar waapas nahi laati," Dhruv said.

Veer looked at the aid beside him.

The answer did not diminish winning. He had expected, perhaps, to be told that he had misunderstood it, that the things he had wanted from the akhada were childish and the wiser man had always known which desires to replace. Dhruv let the desire remain.

"Jeetna seekhna hai," he said. "Par uske baad bhi shareer tumhaara hi rehta hai. Thaka hua ho sakta hai. Galat raaste pe jaa sakta hai. Jiske liye khade hue, uske paas pahunchna bhi baaki ho sakta hai."

Veer thought of the last time he had stood before a fall he could not undo, then of sitting while someone brought help which he could not provide. Victory was not the only thing which failed to carry a person out of a place. Shame could keep him there too.

"Aapko pehle se pata tha?"

"Baat pata thi. Apne baare mein der se samajh aayi."

Dhruv shifted the tender hand to an easier position. The ring on its cord remained against his shirt. He did not lift it to make the answer more difficult to doubt.

He said that the akhada's teaching of return had existed before his own lessons. He had not invented it on the night he learned to take it personally. A person could repeat an old instruction for years while quietly assuming that its most necessary part applied to someone else.

Veer knew something of that.

"Ek raat main ek seekhne waale ko aasman samjha raha tha," Dhruv said. "Raasta mujhe saaf dikh raha tha. Maine samajh liya usse bhi dikh raha hoga."

"Phir?"

"Woh ruka. Maine suna. Dono waapas aaye. Koi badi durghatna nahi hui. Par meri baat utni poori nahi thi jitni mujhe lag rahi thi."

There was no dead person placed between them to give the lesson authority. No vanished student whose name Veer would now have to earn by enduring the rest of the story. There was a teacher who had failed to distinguish his own familiarity from the other person's knowledge.

Veer wanted to hear it.

He also noticed that the shoulder had begun asking him to move. The few minutes he had requested were becoming the time in which he would have to pretend not to notice.

"Baaki kal?" he asked.

"Kal jab theek ho."

Dhruv stood without calling the pause an interruption. He asked whether Veer needed the bag moved before he went. It was moved. Then the teacher left the room with his own hand protected, not with a disappointed look offered as the student's last task of the evening.

---

The nurse helped Veer settle the shoulder and checked the knee before the next rest.

He asked for the banana later, when he was comfortable enough to want it. The peel went into the bin with help; the fruit tasted slightly greener at one end. It was so stubbornly a banana that he smiled at himself for the feeling of relief.

Not every offering from a teacher had to turn into the instrument of a lesson.

He sent Rudra a goodnight message without forwarding Dhruv's unfinished account. The answer came before the phone was put away. Ira and Aadhya would have their own evenings; no one was called merely to make the four part of the same moment.

During the night, the knee needed a position changed. Veer reported the pull and let the person helping examine what had shifted. He did not try the old rising movement to see whether the conversation had made the body more ready for it.

He slept after the support was settled.

---

On day eighty-five, the first questions concerned the night.

The clinician wanted the actual account of the shoulder, ribs and knee. Veer gave it, including the discomfort during the visit and the position which had helped. The therapist considered what he could try with the aid, what the shoulder should not be asked to bear and where the short movement would end.

Veer had a private picture of that movement already: leaving the bed, making the distance neatly, turning without the awkward pause. It was almost funny to recognize the tidiness of it before anybody had begun.

He let the picture go far enough to hear the instructions.

The seat at the other end was placed before he rose. There was help beside him, not a test of whether he could make it unnecessary. His aid was where the permitted hand could find it. The shoulder remained out of work which belonged to the people assisting.

When he was asked whether he was ready, he checked the answer rather than the audience.

"Haan," he said. "Abhi."

The first part went as planned.

Veer rose with the help arranged, found the aid and made the short permitted distance without putting the shoulder into the work. At the turn, the knee began to complain before the rest of him was ready to call the movement finished.

He said so.

The therapist stopped him where the ground was clear, checked the position and helped him take the shorter turn towards the waiting seat. There was no fall. There was also no excuse to describe the knee as comfortable merely because he had remained upright.

He sat, let the leg settle and answered the next questions.

"Poora nahi hua," he said.

The therapist asked which part he meant. They had found where today's effort needed to stop, used the seat which had been placed for precisely that possibility and avoided making the shoulder pay for a longer route. That was part of the trial, not an event outside it.

Veer wanted to argue and could not find an honest version of the argument. The destination had been drawn differently in his head. That did not make the help at this one a failure.

The return to his room was assisted. The limits stayed in the plan. He rested before the next conversation.

---

Dhruv came only after Veer had eaten and had time to lie quietly.

The shoulder was settled, the knee supported. Veer wanted the account now, not a chance to reproduce it. Dhruv asked whether the time still worked and sat where the light did not require the boy to keep turning his head.

"Kahaan the aap?" Veer asked.

"Yahin ki zameen. Akhade ke paas. Kisi aur duniya ki baat nahi hai."

He described an earlier teaching night, without a date or a name to make it grand. A learner, a place already used for practice, the open ground beside the trees. Nothing was hunting them. The task had been ordinary for Dhruv, and he had mistaken ordinary for simple.

He could read the sky from that ground. He knew where the tree canopy broke, where the packed earth became softer and which turn brought a person back beside the practice space. He had walked it often enough that the turns had ceased to feel like information.

What he intended to teach was attention to a reference which did not move merely because the learner became uncertain.

The trouble was not that the reference failed.

---

He had taken the learner out from the practice ground and stopped where a star was clear between the upper branches.

No star was named. The name would not have helped a person who first needed to find what the teacher was looking at. Dhruv described the opening in the leaves, the bright point within it and the relation of that opening to the place where they were standing.

The other person answered that he understood.

Dhruv remembered being satisfied by the answer. The words came back in the right order. The head turned where he pointed. He had asked what was visible and heard a description close enough to his own that he allowed the difference to disappear inside it.

Then he changed position.

From the few steps beside him, the learner's opening in the canopy was not the same opening. A lower branch cut across it. The packed edge of the practice space was less obvious in the dark than Dhruv's familiar feet had made it.

Dhruv knew where both stood. The learner knew where Dhruv stood.

He had not asked whether those were the same kind of knowledge.

---

"Usne bola nahi?" Veer asked.

"Pehle wahi bola jo maine poochha."

"Aur jo aapne nahi poochha?"

Dhruv looked at him with a small, rueful pleasure. Not praise for guessing the hidden answer, but recognition that the question had found the actual difficulty.

"Uski baari der se aayi. Meri wajah se."

The admission did not make the whole night a record of cruelty. Veer could imagine an instruction given too confidently, a reply heard too generously, a student reluctant to turn an ordinary exercise into a declaration that he could not do it. None of those things required a villain. Together they could still make a person less safe.

"Aapko laga woh darr raha hai?"

"Baad mein usne kaha ki galat bolne se bach raha tha. Usse pehle main kaise jaan leta. Tab toh mujhe lag raha tha samajh gaya."

Veer heard the limit left inside the account. Dhruv could tell what he had thought; he could tell what the learner later said. He could not improve the past by pretending to have understood the other person's hesitation all along.

---

The old instruction had been to turn back towards the starting place.

Dhruv was close enough to help, far enough that the boy would have to choose the turn rather than merely follow his heels. It was not a journey into unknown ground. It was a little movement inside a place the teacher thought he knew completely.

He saw the learner turn the wrong way, hesitate and look up again.

The star was still there.

That was what had made Dhruv slow to understand. There was no cloud to blame, no sky closing over them, no broken instrument or failed power. The reference had remained clear from the place where he stood.

For a moment he almost repeated the instruction more loudly.

Instead, he heard the question the learner finally asked.

It was not about the star.

"Aap yahaan aake dikhaa sakte hain?"

Dhruv gave the remembered question in his own present voice. There was no attempt to make the learner's younger voice return to the room. He could remember the request without pretending that remembering preserved every sound of it.

The question had been plain.

Not a better star. Not the name of the thing above them. The person on the ground wanted the teacher to come to the place where the instruction had stopped being useful.

Dhruv went.

---

He crossed the few steps by the ground he knew, slowly enough that the other person did not have to move aside in a hurry to make room. He stood beside the learner and looked from there.

The branch which had barely interrupted his own view cut the opening into two. The bright point could still be found, but the dry, hard edge of the practice ground lay behind a darker patch which he had been treating as obvious. The learner could hear where he had stood before. That did not make the foot's next place visible.

"Yeh wala mod?" the learner asked.

Dhruv had pointed to a turn which was plain from the other side. From here, two small variations in the ground could answer the pointing hand. One led where he intended. The other ended against the spread of roots.

There was time to stand and understand the difference because the learner had stopped rather than guess a movement to keep the lesson looking successful.

Dhruv had nearly made that stop into evidence that more instruction was needed.

It was more looking which had been needed first.

---

"Aapne uske saath chalke waapas dikhaaya?" Veer asked.

"Haan. Apne pairon se. Koi ishaara karke zameen seedhi nahi kar di."

He said it without an impressive account of how little help had ultimately been required. They had named the change in the ground in ordinary words. They had made the turn together, stopped where its appearance changed from the other side and looked back from there too.

Nobody was asked to run the route to prove that fear had been removed.

At the practice ground they sat. The learner had been able to describe the missing part of the instruction once describing it no longer meant competing with the teacher's assurance that it was clear.

Dhruv had listened.

Then he ended the exercise for that night.

It would have been easy to ask for one neat return after the correction. The learner would probably have tried. Dhruv could no longer be certain what a successful-looking attempt would have concealed about the wish not to disappoint him.

They were both back. That was not the moment to make coming back insufficient.

---

Veer looked towards the doorway of the room.

He knew where the corridor ran. He had been helped along it, seen the turn, heard the trolley at the uneven join. If somebody now asked where the nearer seat was, he could point to it confidently.

He did not know what that same stretch looked like from Ira's rested eye, how much of it Aadhya could use without a protected hand reaching for support, which turn pulled at Rudra's ankle. He could not improve those limits by knowing the destination more firmly.

The thought did not require him to become frightened of helping anyone move. It required a question before he decided whose movement he was describing.

"Taara sahi tha," Dhruv said. "Meri jagah galat thi."

Veer heard the care inside the difference. The man was not claiming the sky had betrayed him, or that the student had failed to deserve an instruction which should have been enough. He had stood where knowing had become so familiar that he no longer noticed what he knew.

"Gussa aaya tha?" Veer asked.

"Pehle. Phir samajh aaya kis pe nikaalne jaa raha tha."

"Bola nahi?"

"Baat rok di. Baad mein kehna pada ki jo saaf bol raha tha, uska aadha uski taraf se dekha hi nahi."

That did not make him a teacher without pride. It made pride something which had not been allowed to finish the learner's sentence for him.

---

The staff member who came to check Veer's comfort did not find a pupil practising in the dark.

She found a man seated beside a supported patient, both talking quietly. Dhruv made room for the actual check. The story could wait through a question about the knee.

Veer reported that it had settled after the morning's shorter movement but was beginning to stiffen in this position. The support was adjusted. He asked whether there was time for the end of the account before the next rest and heard the answer before deciding he wanted it.

There was a little time. There was no need to fill all of it.

When the nurse had gone on, Veer looked at the teacher's hand resting without display.

"Aap us raat haare nahi," he said.

"Usne bhi nahi."

Veer had not realized that he had left the learner outside his sentence until Dhruv put him back inside it.

Veer asked whether walking back beside the learner had made it harder for him to try alone later.

The question had been waiting beneath the rest. If someone came every time a person stopped, perhaps stopping became a way of never finding out whether he could go on. He disliked the suspicion in that thought. Disliking it did not prevent him from having it.

Dhruv did not make him dress it in kinder words before answering.

"Us raat ki madad ko uski poori zindagi ka faisla kyun banaate?" he asked.

They had needed to get back safely. A later lesson could ask what the learner now knew, what still needed practice and what help remained necessary. It could not honestly begin by pretending that receiving help once had settled all those questions forever.

"Har baar saath chalna padta toh?"

"Toh dekhna padta kis kaam ke liye taiyaar hai. Akela bhejne se taiyaar ho jaana pakka nahi hota."

He was not promising that teaching could remove every risk. Veer had not asked for such a promise and would not have believed it. He was refusing to use risk as proof that the person exposed to it had finally earned trust.

That was a different refusal.

---

Dhruv said the old account could be told to the other three if they wanted to hear. He would rather answer their questions himself than have Veer preserve every remembered word while lying with a knee which needed another position.

Veer was relieved. It had not occurred to him how quickly a story could become another thing he was responsible for carrying correctly.

"Poochh loonga," he said.

He did not ask the teacher to remain until he had completed the asking. Dhruv had his own hand to attend to and his own rest. He left the room when the conversation had used the time they had agreed.

Veer lay quietly afterwards.

At first he tried to repeat the whole story in his head. The star, the opening in the branches, the turn, the question. He lost the exact order of two sentences and caught himself treating the loss as evidence that he had not listened well enough to deserve the answer.

Then he remembered the man coming to stand beside the learner.

He could ask again. That had been part of what the story was for.

---

Rudra's next message asked how the morning's movement had gone.

Veer replied with the actual result: shorter than he had imagined, knee objected at the turn, no fall, settled after help. He almost added that it was nothing to worry about. He could not know what another person should feel, and the details were more useful than the instruction.

The answer came back that Rudra's own day had stayed within the existing help and protection. No new walking award for being attentive to a teacher. He wanted to know whether the chair at the turn had been comfortable.

"Tere waali shaan nahi thi," Veer sent.

"Upgrade kara denge. Cushion par naam likhwaana hai?"

Veer smiled at the screen. The joke did not ask the boys to pretend the chairs were not needed. It made room for them to dislike a cushion without making the dislike a crisis of identity.

He said Dhruv was willing to tell an ordinary old teaching account later, if the others wanted to hear it. He did not send the story itself in pieces. Rudra wanted to listen when his care allowed.

Aadhya and Ira were asked separately through Sitara. Aadhya said yes, provided there was no practical exercise hidden behind it. Ira asked that any listening fit around the rest already planned for the ordinary eye. Sitara brought those answers, not a declaration that the group had become ready for the next phase of training.

The actual time would still have to be arranged.

---

In the late afternoon, Veer looked towards the corridor when somebody asked for help finding the right room.

His first impulse was to point before the question had finished. He knew the near turn. He had passed it often enough now to want a small use for that knowledge.

He waited.

The visitor was asking about a room on another part of the floor. The nurse gave the directions, noticed that the visitor had not understood the last turn and asked a passing colleague to show the way when she went there.

No teacher was performing. No student was watching for a lesson. A person with a bag and a worried face was allowed not to understand a corridor immediately.

Veer watched the two leave together.

He had not been made useless by failing to become the first person with an answer. He had simply not been the person who knew enough about that particular destination.

When the time came to change his own position, he used the call within reach instead of trying to demonstrate that he had learned the building by moving through it alone.

The aid remained beside him, ready for the next effort his body was actually allowed to make.

Evening put a narrow orange edge on the window frame.

Veer could see it without lifting his head far. Beyond it, the road was losing its separate colours. A horn sounded, stopped, sounded again with less conviction. Somebody outside the room was explaining that a parcel had arrived at the right building and the wrong entrance, as though the building had done this deliberately.

He listened until the explanation became laughter.

The day had not given him the distance he had pictured walking. It had given him a shorter turn, a seat which mattered, a teacher's mistake and the irritating discovery that these did not fit neatly into either progress or failure.

He had wanted a clearer answer from the knee.

The knee remained a knee. It would have to be examined again when the next effort was considered, not persuaded by the quality of the thoughts he had managed to have while resting it.

He asked for the light to be changed before it became uncomfortable to keep looking towards the window. Then he ate what had been brought, slowly enough to notice when he wanted a pause.

---

Later, after the next care, he found one point of light through the space above the lower frame.

He could not see the ground below the window from this position. The sill concealed it. He knew the road existed because he had seen it from elsewhere and heard it now; the small piece of sky supplied almost nothing about where a foot might safely go.

He lay with the two facts together.

He was not practising a star lesson. There was no attempt to hold a form, no command sent towards the light, no count which would turn looking into a permission. The aid remained beside the bed. His shoulder remained supported.

Still, he understood the old error more sharply from here. A thing could be plainly visible and not tell a person how to get where another person wanted him to go.

The light outside did not have to change for that to be true.

He thought of the learner asking the teacher to come closer. It was not a clever answer in a contest. It was an ordinary request which had interrupted the teacher's picture before the picture could become more important than the ground.

Veer had been praised often for knowing when to step forward.

He wanted to learn that other timing too.

---

Sitara brought a possible interval for the shared listening the next day, after the separate care, if the morning's reviews still allowed the move and the attention.

The room would be available for a short time. Ira could have the shaded place. Aadhya would not be asked to hold a book or repeat a hand movement. The boys would have their aids, supports and the ordinary assistance needed to reach their seats. No one was booking a night outside, reopening the akhada's exercises or proposing another passage crossing.

Veer said the arrangement sounded useful. His actual answer about attending could follow the review rather than be treated as a promise which the body must now honour.

Sitara accepted that and went to the next person who needed asking.

He noticed how much quieter an arrangement became when everyone did not have to make the same answer at the same moment.

---

Dhruv returned briefly before the last rest, after the care he had needed for his own hand.

He asked whether Veer had any question left from the account which needed clearing up now. Veer said he had understood what happened. The learner had asked, the teacher had gone to him, they had looked from the other side and walked back together. The part he was still thinking about came afterwards.

"Abhi nahi toh kal," he said.

"Poochh toh sakte ho. Lambi baat hui toh rok lenge."

Veer smiled at the wording. A request could be heard without automatically buying the rest of somebody's evening.

He asked whether Dhruv remembered being satisfied when the learner had reached the practice ground again.

"Hua tha," Dhruv said. "Phir samajh aaya ki sirf mujhe zaroorat padne pe saath chal dena kaafi nahi hoga."

Veer looked at him. The teacher had not withdrawn the value of helping. He was noticing that the next lesson could not depend on the learner discovering the same missing instruction and having to ask for it again.

---

The ring lay quiet against Dhruv's shirt. The tender hand was not raised to demonstrate a corrected way of pointing.

Veer had expected the account to end when both people returned. Now he saw why return was not merely an ending. A person brought something back: a fear admitted, a turn understood, a failure in the teacher's instruction which could be left unchanged if everybody was too relieved to look at it.

He had heard what Dhruv had done once he saw the difficulty. He had not heard what the man did before the next learner had to stop and tell him.

That was a different part of responsibility. It did not wait at the end of a route with a comforting voice. It changed the beginning.

Veer looked away from the little light beyond the window and asked the man beside the bed.

"Uske baad aapne apni agli class kaise shuru ki?"

### END OF CHAPTER 99
