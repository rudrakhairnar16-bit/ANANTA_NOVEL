# ANANTA — BOOK 05 — MERGED

# CHAPTER EIGHTY-ONE — THE NAME HE HAD KEPT

---

> *"I thought the new name would tell me which old memories to throw away.*
> *It did not."*
>
> *— Rudra's later notebook, first leaf of the family account*

---

"Pataal," Ram said.

The word reached Prayagraj without a change in the small hiss behind his voice. Rudra had expected something to happen to the line, to the light at his collar, to the whole ordinary arrangement by which his father could be far away and still speaking beside his ear.

The line stayed clear.

"Mera janm wahan hua tha. Mera Kul Asuran hai. Tummein jo mera hai, kavach use bhi chhupa raha tha."

Rudra looked towards Vedant. Not for a translation. To make sure somebody else had heard a sentence which could not be mistaken for another instruction about breathing.

The man remained in the chair beyond the nurse's working space.

"Aapka?" Rudra asked.

"Haan."

The word was worse in its simplicity than an account full of impossible things might have been. His father was not telling him about a shape which visited the body when it broke. He was telling him where that body had begun.

"Pataal ek Loka hai," Ram said. "Varanasi ke neeche ka koi kamra nahi. Main yahan paida nahi hua tha."

Rudra had spent years learning the limits of the courtyard. Now the man who had made him run them was saying that the courtyard had never contained the beginning of his father's life.

He kept looking at the phone.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini sat very still.

She had not moved the supported shoulder or reached across her husband's injured side. Her good hand rested against her knee. Dhruv had stopped at the chair; Dadi stood where she could see Ram without crowding the nurse.

The small gathering made no sound for the benefit of the boy at the other end. No one offered to explain Ram before he had finished speaking for himself.

"Mera ek aur naam hai," Ram said.

Rudra shut his eyes.

Of course there was. There would be a different name for the man who belonged to a place outside the world in which he had raised his son. For one awful instant Rudra wanted to refuse it, as if the refusal could keep at least the old voice intact.

Then he opened his eyes again.

"Boliye."

"Daavansh."

The nurse glanced at his face. Rudra answered her quiet check before asking the thing he could no longer keep behind it.

"Ram kaun hai?"

His father took time over the next breath.

"Main."

"Aur woh?"

"Woh bhi main."

---

Rudra could remember his father changing a door hinge.

Not a heroic memory. A screw which would not turn, the particular irritation with which Ram had asked him for a different tool and then found that the first one had been right after all. His mother laughing from the kitchen because the man had spent five minutes accusing a screw of bad character.

Daavansh did not fit into that memory.

Neither did he replace it. The voice on the phone was still the voice which had argued with the hinge and called Rudra before dawn and, much later, asked a nurse whether somebody had sent the next college page.

"Toh ghar mein—"

He stopped. The sentence was trying to become an accusation against every cup they had shared.

"Ghar mein jhooth tha?"

Ram did not answer quickly enough to make it feel rehearsed.

"Jo nahi bataya, woh chhupaya. Tumhara baap hona nahi. Tumhare saath rehna nahi."

Rudra heard the distinction. He was not ready to accept that his father could draw it wherever it hurt least.

"Mujhe farq kaise pata chalta?"

Across the two wards, nobody came to Ram's rescue.

"Nahi pata chal sakta tha," he said.

---

The nurse in Prayagraj touched the edge of the phone stand.

Rudra turned towards her. He was breathing faster, and she needed him to attend to the body which was listening as well as the words being heard. He let her check him, stayed within the support and did not make the question an excuse to sit up on the wrist.

At the other end, Ram's nurse spoke softly too.

The truth had reached two injured people. It had not made either of them able to hold an unlimited conversation.

"Baad mein baat karenge," Rukmini said.

Rudra felt the old anger lift its head. Later had always been the room in which they put the answers he was not supposed to touch.

His mother heard the silence after her words.

"Rukne ko bol rahi hoon. Bhoolne ko nahi."

He looked at the clock by the duty station. The hands had moved only a little since the call began.

"Phir phone hoga?"

"Team jab theek kahegi," she said. "Tumhari bhi. Unki bhi."

Ram spoke once more before the nurse helped end the call.

"Naam waapas nahi loonga, Rudra. Jo bola, wahi sach hai."

The line closed.

The phone stayed where it was, an ordinary dark rectangle, while the boy beside it tried to understand how his father could have come from somewhere else and still be the person whose silence hurt him most.

---

For a while Rudra wanted to hear the word again.

Not the whole call. Only Pataal, in the voice which had spent his childhood naming distances he was expected to cover. He wanted to find the instant at which the old voice became a stranger's, and could not.

The nurse finished the check. He told her the pressure had not suddenly become a different pain and answered the questions she needed. The dressing was attended as part of his care, not lifted to discover whether a new family name had changed the colours underneath.

It had not.

His wrist still lay in its brace. The supported ankle occupied the same inconvenient stretch of bed. He had imagined truth as something which might at least rearrange the pain into an order he could understand. His body had kept its own arrangements.

Vedant waited until the nurse had finished before speaking.

"Kuchh chahiye?"

Rudra almost asked for the mirror.

He knew what would be in it: his face, tired and badly slept, with nothing in the cheekbones which could tell him how to be the son of a place he had never seen.

"Abhi nahi."

---

The name on the patient's band was his ordinary name.

Rudra Vashisht.

He read it twice. The letters had been printed before the last part of the kavach failed, before Daavansh became a sound he knew. Neither the person who had printed them nor the boy wearing them had been trying to conceal a foreign birth. He had been born into this life, gone to its schools, learned where a particular step collected rain outside his college.

He could remember jumping that wet step with Veer and landing badly enough to lose a slipper.

Asuran, he thought, and the memory refused to become grander.

He had still gone back for the slipper.

---

"Kisi group mein mat bhejna," he said.

Vedant looked at him, then at the phone.

"Naam?"

"Jo unhone bataya. Sab. Abhi."

It was not a request to lie to the clinicians. Rudra made that part explicit. They needed the facts relevant to treating him, and he would answer them honestly. He did not want the account sent round the post as another version of the final, available for a stranger to improve while drinking tea.

Vedant put his own phone down.

"Tumse poochhe bina main kahaani nahi bhejoonga. Ilaaj ki baat team ko batayenge."

Rudra nodded.

The distinction felt small and fragile. The cover which had hidden his signature was gone; that did not mean the rest of his life had become public property.

If anyone beyond Earth could recognize him now, he could not choose what they noticed. Here, in this room, he could still decide whom to call by name.

---

He slept after the examination, more from exhaustion than a successful attempt to make peace with anything.

When he woke, the afternoon had moved along the curtain. Somebody had changed shifts. Vedant had handed over before resting his own forearm, and the nurse who answered the call button knew about the previous night without asking Rudra to perform it again.

He asked for the time and then whether there had been a message from home.

There had. His father was resting after care. His mother knew he was asleep. No one had withdrawn the words said in the morning or asked that the boy be told he had misunderstood.

Rudra let the nurse finish before looking away.

He had not known he was waiting for that particular betrayal until it failed to arrive.

---

His first anger had been almost clean: they knew, he did not, therefore every hurt belonged on the same side of the line.

Afternoon made it less convenient.

His father had concealed a birthplace. His father had also come to every ordinary place in Rudra's life carrying that concealment: the college gate, the chemist, the little repair shop where he would rather mend something twice than throw it away. Those memories did not prove him innocent. Their refusal to become false made the anger harder, not smaller.

Rudra did not want to be told that a frightened man could not help frightening a child.

He knew too much about being the child.

He did not want to discover that the man had never been frightened at all, either. That would make the years simpler in a way he no longer believed.

There were more things to ask than one tired voice could safely answer this afternoon.

For the first time, he could see that choosing the next question was not the same as surrendering the rest.

---

After his next review, the team allowed a short call if he still wanted one.

He asked for Veer.

The phone was set up without his wrist having to hold it. The post contact checked that Veer was awake and able to speak; another person stayed responsible for the work at the table.

Rudra watched the line connect. He could have sent a message with all the difficult nouns in it. Instead he waited for the familiar voice to say his name.

---

"Bol," Veer said.

He was on the supported bed at the post, with the phone propped where he could hear without making the slung shoulder take its weight. The volunteer had asked whether he wanted privacy and moved just far enough to give it without leaving him unable to call for help.

Rudra looked at the patient band once more.

"Papa ne bataya. Woh Pataal se hain."

Veer did not answer.

"Asuran Kul," Rudra added. "Unka naam Daavansh bhi hai."

He waited for something large enough to justify the silence.

What came was a quieter question.

"Unhone khud bola?"

"Haan. Abhi."

"Tu theek se sun paaya?"

Rudra told him where the conversation had taken place, who had been there and that the call had ended because both patients needed to stop. He was not describing a voice heard while his body shook in the dark. His father had said it, awake, on a clear line.

Veer let him reach the end of that account.

---

"Kuchh bol," Rudra said at last.

"Soch raha hoon."

"Itna?"

"Pehli baar dhang se kar raha hoon. Time lagega."

Rudra looked away from the phone. The old joke had arrived in the new room without asking what sort of blood it was allowed to speak to.

Veer heard the silence differently this time and did not fill it.

"Naam bada ajeeb lag raha hai," Rudra said.

"Unka?"

"Sab."

On his own bed, Veer moved the good fingers over the crease in his blanket. He had known the sound of Ram's training staff before he knew why an adult might be awake so early. He had known which bruises Rudra explained with a joke and which ones he would not let anybody see. A birthplace did not erase that knowledge. It also gave him no right to decide what his friend ought to do with it.

"Mujhe tu phone kar raha hai," he said. "Main tujhe hi sun raha hoon."

Rudra swallowed.

For a few seconds that was enough of an answer.

---

He told Veer about asking whether the house had been a lie.

The question sounded uglier outside his own head. He expected his friend to protect at least his mother from it, or point out that Ram had nearly died trying to get them out of the ghat.

Veer did neither.

"Jawaab diya?"

Rudra repeated the answer, carefully enough that it stayed his father's and did not become a kinder thing of his own making.

"Maan liya?"

"Pata nahi."

"Toh abhi pata nahi."

Rudra had never envied another person's certainty quite as much as he envied Veer's willingness not to counterfeit it.

"Mujhe lag raha hai main unse gussa bhi nahi ho sakta. Raat ko—"

He stopped before turning the night into a reason to surrender the years before it.

Veer waited.

"Ho sakta hoon," Rudra said, almost to himself.

"Haan. Baat bhi kar sakta hai. Dono."

It was not an instruction to forgive. The possibility of speaking without forgiving had simply not occurred to him with the same force.

---

At the table outside Veer's small space, somebody asked for the number of clean cups. The volunteer answered. Work continued beyond the edge of the conversation.

Rudra could hear it through the phone and felt an absurd relief that Pataal had not stopped a man from wanting tea in the proper number of cups.

"Kisi aur ko bataya?" Veer asked.

"Tujhe. Ward mein jo the unhone suna. Girls ko main bataaoonga."

"Theek. Main aage nahi bhej raha."

No oath followed. Veer did not make a private confidence into something whose protection required a ceremony between injured hands.

He asked whether Rudra wanted him to stay on the call or let him rest.

Rudra wanted both. He chose the part his body was asking for more clearly.

"Baad mein."

"Haan."

For once the word did not sound like a door being shut.

---

Veer left the phone propped up after the line ended.

The volunteer returned when called. Veer asked for the support to be checked before the next rest interval and answered honestly about the ribs. There was no request for a vehicle to take him to Rudra's bedside, no sudden new right to stand guard because he had learned a more frightening word.

"Sab theek?" the man asked.

Veer looked at him.

"Baat hui. Abhi utna."

The man accepted the limit and helped him settle.

In Prayagraj's monitored bay, Rudra had closed his eyes without trying to find a different face behind them. The name had crossed the distance to one friend. It had returned in the same friend's voice, without a verdict attached.

He could not yet decide how to speak to his father.

He no longer had to decide entirely alone.

---

In Varanasi, Ram tried to ask what Rudra had said after the call.

The question escaped before he could hear the impossible part of it. Rukmini had been in the same room as him. She had not travelled down the line to wait beside their son when it ended.

"Mujhe nahi pata," she said.

He looked at the phone, then away.

For years she had watched him turn ignorance into another task: go out, check a route, mend a joint, make the next morning begin early enough to outrun the thing he could not prevent. Here there was a hospital bed under him, a dressed injury at his chest and the small humiliating requirement to let another person bring water when he needed it.

The nurse asked whether he wanted some now.

He said yes.

Rukmini watched him drink after she had helped place the cup. The drinking took more of his attention than a healthy man would have thought possible. She waited until it was finished before speaking again.

"Usne suna. Poora suna, jitna tumne bola."

"Samjha?"

"Woh usse poochhna."

---

Ram had thought the hardest part would be saying the name.

The name had taken less effort than the question about the house.

He could remember Rudra following him round the courtyard with a tool too large for his small hand, asking why a door had to be hung before it could be shut properly. Ram had answered that question. He could remember the answer now, its needless length, the serious attention the child gave a practical explanation offered without a threat attached.

There had been other questions he had refused to hear in that tone.

He had made a habit of calling the refusal protection.

Rukmini did not have to guess where his thoughts had gone. She had lived in the house too.

"Ab usse tumhe samjhaane ka kaam mat dena," she said.

He looked at her.

"Jo chhupaya tha, tumne batana hai. Tumne kyun chhupaya, woh bhi. Usko tumhari taraf se achha jawaab dhoondh ke dena nahi hai."

The nurse adjusted the blanket clear of the dressed site. Ram waited through it instead of making pain the reason the conversation could not apply to him.

"Pata hai," he said afterwards.

Rukmini's good fingers moved over her own knee. The pulse at her wrist was visible for a moment, quick and ordinary. She put the hand flat until the trembling eased enough for her to lift the cup without spilling it.

Her husband had spoken. Their son was alive to hear. Neither fact made it easy to be the person who had known how long the words were waiting.

---

Dhruv returned from the passage with the current ward update.

Rudra had rested. He had asked to speak to Veer, and the short call had taken place with permission. No new deterioration had been reported in that account. Continued observation did not end because the family's conversation had changed subject.

Ram listened to the report without treating it as an answer to whether his son had forgiven him.

"Agli baar woh jab poochhe," he said, then stopped.

Rukmini let the sentence remain incomplete.

He began again.

"Main tayyar rahoonga. Jitna bol paaun, sach boloonga."

Dhruv put the phone where the nurse could reach it. He had his own arrangements to keep outside the room, and they named who would be present while he went rather than leaving his absence to surprise anyone.

Dadi brought the folded cloth for Rukmini's shoulder position. She placed it where it could be used without pulling the arm higher.

"Jawaab ki rehearsal mat karo dono," she said.

Rukmini looked up.

"Nahi toh uske sawaal ki jagah hi nahi bachegi."

For the first time that day, Ram's mouth changed a little without trying to become a reassuring smile.

He had been caught doing exactly that.

---

Towards evening, Rudra asked whether the clean shirt could be used.

The nurse checked what could be changed safely and helped him with another member of staff, keeping the wrist supported and leaving the collar dressing accessible. The front fastening let the cloth move without dragging his arm through a narrow sleeve. He did the small part she asked him to do, then stopped when the rest needed help.

The old shirt was folded away. Nothing else had been removed with it.

The colour at the collar stayed faint. No joined edge had returned to make the change of clothes feel like a new layer of protection.

Rudra asked for the page stand to remain lowered afterwards. He was not trying to use up the brief interval of feeling cleaner by proving he could study through it.

Vedant had returned after his handover and rest. He noticed the shirt, asked whether the movement had been comfortable and accepted the answer without turning it into a measure of obedience.

"Ghar pe message?" he asked.

Rudra considered sending only the small fact about the shirt. His mother would want that fact. It would also have made a good place to hide the questions he still had for his father.

"Baat karni hai," he said.

Vedant asked the team, then helped arrange the next short call after both patients' care allowed it.

The phone was not handed to Rudra as a reward for having behaved well that afternoon. He had asked to speak. For the moment, his body could manage a little more, and the people helping him made room for it.

---

This time Rudra asked for his father first.

Ram answered from the same ward, his voice rested but not suddenly strong. Rukmini was there. The nurse had placed the phone where neither of them had to hold it; in Prayagraj, Rudra's own team had kept the call within reach of help.

He told them that the shirt had been changed. His mother asked whether it had hurt at the wrist. He answered the actual question, and she let the little domestic report end without making it a substitute for the next one.

"Papa. Ram naam jhooth nahi tha, aapne bola."

"Haan."

"Par main kaise alag karoon? Kis baat pe bharosa karoon?"

Ram moved his good hand against the blanket. He could have listed things he had done for the boy. Every one of them would have been true. Every one would have sounded like a price being quoted for the right not to be questioned.

He left the hand where it was.

"Aaj jo bola, usse shuru kar sakte hain. Tum poochho. Main jo jaanta hoon, bataaoonga. Jahan nahi jaanta, nahi jaanta boloonga."

Rudra listened to the difference between that and the instructions with which their mornings had begun.

For once, his father had not chosen the first movement.

---

"Main aapka hi beta hoon?"

The question came out before he could decide whether it was stupid.

Ram's answer was immediate.

"Haan."

"Waisa beta. Sirf—"

He did not finish. He could hear how much the unfinished sentence might wound someone whose life was different from his own. That was not the thing he was trying to ask. He needed to know whether the man had described a bloodline which belonged to their bodies or a duty he had decided to pass on.

Ram understood.

"Mera janm jis Kul mein hua, woh tummein bhi hai. Main tumhara pita hoon, Rudra. Is hisse mein koi doosri kahaani nahi."

Rudra looked at the patient's band again, then stopped doing it.

The answer did not settle the other questions. It removed one fear he had not wanted to admit: that he might lose the right to be angry with this particular father because a revelation would take the father away first.

"Mujhe abhi bahut gussa hai," he said.

"Suna maine."

"Sirf aaj ki baat pe nahi."

Ram shut his eyes for the space of a breath.

"Woh bhi samajh raha hoon."

---

A wheel rattled in the Varanasi passage.

Rudra heard it through the speaker and remembered the stretcher wheels he had followed after Manikarnika, the hand which would not close round his fingers, the unreasonable conviction that if he could make the man open his eyes he would never again ask anything difficult of him.

He had meant that bargain while he was making it.

His father had lived long enough for it to become impossible to keep.

Being grateful for that life and angry at what it had done to his childhood occupied the same chest. He could no more choose one feeling to make the other false than he could choose which old injury deserved attention because a larger thing had happened through his skin.

"Aapko laga tha main nahi sambhaal paaunga?" he asked.

"Mujhe darr tha."

"Mujhe bhi tha. Mujhe naam bhi nahi pata tha kis cheez ka."

The line held the silence which followed. Rukmini did not reach across it to soften either voice.

"Tumhara darr maine kam nahi kiya," Ram said at last.

It was not the complete apology Rudra did not yet know whether he wanted. It was the first answer that evening which did not make the older man's fear the most important thing in the room.

He let it stay.

---

The nurse asked how tired he was.

He answered honestly. They paused, let him settle, and kept the next part brief. In Varanasi the phone moved a little as Ram's nurse checked that he could continue too. Neither son nor father tried to buy more words by saying the body was fine when it was not.

Rudra had a list in his head: what could hear him now, what the other colour meant, what his father had recognized before the others did. A list was not the same as knowing which question to ask a person rather than an instrument.

He thought of the hinge.

Of a man who could explain a screw for five minutes and leave the beginning of his own life unspoken for years.

"Aap yahan kaise aaye?"

Ram did not answer about combat.

"Bahut chhota tha."

Rudra waited.

"Main khud chal ke nahi aaya tha."

---

All afternoon he had been trying to put his father into another landscape without changing the man inside it.

He had supplied the familiar upright body, the measured feet, hands which knew what to take apart. Even his anger had imagined a man choosing a door, stepping through it and deciding to keep what lay on the other side from his son.

His father took that picture away with the next words.

"Main godh ka bachcha tha, Rudra. Varanasi ki zameen se nikla tha. Mujhe wahaan se uthaya gaya."

The nurse's hands moved quietly beside Rudra's bed. The floor beneath it remained the ordinary floor of a monitored ward.

Somewhere else in the same country, his father lay with the wound he still could not heal for himself, describing a time before he could even stand.

Rudra could no longer fit the account into a warrior's arrival. There was a child in it now, unable to choose a road or explain the place he had come from.

Someone had found that child.

"Kisne?" he asked.

# CHAPTER EIGHTY-TWO — WHERE THE GROUND OPENED

---

> *"The first hand to lift him did not ask him to prove whose child he was.*
> *We learned to ask that question later, and called ourselves wiser."*
>
> *— a later private account, copied beside Ram's words about Varanasi*

---

"Vedic Kul ke logon ne," Ram said.

Rudra kept his gaze on the phone.

He had asked who found the child. His father had answered with another family, not a hidden name for the one he had already given.

"Unhone uthaya. Unhone paala."

The breathing support made a small sound beside Ram. The nurse waited where she could hear him without entering the conversation. He used the next breath to make the distinction plain.

"Janm Asuran Kul mein hua. Bachpan yahaan, unke beech beeta."

Rudra had seen his father tend wounds. He had watched him fail to heal the chest which now needed ordinary care. The word Vedic reached those memories before it reached anything he thought he knew about inherited power.

"Aapko yaad hai?" he asked.

"Uthaya jaana? Nahi."

For the first time since morning, the answer contained a limit which did not feel like a door being deliberately closed.

"Main godh ka bachcha tha. Zameen se nikalne ki baat mujhe bataayi gayi. Meri apni yaad mein tab tak ghar hai. Log hain. Main unke paas hoon."

No memory of an infant watching a road between worlds. No long account of a battle he could not have understood. The beginning had reached him through people old enough to have lifted him, not through a voice which woke inside his son tonight.

Rudra let the difference become real before asking anything else.

---

"Aapko apna maana?"

The question was meant to be practical. Food, a place to sleep, someone who knew whether the little body was getting ill.

It escaped with a child's need in it.

Ram heard that too.

"Haan."

"Asuran the, phir bhi?"

"Bachcha tha," Ram said. "Pehle woh dekha unhone."

In Varanasi, Rukmini looked at the man beside her. The name he had kept back was not the only part of his childhood she could hear returning to his voice. There was a different softness in the way he said the word for a child, a softness which had not always survived into the mornings he gave their own son.

She did not correct the memory on Rudra's behalf. She stayed long enough for Ram to be heard without being excused by it.

The nurse gave the small sign that they needed to stop.

Ram saw it.

"Aage bataaoonga," he said. "Jitna mera bachpan hai, woh main bata sakta hoon. Jo mujhe baad mein pata chala, usse alag."

Rudra said he understood the limit.

This time he did not have to pretend the answer had been nothing.

---

They ended the call and kept the rest the team had asked them to take.

The night in Prayagraj was staffed, interrupted by the necessary checks, not by another attempt to call the form or test whether a father could name a broken seal whole again. Rudra slept in uneven intervals. When he woke frightened, the nurse answered. He did not have to wait for Vedant's injured arm to become the only thing between him and the room.

By morning, a full night had passed since the family names were spoken, and more than a day since the kavach itself had gone.

Day thirty-eight began with the same supports and a fresh clinical review.

The chest discomfort had eased further. The wrist and ankle remained injuries with their own instructions. Continued observation and the care plan stayed in place; no travel permission arrived attached to a quieter night.

Rudra answered what hurt now, listened to what he could do and asked when another short call might be possible. The team allowed it after the review and rest, with help available and no obligation to keep talking if either patient tired.

He wanted to hear about the house which had existed in his father's memory before Rudra's own house began.

---

In Varanasi, Ram had woken before the next call without asking to be lifted out of bed.

His old habit offered its usual answer to a difficult morning: stand, do something with the hands, make what was wrong into a piece of work which would obey enough attention.

He looked at the dressed site by his chest and let the habit pass without mistaking it for permission.

Rukmini came after her own morning care, the shoulder supported. Dadi helped settle the cloth before the call was put where they could hear.

"Kahan se shuru karoon?" Ram asked when Rudra answered.

The boy looked at the curtain, at its lower edge which stopped just above the ordinary floor.

"Jahan se aapko yaad hai."

It was a small request. For Ram, who had spent so many years choosing the portion of his past which could be useful as a warning, it was almost a different way of being asked to speak.

---

The first place Ram could remember had a low shelf he could not reach.

Not the whole house. Only a few things had kept that early clearness: the shelf, an earthen pot with a dark damp ring beneath it, the drag of somebody's cloth at his cheek when he was lifted out of the way.

He could not honestly put them in order. The shelf might have belonged to a later room than the one in which he first learned to answer his name. Childhood had kept sensations more faithfully than dates.

"Paani maangta tha," he said into the phone. "Khud nahi le paata tha."

Rudra listened.

His father, who had made another person's dependence so difficult for so many years, could remember a life which began by asking to have a cup brought down.

---

The pot was cool even when the air in the room was not.

Ram remembered standing with both hands round the small cup, drinking too quickly and being told to slow down. He remembered spilling some across his shirt and then insisting he had not, though the wet place was travelling towards his stomach in full view.

A woman laughed. She was one of the people who raised him, and the laughter had the familiar impatience of someone who would have to change the shirt she had already changed once that morning.

"Paani ne kiya hoga," she said. "Tu toh bilkul sookha hai."

He looked down at the dark cloth, betrayed by the evidence on his own body.

She put out her hand. He gave her the cup.

The memory contained no great kindness which the district would have known how to announce. She had simply fetched another shirt, complained about the washing and let the child stay close while she finished what she was doing.

He had belonged to her day's work without having to justify the space he took in it.

---

There was another memory of broken clay.

He was bigger then, old enough to be sent across the room with something breakable and too impatient to understand that this was a kind of trust. He could not now recall whether he had caught his foot or turned too quickly. He remembered the sound, his empty hands and the wonderful hope that if he stayed very still the pieces might not quite be his fault.

The man who came to the doorway looked at the floor first.

"Hato. Nange pair mat."

Ram moved. Only after the sharp pieces were out of reach did the scolding begin.

He had wanted the scolding finished quickly. At some point he asked whether he was to go.

"Kahan?"

He could not answer. He knew only the old fact which had been told about him: he had been found. Something found might be put elsewhere if it proved too much trouble to keep.

The man looked at the little face above the broken pot.

"Yahin rehna hai. Isi liye sambhaalna seekh."

Then he gave him the small ordinary part of cleaning which would not cut his feet.

The child disliked that part almost as much as the fear which had come before it. He was hungry, ashamed and sure somebody else could have done the work faster.

He also remembered being called for food afterwards.

There had been no decision about whether he still belonged by the time the meal was served.

---

On the phone, Ram paused after telling Rudra about the pot.

Rukmini kept her eyes on him, not on a version of the past in which every person who had raised him must have been perfect. They had been a household. People got cross. Work went wrong. A child made himself impossible at precisely the moment someone needed him quiet.

"Unhe pareshan bhi karta tha," Ram said.

"Aap?" Rudra asked.

The surprise was so unguarded that his father heard the small opening inside it.

"Haan. Main bhi bachcha tha."

It ought not to have been new information. Rudra had never been given much with which to imagine it.

---

As he grew, the rooms became places where other people's pain arrived.

A member of the Vedic Kul could be family at breakfast and a person somebody urgently needed by afternoon. Cloth had to be clean. Water had to be carried. In that household, an adult would find a small task he could do without making the patient's hurt into his lesson.

He was sent for things before he was trusted to help with them.

Sometimes he disliked it. The person in pain took the attention he wanted, or the meal was late, or an adult sent him away just when he thought he was old enough to stay.

There were evenings when he was given the part no one would sing about: washing, fetching, waiting until the hand doing the work could be freed to take the cup.

Those evenings had not changed the place of his birth.

They had made another belonging real, one ordinary repetition at a time.

In Prayagraj, Rudra watched a nurse carrying clean things past the opening of his bay. He had come to the call wanting a map of another world. His father was telling him about the people who had first made this one large enough to keep him.

---

"Isliye aap zakhm—"

Rudra stopped before making the question into an accusation against the injury still keeping his father in bed.

Ram completed it for him.

"Vedic Kul se seekha. Jinhone paala, unhi se."

He was not describing another name for the combat force with which he had fought at the ghat. Rudra had watched those hands try two different kinds of work, and fail at more than one of them. Until now he had treated every thing his father could do as something a father simply arrived knowing.

"Asuran hone se nahi?"

"Nahi. Janm ka Kul alag. Yeh unke beech seekha."

Rudra looked at his own fingers.

He had not meant to ask the next question. It was there anyway, in the memory of an injured workman's arm and a grip which had been meant to stop a weapon, not hurt the person holding it.

"Mujhmein?"

Ram heard what the little word was trying to reach.

"Woh tumhein mujhse viraasat mein nahi mila."

The boy kept looking at the fingers.

The name Asuran had not caused them to become stronger overnight. Hearing Vedic did not give them a way to repair what strength had damaged either.

---

For one breath Rudra wanted to argue.

It seemed unjust that the man could have learned a way of helping and passed down the part which so easily broke things. The thought was childish, and painful enough that recognizing its childishness did not make it disappear.

Ram did not tell him to hide it.

"Maine jo seekha, woh sab tumhare khoon mein aa jaata, aisa nahi hua," he said.

"Toh aapko dekh ke—"

"Tumhare zakhm ko dekhna hoga. Tumhe kya ho raha hai, woh. Mera naam bataane se ilaaj tay nahi ho jaayega."

Rudra looked towards the nurse by the opening. She was helping another member of staff with something beyond his bed. She had not waited for Daavansh to become a word she knew before deciding that his ankle padding needed moving.

The old fantasies came back, stripped of their useful disguises: if only the force could be held differently, if only a better count could put back what a frightened hand had done.

He left his hand where it was.

This answer was about a life his father had lived, not a new exercise left for the son to attempt alone.

---

"Unhone aapko doosra bana diya?" he asked after a while.

The question surprised Ram.

"Kaise?"

"Asuran nahi rahe? Jab unke saath—"

"Asuran raha. Unka bhi raha."

The answer travelled the line without requiring either family to become a lesser one.

Rudra had heard enough of people using a Kul to tell somebody where to stand, whose road to take or which body they owed their support to. His father had just described something which would not fit into that demand: a child with one birth and a real place in another household.

"Dono sach?"

"Haan."

He did not call the word a solution to every question about bloodlines. It was enough that it was true of the life being discussed.

In Varanasi, Rukmini watched Ram lean back into his support when the nurse asked. The man who had been raised by healers still needed to let another person's judgment matter at his own bed.

---

Rudra wanted to ask why none of this had been said at the kitchen table.

The question was still there. It had not grown smaller because the story contained kindness.

He asked it.

Ram did not say that a child's ordinary happiness had required ignorance of the people who had made his father's happiness possible.

"Darr ko ghar se bahar rakhna chaaha," he said. "Bahut kuchh saath hi bahar rakh diya."

"Darr toh andar tha."

"Haan."

The small agreement was difficult to hear. Rudra had expected an argument he could push against. His father had left him with a true thing which did not tell him what to do with the hurt.

"Main abhi sab theek nahi bol sakta," he said.

"Maanga nahi."

Rudra listened closely to that answer before accepting it for the moment.

---

When the call ended for rest, he did not ask for another one at once.

The morning had given him more than a strange name. A place of birth, an account of being found, the beginning of remembered childhood and the source of a healing his own body had not inherited. His father had told him what he remembered and where memory stopped. For this morning, Rudra could listen without making the gaps disappear.

He told the clinician the family-history information which might matter and kept the uncertainties clear. The care plan was reviewed on what the team could actually assess; it did not become an invitation to demonstrate either inheritance.

Later, when the nurse helped move the blanket clear of the supported leg, Rudra thanked her before she had to ask what still hurt.

A person could be unable to heal and still answer when somebody needed to know where the pain was.

---

After the afternoon rest and check, the team allowed another short call. Rudra asked to speak to the girls.

The ward contacted Sitara first. She checked the girls' care interval and asked whether they wanted the call now. They did. A helper set the phone where Aadhya did not have to grip it and Ira could listen without using the ordinary eye for a long screen interval.

Rudra heard the two familiar voices before attempting the nouns.

He told them that his father had spoken, that he came from Pataal and had been born into the Asuran Kul. That Daavansh and Ram were the same person. He gave the rest slowly: an infant found in Varanasi, raised among the Vedic Kul, a childhood which belonged to Earth even though the birth did not.

Neither girl interrupted to make the story easier for him to finish.

"Isliye nahi bataaya tha kal," he said afterwards. "Khud samajh nahi aa raha tha."

"Ab aa raha hai?" Aadhya asked.

"Thoda. Phir nahi."

"Theek. Humein bhi time lagega."

She gave him the honest answer without making him comfort her for needing it.

---

There was a scrape of cloth near Aadhya's end of the line.

She asked the helper to move the support, waited while it was settled and came back without pretending the interruption had not happened. Her left hand still needed the prescribed care; the right fingertips had not recovered because the conversation was important.

"Bol," she said.

Rudra had spent the pause deciding whether the story ought to have made him more impressive. Hearing that one ordinary word undid the effort.

He told them about the broken pot.

A child who thought he might be sent away, then disliked being made to help clean the safe pieces because he was hungry. A boy who spilled water on his shirt and denied it while the stain spread.

"Unhone khud bataaya?" Aadhya asked.

"Haan."

She was quiet for a while.

"Aise kabhi socha nahi unko."

"Maine bhi nahi."

They let that be something they had discovered together rather than a reason to rush towards a kinder judgment of every morning which had come afterwards.

---

Ira had listened with the covering still in place.

"Unki yaad aur jo unhe bataaya gaya, alag rakha?" she asked.

Rudra told her how Ram had answered about being lifted from the ground. No memory claimed. A finding told to him, followed by a childhood he could speak about himself.

"Achha," she said.

The word was not a certificate that an ancient history had now been proved by a phone call. It was relief at hearing somebody resist a perfect story where a truthful one had edges.

Rudra knew the difference well enough not to ask her to make it more flattering.

"Likhegi?"

"Tu chahe toh chhota sa. Tere liye. Bhejna kisi ko nahi."

He thought about it. Then he said yes to a few plain lines, the names and the distinction about childhood memory, with no drawing of the signature and no claim to a route.

The helper took the short dictation rather than making Ira strain the eye to write through a tiring interval. The note stayed private. A spoken account had acquired a careful reminder, not a new ancestral document which could answer more than its speaker had said.

---

"Vedic unka Kul nahi tha?" Aadhya asked.

"Janm ka nahi. Unke log the."

Ira's face turned towards the speaker.

"Toh unka tha bhi."

Rudra thought of the way his father had said both answers were true.

"Haan."

She did not need to supply a missing file about herself to understand that much. Sarokaar had become real through people who knew her and work she had chosen to do. Its reality did not depend on pretending every question about birth had been answered.

For a moment she wanted to say all of that.

Then she heard the tiredness in Rudra's voice and kept only the part which belonged in this conversation.

"Dono ko rakhne ki jagah ho sakti hai. Ek ko jhooth bolna zaroori nahi."

Rudra looked at the phone as if there might be a little more space around it than before.

"Mujhe abhi Ram naam sunna achha lagta hai," he admitted. "Pata nahi iska matlab—"

"Wahi naam suna hai tune saari zindagi," Aadhya said. "Usko chhod ke sunna shuru karna zaroori hai kya?"

He had not been given a new order. He had been released from one he had made for himself.

---

When he tired, he said so.

They ended before the call became something he had to complete for their benefit. Sitara kept the next clinical contact where the helper could find it and let the girls rest too.

The private note remained with the few facts actually spoken. No old dream had been recruited to authenticate Pataal; no absent childhood paper had suddenly provided the same answer for Ira.

In Prayagraj, Rudra asked whether the nurse could put the phone back beside the charger. He left the college page lowered and the hand open on the sheet.

He had told the story to people who mattered without being required to know all of it first.

That evening there would be another question for his father. For the moment, there was a little room in which the answers already given could stop sounding like separate alarms.

---

Rukmini heard about the private note during the evening call.

Rudra told her before the account could grow a second life in somebody else's version. He had asked for the girls to be told. A few names and limits had been written for him, not sent to a group or turned into a public claim.

"Tumne chaaha tha?" she asked.

"Haan."

"Toh theek."

She did not ask to read it before he could decide whether it said what he meant.

The phone remained supported. Ram had finished the care which needed quiet before joining the short call; the nurse was close enough to hear if speaking became too much. In Prayagraj, Rudra had rested since the afternoon. The shirt sat comfortably round the accessible dressing, and the wrist and ankle remained where the team had put them.

The day had been quieter than the night of the shatter.

It had not restored the thing which shattered. At the collar, the faint gold was still part of the exposed signature, not a border joining round it.

---

Rudra told his father what the girls had heard.

He wanted to make sure he had not joined the pieces wrongly while trying to say them aloud. Born in one Kul. Found as an infant in Varanasi. Raised by the Vedic people. Childhood memories of Earth, the finding itself told to him.

Ram confirmed the account without adding an impressive detail to reward being listened to.

"Unke beech rehke bhi Daavansh the?" Rudra asked.

"Haan. Ram bhi tha."

The boy looked towards the edge of the curtain. He could remember thinking that a name ought to sort the memories into true and false. Instead he had been given more people whose lives would not fit into that division.

"Unhe bura nahi lagta tha? Aapka doosra naam?"

Ram considered the question before answering.

"Mera wahaan janm hua tha. Unke ghar rehne se woh baat badli nahi. Mujhe unka hone ke liye apne janm ko jhooth bolna nahi pada."

He did not turn one household's life into a promise about every person who would hear the name now.

Rudra did not ask him to.

---

In Varanasi, the blanket had slipped a little at Ram's good side.

He began to pull it back, stopped when it tugged at a sore place and let the nurse help. The phone caught the little ordinary exchange. Rudra heard his father say thank you before coming back to the question.

For some reason that made it easier to speak.

"Mujhe abhi lagta hai main kisi ka naam galat bol doonga. Kisi ek ko chhota kar doonga."

"Kiska?"

"Jo aapke the. Dono taraf."

There was a pause at the far end.

Ram had prepared himself for questions about strength, the world below, the things which might be able to recognize a signature no longer hidden. He had not prepared for his son worrying that remembering one family might wrong the other.

"Mujhe paalne walon ko kam mat samajhna," he said.

"Nahi samajh raha."

"Aur jahan se main aaya, unko bhi sirf ek naam mat rehne dena."

Rudra heard the change. Until then, Asuran had been a difficult word about himself. His father had just used it to leave room for other people.

He waited, without bringing a pen or a diagram into that room.

---

The remembered household had become easier to imagine.

A cold pot. A shirt. Somebody cross enough to scold a child and careful enough to move the sharp pieces first. He could hear how a life was made there, not by a single rescue which demanded gratitude forever but by being kept through days when the rescued child was inconvenient.

The beginning of that life still contained a missing part.

A child had arrived from somewhere.

Not a warrior with a declared reason for exile. A body too small to choose whether to remain in one world or appear in another, to keep one name or answer to a new one.

"Jo pehle the," Rudra said. "Aapko janm dene waale."

Ram kept his eyes on the phone.

"Unhone aapko—"

The word left him unwillingly.

"Chhod diya tha?"

---

The nurse at Ram's bed settled the blanket and stepped back.

He could hear the young voice waiting in another ward. Yesterday that voice had asked whether he was still the same father. Today it had found a way to ask about the people who existed before he could become one.

He did not have their lifting hands in his own memory. He would not invent the touch now because the boy needed something gentle with which to imagine them.

He knew what the separation had been for.

Rukmini stayed beside him. The good hand on her lap did not move towards the phone to finish his sentence or make the answer less painful in advance.

"Nahi," Ram said.

In Prayagraj, Rudra watched the screen, though there was no picture on it which could supply the missing faces.

"Unhone mujhe pheka nahi tha," his father said. "Unhone mujhe bheja tha."

The distinction was a door opening onto a darkness quite different from abandonment.

Rudra waited to learn what a parent had been trying to send a child away from.

# CHAPTER EIGHTY-THREE — NOT A WAR

> *They had been born into the side someone wanted gone."*

---

"Mere maa-baap mar rahe the," Ram said. "Jab unhone mujhe bheja."

Rudra stayed very still.

The blanket beneath the supported leg had a small uneven fold in it. A moment earlier he had been thinking of asking the nurse to smooth it. Now he could see the fold without remembering how to make the small request.

His father had not finished.

"Asuran Kul ko maara jaa raha tha. Sirf ladne waalon ko nahi. Parivaar. Bachche."

He stopped for breath. No one in the two wards spoke over the pause.

"Wahaan rehta toh mujhe bhi maar dete. Isliye bheja."

The explanation reached Rudra in two parts: a parent trying to keep a child alive, and the reason a child needed to be taken out of an entire world to have that chance.

"Ladaai mein?" he asked.

"Sirf ladaai nahi thi. Kul ko mitaaya jaa raha tha."

A massacre.

The word had a size which the voice giving the account did not try to imitate.

---

Rudra looked towards the opening of the bay.

Somebody passed with a jug. Beyond them a woman was asking a question about visiting time, an ordinary question about how long a person could remain beside somebody she loved. The answer came back softly enough that he could not make out its exact words.

His father was saying that there had been people for whom staying beside a child would have ended the child's life too.

He wanted a picture. The want was immediate and terrible: a hand, a room, the moment somebody decided that letting go would have to count as protecting.

Ram gave him none.

"Mujhe woh pal yaad nahi," he said when Rudra asked. "Main unki aakhri baat tumhe sunaane ka daava nahi karoonga."

"Par aapko pata hai?"

"Jo baad mein jaana, usmein yeh hai: woh mar rahe the. Mujhe zinda nikalna tha. Unhone bheja."

He made no claim that the finding in Varanasi explained the route between one place and another. Nor did he turn a later account of his own survival into a perfect memory of the hands which sent him.

The limit did not make the people less real.

Rudra had no faces for them. He had the fact of what they had tried to do.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini heard the silence change at the far end of the line.

She had heard her son angry, frightened, sarcastic and too tired to finish a sentence. This quiet was not quite any of those. He was trying to find room for people whose absence had already shaped the man who raised him.

She waited until the nurse had checked Ram's breathing before asking whether Rudra was still there.

"Haan."

"Kuchh chahiye?"

He looked again at the little fold beneath the ankle.

For an irrational moment, saying that something so small was uncomfortable felt like asking the room to forget the dead.

Then the nurse beside him asked what he was looking at.

He told her. She moved the fold and waited for his answer.

The pain eased at that one place.

His grandparents did not become less dead because a living person had been allowed to say where the bed hurt.

---

"Unhe chhod ke aana—"

Rudra stopped.

He had almost asked his father what it had been like to choose, forgetting the infant who had arrived in the last part of the story.

Ram understood the unfinished question.

"Maine chun ke nahi chhoda tha."

The boy nodded before remembering the phone could not carry that movement.

"Haan."

He said it again, more clearly, and the older man heard it.

A child sent away. A child found. A man who would one day stand over another child at dawn, determined that helplessness should never happen again.

Rudra could see the connection beginning to form.

He was not willing to let it answer everything.

---

They ended the call when the teams asked them to stop.

Rudra could still hear the distinction after the phone went dark: his father's birth people had been deliberately killed, his dying parents had sent their infant out to live, and the household which raised him had not been the reason the first one disappeared.

That night Rudra slept in broken intervals without asking the inner hush to supply an inherited scene. There was no remembered battlefield waiting behind his eyes. Only the word massacre, the ordinary cup at his bedside and the uncomfortable knowledge that a story could change a family without changing a single injury on the people hearing it.

The staffed night went on.

When the nurse woke him for the next check, he answered and let her finish. The question he wanted to ask his father in the morning stayed with him after he closed his eyes again.

---

Day thirty-nine began with the work of remaining alive already under way.

The night team handed over. Rudra answered the morning clinician's questions, let the dressing and supports be checked and listened to the continuing plan. The chest discomfort had eased in the quieter intervals. He was still under observation; his wrist and ankle had not been granted different permissions by the history he now knew.

He did not hide how badly he had slept.

When a short family call was allowed after rest, he asked for it. In Varanasi, Ram had finished his own care before the phone was placed near him. His voice held more of itself than it had on the first morning, but the nurse remained within reach.

Rudra did not begin by asking for another name.

"Aapko paalne waale," he said. "Unhone aapko daraaya nahi tha. Ki galti ki toh apne nahi rahoge."

Ram listened.

"Aapne bataaya. Bartan toota tha."

"Haan."

"Mujhe toh har baar lagta tha ki agar theek nahi kiya, toh main aapka beta hone laayak nahi hoon."

The words reached their destination without a raised voice.

---

Ram looked at the empty hand on his blanket.

He could have told the boy he had never meant that. It would have been true and, by itself, almost useless. The boy was describing the meaning he had lived with, not asking the man to certify which meaning he had intended.

"Maine tumhe kabhi apna beta hona kamaane ko nahi kaha," he began.

He heard himself before Rudra had to answer.

"Par aisa laga tumhe," he said. "Mere kiye se."

Rudra stared at the phone.

His anger had arrived ready to push against a denial. The absence of one left him feeling abruptly younger, not less hurt.

"Aapko pata bhi tha?"

"Tum mujhse darte the, pata tha. Main usko bahut baar discipline samajhta raha."

The nurse checked Ram's position. He let her help before speaking again.

"Mujhe laga, jisse main darr raha hoon, uske saamne tum bach jaao. Mere saamne tumhara darr badhta gaya. Maine use roka nahi."

Rudra looked down at the brace. There was no transformation waiting in the answer, no great release which would move a damaged joint back to an earlier day. The truth entered a body which would still have to heal at its own pace.

He kept listening.

---

"Jo seekha, usse jaan bachi," he said.

The admission hurt. He had used his father's lessons at the ghat. Refusing the fact would make him argue with things his own hands had done.

"Mujhe pata hai. Par har subah ka darr bhi tha. Woh kya karoon?"

Ram's face changed at the far end, where Rudra could not see it.

Rukmini could.

She did not tell their son that survival was the answer to every question about the price of reaching it. She had been in the house for those mornings, heard a child's crying and her husband's terrible certainty that enough preparation could leave no room for helplessness.

Ram said, "Mera darr sach tha. Har chot zaroori nahi thi."

Neither ward fell silent for the sentence. A request was answered beyond the curtain. Someone at the Varanasi duty station began speaking to another relative.

Rudra had imagined that an admission like that would come with a change he could feel everywhere.

It came with the same tired wrist and a father who had finally said a thing the child should not have had to prove.

---

He wanted to ask why it had taken nineteen years.

He also wanted, with an anger he could barely bear, to become small enough for the man to try again without being able to forget what had already happened. There was no way to make those wishes occupy a real morning.

"Ab bolne se—"

"Peechhe ka nahi badlega," Ram said.

He did not ask the boy to call it enough.

"Toh aage ki baat mein mujhe chup mat karna," Rudra said. "Sawaal poochhoon toh use taiyaari ki kami mat bolna."

"Nahi boloonga."

The promise was small enough to be tested by the next conversation. It did not claim to repair the whole childhood in advance.

Rudra breathed out.

He could ask again if the man broke it. That possibility mattered almost as much as the promise itself.

---

Rukmini spoke when her son asked whether she had heard.

"Haan."

He had not asked her to choose a side for him. He needed somebody who had lived in the same house to stop the words from becoming another private thing which could later be misunderstood.

"Maine suna," she said again. "Tumne jo kaha. Unhone jo kaha."

She did not offer the family's love as an eraser. Nor did she turn herself into a witness untouched by the years being discussed. Her good fingers had tightened against her knee; she loosened them before reaching for water.

Rudra heard the small movement through the speaker.

"Aap bhi rest karo," he said.

It was not a surrender. A son could want his mother helped without putting the question back where it had been hidden.

They ended when the nurse asked him to rest. Ram repeated that he would answer the next questions when both could speak again.

Rudra did not thank him for giving permission.

He lay back into the support and let the morning be difficult without calling the difficulty a failure to understand his father well enough.

---

Afterwards Rudra wanted a face for the people who had died.

He tried to put an ordinary pair of hands into the account, then caught himself choosing his father's hands because they were the only ones he had. He knew nothing about whether his grandfather had held a tool like that. His grandmother had become, for a moment, the shape of a woman he had seen lifting another person's child through a crowded doorway.

He let the borrowed picture go.

It was painful to have people and not memories. Making their faces convenient would not bring them nearer.

Vedant sat where Rudra could speak without raising his voice. His forearm was supported, the pencil put away. He had not come to ask how well the boy had taken the morning's conversation.

"Kuchh yaad nahi hai unka," Rudra said.

Vedant waited.

"Kaise hoga. Paida hone se pehle—"

He stopped. Of course there could be no memory to retrieve. The loss was not a problem which a better inward look would solve.

"Phir bhi bura lag raha hai."

"Lag sakta hai."

The small permission embarrassed him more than a lesson might have done. He had been waiting for grief to prove itself by producing something only a grandson could know.

There was no such thing to offer.

He did not have to invent one.

---

Later, after rest, he asked whether a short call to Veer could be arranged.

The post confirmed that his friend was awake and able to speak. The phone was supported at both ends; the people keeping the work moving did not become an audience because the line connected.

Rudra gave him the things he had not yet heard.

The infant found in Varanasi. Vedic people raising him. Healing learned among those people, not inherited by Rudra. The parents who sent the child while they were dying, the Asuran families being killed.

Veer listened without announcing that the father he had known must therefore have become a different man.

"Abhi kya chahiye?" he asked.

Rudra looked at the open space by his bed.

"Pata nahi."

"Phone pe rehna hai thodi der?"

"Haan."

So they stayed, within the little time the team had allowed. There was a sound from the post's passage, a cup set down and the scrape of somebody moving a chair. Neither friend pretended that the quiet was empty because it contained nothing clever enough to say.

---

"Mujhe gussa aa raha hai," Rudra said eventually.

"Kispe?"

That was the difficulty. His father for what had happened in the courtyard. People in another history for what they had done before he existed. Himself, absurdly, for receiving a life through an escape he had never been asked to survive.

He tried to put those angers into separate words.

Veer did not choose the most useful one and discard the rest.

"Unke marne ka karza nahi hai tu," he said when Rudra stopped.

The sentence hurt in a place the story had only just made.

"Unhone bachaane ko bheja tha. Tu paida bhi nahi hua tha. Ab unka hisaab chukaane ko khud ko tod dega toh—"

He stopped before making another person's dead into an instruction either.

"Main kya bolna chahiye, woh nahi jaanta," he said more quietly. "Bas tera jeena sazaa jaisa mat bana."

Rudra kept his eyes on the phone.

The world had already found so many ways to make a family supply a body for somebody else's need. He had learned enough of that through the disputes over work, roads and compulsory watch. He did not want to bring the same demand inside himself and call it ancestry.

"Pehle poochhoonga," he said.

"Kya?"

"Jo hua. Kisne kiya. Kyun. Seedha."

Veer did not offer to guess a culprit for him.

"Haan."

---

They ended before either body had to become an argument for keeping the other one company.

The volunteer returned to Veer's bed when called. He helped with the position of the knee, heard the actual answer about the ribs and left the next drink where it could be reached without lifting the injured shoulder. Veer said he did not need another call made on his behalf just now.

In the monitored bay, Rudra listened to the next ordinary request outside the curtain and thought about the word enemy.

It would have been easy to make it large enough for anybody he already disliked to fit inside. A man who had blocked a road. A stranger who had demanded labour. The people who had worked the apparatus at the ghat.

None of those acts was made smaller by refusing a false connection. None was proof of who had killed his father's people.

He would not make a nearest face into the right one merely because grief wanted somewhere to look.

---

When Vedant asked whether he wanted the later family call requested, Rudra said yes.

Then he asked for water within what the team had allowed, let the nurse help and rested before the next part of the day. There was no old memory trying to speak through the colours at his collar. The colours did not tell him which question he was entitled to ask.

He would have to ask it himself.

---

In Varanasi, Ram found that he could remember the sound of a cupboard closing more clearly than the word he wanted for his birth mother's face.

The cupboard belonged to the house in which he grew. Its lower edge scraped until somebody lifted it by a particular fraction. That small inconvenience had survived in memory with ridiculous exactness. He could hear the scrape now, the impatient hand which knew how to correct it, the sound of life going on without asking to become history.

The parents who sent him away had no such ordinary sound he could honestly give his son.

He knew more than an infant could know. He had learned what happened to his people later. Knowing did not produce the remembered voices which grief kept asking for.

Rukmini sat beside him while the nurse finished helping him settle after care. She waited until the movement was over before putting the water within reach.

"Usko chehre chahiye honge," Ram said.

"Shayad."

"Mere paas yaad nahi hai."

She looked at him.

"Toh wahi bolna."

It was not permission to make the parents smaller. It was permission to let the absence be real instead of filling it with a better story.

---

He could have furnished his son with an account full of fire and impossible strength.

There was enough violence in what happened without inventing a single blow. He knew what a bloodline being destroyed meant; he did not need to pretend his own baby eyes had understood it. If he made the dead large only through their capacity to fight, he would have to explain away the little bodies which had not yet learned how.

Rudra had asked about a child.

He had asked about people who loved one.

Ram's first instinct had still been to reach for a warning useful enough to keep the next child alive.

He understood why he did it. The understanding had become less comfortable since morning. A reason could remain true and fail to carry the weight of being right about everything which followed.

Rukmini watched his good hand begin to gather the blanket into its old tight grip.

"Dard badh raha hai?" she asked.

He let the cloth go before answering.

"Thoda."

The nurse was told. The response belonged to the care plan and the reassessment, not to whether Ram could summon enough control to deserve relief. He accepted the help without trying to turn his own chest into an example for a boy in another city.

For a while they said nothing about Pataal.

Ram asked whether Rukmini had eaten. She had, and she told him what there had been, refusing the old small lie by which a person who had not eaten would say yes to keep one more worry out of a room.

She asked whether the new position was easier.

He gave her a proper answer too.

It was a strange kind of intimacy after the family history: not knowing everything about a person, but ceasing for a moment to make their concern work against a prepared account.

Rukmini moved the folded support at her own shoulder with help. She could not remain in the same position indefinitely simply because their son had begun hearing the truth.

"Thodi der aaraam karoongi," she said.

Ram nodded.

She did not ask whether he would be able to manage without her as if there were no nursing team, no other people whose care had kept him alive. Dadi would help her settle in the nearby room. Dhruv had confirmed the period he could stay before his own rest, and the receiving contact knew whom to call if anything changed.

No one had to become tireless to prove the family had stopped hiding.

Before leaving, Rukmini looked at the phone.

"Main bhi usse baat karoongi," she said. "Ghar ki. Jo maine dekha. Jahan mujhe aur karna chahiye tha."

Ram turned towards her.

"Meri taraf se nahi," she added.

"Pata hai."

She did not say more for him to rehearse. When her own conversation came, it would belong to her and the son who had lived those mornings, not to a joint statement prepared beside another hospital bed.

She went with Dadi when the help was ready, shoulder protected, her good hand taking only the thing it could carry comfortably.

Ram watched the doorway after they had gone.

At Manikarnika, he had asked where his wife was while barely able to take air. Now she had been able to leave a room to rest because leaving did not have to mean losing her. He let himself notice the ordinary difference.

It did not make the older losses small.

It gave the living a little space the losses had been using.

In Prayagraj, Rudra woke from the afternoon rest to a bay in which the light had moved but the care had not vanished.

He answered the next check and asked about the later call. The receiving contact confirmed that his father would speak after care if he still could. His mother was resting and knew he was awake; she did not have to be recalled to prove that she was present in his life.

Vedant had rested his own arm during a handover. The nurse adjusted the phone stand without asking Rudra to hold it.

The raw colours at the collar remained faintly distinct. There was still no joined gold contour, no returned wall in which he could hide the question until it became somebody else's responsibility to ask.

Rudra waited until the line was ready.

He knew what he wanted to know next.

---

"Main sunna chahta hoon," Rudra said when Ram answered.

"Haan."

"Unke baare mein. Sirf isliye nahi ki mujhe kis se ladna hai. Woh log kaun the. Unke saath kya hua."

His father let the request reach him before choosing the next part of the account.

Rukmini was resting. She knew the call had been arranged and had not been brought back to the chair to make the room complete. Ram's nurse helped with the phone; in Prayagraj the stand kept it where Rudra did not have to grip it. The two wards remained separate places with their own work.

"Asuran Kul ke logon ki taaqat badh rahi thi," Ram said. "Unke Swaroop. Jinke paas hukm dene ki taaqat thi, unhe us badhti taaqat se darr tha."

He gave the reason without offering it as a reason good enough.

"Isliye?" Rudra asked.

"Jo woh ban sakte the, usse khatra maana gaya."

Rudra looked at the little part of his own hand visible beyond the brace. His father was telling him why a child might be marked before it could hold anything at all.

He had heard strength spoken of as a prize for so long that the idea of it appearing in an accusation took a moment to become clear.

---

"Bachche bhi?"

Ram did not borrow a clean answer from the fact that his son was asking it.

"Kul ko khatam karna tha. Sirf kisi ek ko haraana nahi."

The difference was not a scale larger than the battles Rudra knew. It was a different intention.

At the tournament, people could lose and go home. At the checkpoints, a person could be made to pay for a claim he had never agreed to. Here the claim had been carried into birth itself, made wide enough to include someone who had no act to answer for.

"Paida hona jurm kaise hua?" he asked.

Ram waited until he could speak clearly.

"Nahi tha."

Rudra had not needed the man to explain that part. He needed to hear him refuse to leave any doubt in it.

"Tumhara bhi nahi hai," Ram added.

The boy looked away from the hand.

For two days he had been trying to decide whether the name explained something wrong which had always been hidden in him. His father had spoken of an inherited power and a people. Neither thing was a confession of a crime committed by the child receiving it.

---

There was anger now which no longer needed to look for a mistake in the pronunciation of Pataal.

It did not lift him from the bed. It did not teach the fingers how to hold, make a new gold edge or put a voice behind the silence of the lost kavach. It was simply colder than the confusion with which he had begun asking, able to remain in the room without searching for something nearby to break.

He could be the grandson of people who had died and the son of people who had hurt him. He could love the people who raised his father without turning that childhood into evidence that every lesson passed on afterwards had been kind. None of those truths required him to find one verdict large enough to erase all the others.

He had wanted the wall's breaking to produce an answer about himself.

It had produced people.

He did not yet know how to live with all of them.

He would not make their existence another thing he was forbidden to ask about.

---

"Jitna bataaya, main usse aage apne se nahi jodoonga," he said.

Ram listened.

"Jo humein yahaan mile, jo rasta rok rahe the, jo ghat pe the. Unka apna kiya hai. Aapki baat se sab ek hi nahi ho jaayenge."

"Sirf isse kuchh saabit nahi hota," his father said.

The answer did not acquit anybody. It kept the dead from being turned into a convenient explanation for every living enemy.

"Aur jo nahi pata?" Rudra asked.

"Jahan nahi pata hoga, wahi boloonga."

"Aapne bola tha."

"Haan."

The promise from the morning had survived its first return without becoming an argument about respect.

Rudra rested his head back against the support. He told the nurse how tired he was when she asked and listened when she said the call would have to end soon. At the other end, Ram let his own nurse check him too.

There was room for one more question, not an unlimited history. Rudra did not waste the room by reassuring either parent that everything had become easy.

---

A child had been sent out of Pataal while the parents who sent him were dying.

Vedic hands had lifted that child in Varanasi and kept him through the ordinary days in which belonging became a life. The child had grown, learned to mend and to hurt, become Ram and remained Daavansh. He had raised a son under a cover which could no longer hide what the son carried.

Rudra had enough of the story now to stop mistaking its beginning for a fable about his own strength.

There were people in it who had not been allowed to grow up.

There were people who had decided that should happen.

He kept his gaze on the phone, his wrist supported, his foot where the nurse had left it. He did not need to stand before he could ask what came next.

"Papa," he said. "Yeh hukm kisne diya tha?"

The line stayed open.

"Lokas ki satta sambhaalne waalon ne," Ram answered. "Jo insaani Kulon ke upar the. Unka faisla tha. Unhone hi Asuran Kul ko mitaaya."

"Nahi. Yeh unse upar ki satta ka kiya tha."

The answer did not supply one convenient face. It supplied responsibility, placed somewhere Rudra had not understood responsibility could reach.

At the far end, Ram waited through the next breath before continuing.

"Asuranon ki badhti taaqat se khatra tha. Unhone use rokne ke liye logon ko khatam karna chuna."

Chuna.

A choice, then. Not an illness which had moved through Pataal. Not the earth giving way beneath a house. Somebody who could choose had looked at the people inside and decided that their future was the thing to destroy.

"Toh unke liye sahi tha?" Rudra asked.

Ram heard the older argument behind it. Good and bad, right and wrong: words he had given a child with so much certainty that the child had sometimes been left wondering whether pain was proof that the lesson was being done correctly.

"Unhone kiya," he said. "Isse sahi nahi ho gaya."

The sentence was short. He did not dress the dead in a philosophy which could make killing them sound more difficult to condemn.

Rudra's good fingers had begun pressing into the blanket. He noticed the effort and let them ease before it reached the wrist inside the brace.

"Aap chahte hain main unse—"

"Abhi tumhara ilaaj ho raha hai."

The old anger flashed: another question turned into an instruction.

"Main poochh raha hoon aap kya chahte hain."

Ram stopped.

The promise from the morning was no longer an easy thing said after an admission. His son had put a hand against it and found whether it would hold.

"Main tumhe badla lene ka kaam nahi de raha," he said. "Yeh tumhara poochha hua sach hai. Hukum nahi."

Rudra listened until the two things separated.

He could ask about the dead without agreeing to become the weapon they had supposedly been waiting for.

The nurse beside him said they needed to end the call.

This time it did not feel as though the question had been carried out of the room unfinished so that a new day could disguise it. Ram had answered who bore responsibility. Rudra would still want to know more about those beings and what authority could possibly mean after an act like that. The remaining questions did not make the answer already given disappear.

"Kal aur baat karenge," his father said, then corrected the certainty. "Jab dono taraf bolne denge."

"Haan."

"Thak gaye ho?"

"Haan."

The ordinary answer took effort. He gave it anyway.

In Varanasi, Ram told his nurse he needed to stop too. Neither man had to keep breathing into the phone to prove which one could bear more of the truth.

The call ended. Their separate care continued.

Rudra slept badly at first.

In the dark between checks, he could hear the word above trying to become a direction. He would catch himself looking at the ceiling as though the plaster marked a border between the people being judged and the people who had never been made to answer.

He knew better than that. Pataal was not a room under Varanasi. Whatever governed another Loka was not waiting on the next floor of this hospital.

Still, the mind wanted a simple shape when the world became difficult. Above and below. Pure and dangerous. A family high enough to give an order; a child low enough to be included in it.

He lay still until the nurse came for the next check and asked for help with the position of the blanket. The ceiling returned to being plaster, stained near one corner, separating this bay from the work of people who would also be tired before the shift ended.

He could leave the impossible distance unmeasured tonight.

---

Day forty arrived through ordinary sounds: the handover, a wheeled stand being moved, somebody apologizing for knocking a cup against the bedside table.

Rudra answered the review honestly. He had slept in pieces. The pressure was less than on the night the kavach broke; the wrist and ankle still needed their supports, and the team had not cleared him to load them. Observation continued. There was no journey hidden inside the word better.

After the check and a period of rest, Vedant asked whom he wanted contacted first.

"Ma ko," Rudra said.

He had heard his father speak about the house.

His mother had said she would speak for herself.

Rukmini took the call after breakfast and her own prescribed care, with the shoulder supported and the phone placed so she did not have to keep one arm raised. Ram was not beside her. Dadi waited within reach, far enough from the speaker to give them the privacy they had asked for.

Her son said, "Aapne kal bola tha. Ghar ki baat."

"Haan."

He heard a small adjustment at her end and waited for it to finish.

"Main tumhe rote dekhti thi," she said. "Subah. Aangan mein."

There was no new place in the sentence. No secret country. Only the doorway whose position he could still have found with his eyes closed.

"Maine kai baar roka. Kai baar tumhare paas aayi. Par har baar nahi roka. Jo nahi rok paayi, use sirf tumhare papa ka faisla bolke apne se alag nahi karoongi."

Rudra shut his eyes.

He had been prepared for her to say that his father had been frightened. She had begun with the child she had seen.

---

"Aapko bhi lagta tha zaroori hai?"

Rukmini looked at the window, where the light had reached the lower frame.

"Mujhe tumhari jaan ka darr tha," she said. "Aur bahut baar us darr ke aage maine apni baat chhoti kar di."

She could have put the fear into an older language, given it the distance of extraordinary dangers. Her son would still have been left with the mornings when the kitchen smelled of breakfast and the stones under his feet had hurt.

"Tumhara dard chhota nahi tha," she said.

He opened his eyes again.

A child's crying had once seemed to him an embarrassing piece of evidence, something he needed to outgrow before he could be believed about how hard the training had been. Hearing his mother say she had seen it made the old shame move without quite leaving.

"Main kamzor tha?"

"Tum bachche the."

The answer came immediately.

Not a promise that he had secretly been the strongest thing in the courtyard. Not a claim that he had never needed to learn. A child could need teaching without having to deserve the right to cry.

---

For a while neither knew what to do with the quiet.

Rudra had always been able to ask his mother for something to eat. He had not known how to ask whether she remembered the person who had sat at the table with split skin and tried to act as though hunger was the only thing wrong.

Now she had answered a question he had never managed to make complete.

He wanted to comfort her. The wish came automatically when her voice thinned, a small domestic reflex as old as fetching the glass before she could reach for it.

He did not tell her she had done nothing wrong.

"Aapki awaaz theek nahi lag rahi," he said instead.

"Rona aa raha hai. Saans theek hai."

The precision nearly undid him.

She was letting him care about her without making that care disprove what he had said.

"Paani paas hai?"

"Haan. Abhi liya."

They waited. A nurse passed at Rudra's end and checked that the phone was not making him turn against the support. He let her move it nearer. The small interruption gave both of them time without requiring either to pretend the conversation had become easy.

---

"Tumhare papa tumse pyaar karte hain," Rukmini said later.

Rudra's face tightened.

"Mujhe pata hai."

She heard what he had been expecting after the sentence and did not give it to him.

"Uske baad lekin nahi bol rahi," she said. "Pyaar tha. Jo chot pahunchi, woh bhi thi. Tumse dono mein se ek chunne ko nahi keh rahi."

He looked at the strip of daylight on his blanket.

All his life, the demand had felt like a choice even when nobody stated it: either understand the father or speak for the child. If he did one honestly enough, the other would have to become a mistake.

His mother had not made the old mornings harmless.

She had stopped asking love to perform that work.

"Mujhe abhi maaf karna nahi aata," he said.

"Abhi maang bhi nahi rahi."

She would have to live with what he did not yet know how to give. So would he. Neither could finish the years in the small allowance of one call.

For years, that survival had looked to him like something borrowed in a place where it could be withdrawn without warning. A meal eaten, a gate shut, a boy asleep in another room: he had loved those things and listened through them for the sound which might end them.

"Aaj har baat khatam mat karna," she said.

For a moment he thought she meant the history.

Then she touched the edge of the water tray with her good fingers, making sure it sat where the nurse had put it.

She meant the strength with which he would try to tell it.

"Jitna bol sakoon," he said.

"Jitna bolne dein."

He gave her a tired look, and this time the small smile belonged to both of them.

By evening, Rudra no longer wanted to look at the ceiling.

He asked for the phone to be set where it had been in the morning and waited while the nurse made sure he was comfortable. Vedant had returned after rest, his arm supported, but did not sit in the place where Rudra's eyes would have to pass over him with every question.

"Unke paas itni taaqat thi," Rudra said. "Phir bhi darr tha?"

"Haan."

"Toh main kitna bhi seekh loon, kisi ko phir bhi khatra lag sakta hoon."

Ram heard the boy find a terrible way to become responsible for somebody else's choice.

"Lag sakta hai," he said. "Usse tum galat nahi ho jaaoge."

The answer was less comforting than a promise that enough discipline would make him safe. It was also the first one Rudra could imagine remaining true after he left this bed.

---

"Phir main kya badal sakta hoon?"

Ram looked down at the hand which had once corrected the position of a child's foot with no patience for how much the correction hurt.

"Apna kiya," he said. "Kisi ko tumse khatra lagta hai, isliye tumhe us jaisa hona zaroori nahi. Aur darr lagta hai, isliye mere har kiye ko tum sahi mat maan lena."

Rudra stayed quiet.

He had not expected the admission to travel into an answer about power. His father had usually described a world hard enough to make hardness sound like the only sensible preparation for it.

There were still hard things in the world. The dead in Pataal had not been saved by someone refusing to become cruel in imitation of their killers.

Yet the man telling the story had a son who could ask a question. He had been given that much of a future. What he did with it could not be determined entirely by the people who had tried to end his own.

"Mujhe achha nahi lag raha aapse yeh poochhna," Rudra said.

"Poochho."

The word arrived without again behind it.

---

"Aapne kabhi socha, agar main kabhi ladna hi nahi chaahoon?"

The question had come larger than he intended. He was not promising never to fight. He could remember Veer falling and the people at the ghat, could not imagine standing safely aside while those things happened again.

He was asking whether the possibility belonged to him at all.

Ram took time.

"Maine tumhare bachne ke baare mein socha," he said. "Bahut. Tum kaise jeena chaahoge, uske baare mein utna nahi poochha."

Rudra looked towards the curtain opening. Nobody was listening there on behalf of a lineage, waiting to hear what the boy would offer.

"Ab poochh sakte hain."

"Haan."

The exchange did not settle what he would choose. It changed who might be allowed into the choosing.

There had been afternoons when going to the ghats instead of straight home had felt like a rebellion serious enough to require an explanation. He had not known how to ask for a life larger than that detour. Now the question had reached his father without either of them turning it into a test of how much he loved the family.

---

He asked whether his father thought somebody was coming to this hospital.

It was the practical fear beneath the ceiling, the one he had been putting off because the old history was easier to give a shape than an unknown person walking through the duty station.

"Abhi kaun kya jaanta hai, main uska daava nahi karoonga," Ram said. "Jitna pakka pata hoga, tumse chhupaane ko jawaab nahi banaaoonga."

He gave no invented assurance that every danger had ended, no certainty about a person or an arrival for which he had no evidence. Rudra would still tell the team about anything new he felt or saw. The people caring for him would not be made to work around a silence his family called protection.

The nurse checked his fatigue. He told her he could manage a little longer, and she allowed only a little.

"Aap bade ho gaye the," Rudra said. "Yahaan reh rahe the. Phir bhi aisa laga ki kabhi nahi ruk sakte?"

Ram's answer was quiet.

"Khatra sirf bachpan mein nahi tha. Baad mein bhi saamna hua. Main jeeta bhi."

---

For years, Rudra had imagined his father's victories as the places where fear must have ended. A man who could do what Ram did should surely have found a day on which enough became enough.

"Phir?"

"2020 mein bhi ek ladaai jeeti thi," Ram said. "Uske baad bhi ghar aake darr khatam nahi hua."

Rudra lay still.

That year belonged to his own memory. Not the arrival of an infant he could only imagine. He had already been training for years; he said so, and Ram agreed at once.

"Pehle se darr tha. Us saal aur badha."

Rudra could hear the distinction without yet knowing the event inside it.

His father had gone out into a world the boy knew only in pieces, fought somebody and come home. The same father had stood in the same courtyard afterwards, and Rudra had understood his silence as another judgement on whether the morning's work had been good enough.

He looked at the phone.

"2020 mein kya hua tha, Papa?"

# CHAPTER EIGHTY-FIVE — THE VICTORY HE BROUGHT HOME

---

> *"He came home from the fight alive.*
> *For years, I thought that meant he had left the danger outside."*
>
> *— Rudra's later notes on the year 2020*

---

"Adhira se lada tha," Ram said. "2020 mein. Maine haraaya."

His son listened to the name without finding a place for it in his own memory.

"Aap jeet gaye the?"

"Haan. Us ladaai mein."

Ram gave him the outcome plainly. He had not run from that fight or mistaken escape for victory. He also did not promise that winning one encounter had made every future danger knowable.

The nurse reminded him that the call had reached its limit. In Prayagraj, Rudra's team was saying the same thing. He had the first answer; the rest would have to wait until they could speak again after the night.

"Kal poochhoonga," Rudra said.

"Poochhna."

They ended. The two phones were put down in separate cities.

While the nurse helped him settle, Ram found the old ground rising into memory, clear in some places and missing at the edges. Not an infant's borrowed history now. He had been there. The body remembering the fight was the body which had fought it.

---

In 2020, the stone under his left heel had moved.

Only a little. Enough.

Adhira's next blow reached the place where Ram's head had been, struck the edge of the opening behind him and sent grit against his cheek. He had turned instead of trying to set the foot again. His shoulder hit the wall. The force took the air from him in a short, humiliating sound.

No time to draw it back properly.

He ducked under the reaching arm, drove forward and felt his first strike stopped before it could reach the body. The impact ran through his knuckles to the elbow. He let the arm bend rather than hold it straight and lose the joint.

Adhira pushed him off.

Ram slid across the broken stone, found ground and moved before the next strike arrived. Red-black force tightened along his forearm. He met the blow at an angle; a section of the low brick edge behind him cracked where the impact went through.

Dust rose between them.

He did not wait for it to clear.

---

The next few moments had never returned to him in a neat sequence.

A heel beside a narrow drainage cut. His own palm scraping something sharp. The horrible certainty that he had not moved quite far enough, followed by pain along the side which proved it. He remembered those pieces better than he remembered the words either of them might have said.

He would not improve the memory for a listener.

Adhira pressed him towards the opening again. The broken paving gave him little room on one side. The low wall gave him less on the other. He could move back into the place where his foot had already failed or try to go through the attack before it was complete.

He went forward.

His first blow was caught. The second made room for his knee. He struck, felt the opposing weight change and drove with the planted leg while he still had a place to put it.

They hit the low wall together.

Brick scraped his back. The breath he had been trying to save was gone again. A hand closed against his upper arm, found leverage and began to turn him into the broken edge.

Ram did not hold himself rigid against it.

He went with the turn, ugly and too close, and drove his free forearm between them.

---

For a moment he had a strip of space no wider than his own wrist.

He used it to breathe.

That was all. No speech. No view of the whole struggle from somewhere above himself. Air, pain and the next hand coming for his throat.

He caught the arm before it closed, shifted his weight and made Adhira bear the effort of holding him against the wall. The brick under his heel ground loose. He had one chance to change the position before the ground changed it for him.

He let his knee give.

The grip followed him down. Not far enough.

Ram drove up beneath it, shoulder and hip together, red-black force burning close to skin. Adhira struck the remaining wall hard enough to loosen a second run of brick. Ram tore himself clear before the fragments came down.

His foot landed badly.

Pain flashed up from the ankle. He moved anyway, not well, only soon enough that the next impact passed across his raised arm instead of his jaw.

He was still standing.

He had not won yet.

---

Adhira came through the dust before Ram could put the injured foot down properly.

He met the first strike, failed to stop the second and took it across the ribs. Pain cut his next breath short. The body wanted to fold around the hurt; he let it turn with the impact and kept the turn moving.

A fist passed close enough to brush his ear.

He struck underneath it.

Adhira gave ground, one hard step. Ram followed, then stopped himself. The broken paving narrowed towards the opening. His opponent had more room to turn there than he did, and his foot could no longer correct a bad decision as quickly as it had a few moments earlier.

He stayed where the floor was wider.

Adhira's head lifted.

Ram could see blood at the edge of the mouth now, could see the effort it took to pull air through it. The sight did not make the next blow less dangerous. It made the distance between an impossible enemy and a body terribly small.

Both of them were breathing.

Either could still stop the other doing it.

---

The next attack was slower only at the beginning.

Ram saw the change in the shoulder and moved to meet it. The force came from the other side. He had answered the invitation instead of the strike.

It hit him high in the chest.

For a moment the ground vanished beneath his feet. The remaining wall struck his back, then the floor caught him on one hip. Loose grit went into his mouth. He tasted dirt and blood together.

He rolled before he knew whether the ankle would take his weight.

Stone cracked where his head had been.

He got a knee underneath himself. Adhira reached him before he could rise, seized the cloth at his shoulder and began hauling him up into the next blow.

Ram did not try to get upright.

He drove his weight down through the planted knee, turned across the grip and struck at the leg which was bearing Adhira's advance. The grip dragged him sideways. His shirt tore. He heard the cloth go with an absurd clarity, a cheap domestic sound in the middle of the struggle.

He would remember that sound for years.

---

Adhira's footing broke.

Not the leg. The position.

Ram went after it before the balance could be recovered. The injured ankle protested when he drove forward, but he had already put his shoulder beneath the opposing arm. He could no longer turn away without offering his neck.

He kept moving.

One impact against the low edge. A second against the ground. Red-black force shuddered through his arms, close and violent, making the loose fragments jump. He held the position until the hand pushing against him lost its line.

Then he changed the hold.

No graceful finish remained in the memory. His own forearm was slippery. Adhira was still trying to move. Ram used the uninjured leg, pinned the shoulder and struck hard enough to stop the effort to rise.

He drew back, ready for another attack.

It did not come.

He waited, breath rough, his hand shaking with the force he had used. Adhira's chest moved. The eyes opened, then narrowed against the dust. The hand beside the broken brick no longer reached for him.

The fight was over.

Ram had won.

---

For a long moment he could not stand straight.

He had expected the next breath to feel different once the struggle ended. It hurt in the same place. The ankle had not been waiting for victory before deciding whether it was injured. Blood continued moving down his palm until he pressed torn cloth against the cut.

He looked at Adhira again.

The defeated figure was neither a vanished nightmare nor proof that the world had become harmless. It was the person he had fought, down on the broken ground, unable to continue that fight.

He could leave the next blow unstruck.

That was part of having won too.

Ram got his weight over the sound leg and used what remained of the wall to rise. The bricks shifted under his hand. He let them settle, then tried again, more carefully.

This time he stood.

There was no crowd to tell him what it meant. He had the torn cloth, the hurting side and the knowledge that the attack in front of him had ended.

He began the work of getting home.

---

The memory returned to 2026 by way of the nurse's voice.

Ram opened his eyes. He had been quiet while she finished helping him, not gone from the ward. The support was where it needed to be. The chest pain belonged to the injury being treated now; a remembered blow had not put another wound beneath the dressing.

"Yahaan tak theek?" she asked.

He told her what the position felt like.

She adjusted it once, checked the answer and moved the phone out of the way. The call was over. Nothing he remembered required him to summon the strength for another one tonight.

He let his eyes close again without trying to finish the story aloud to an absent listener.

In another city, his son knew a name and an outcome.

He had not heard every blow his father had just remembered.

---

Rudra tried to imagine his father losing.

Not the way he had seen Ram fall at Manikarnika, where the knowledge of an injured parent had been too immediate to leave room for thinking about anything else. An earlier defeat. One which might have happened while Rudra was still going to classes, still deciding how much of his tiredness to turn into a joke for Veer.

There would have been an ordinary day on both sides of it. The boy would not have known which one was about to become the day without his father.

His good hand had curled on the blanket again.

He let it open.

Vedant asked whether he needed anything before the next handover.

"Aapse kuchh poochhna hai."

The man waited until the nurse had finished the check at the next bed. Then he moved his chair nearer, keeping the space for the team clear.

Rudra looked at the supported forearm.

"Rehne dein. Thak gaye honge."

"Thaka hoon," Vedant said. "Thodi baat sun sakta hoon."

Neither part of the answer cancelled the other.

---

"Papa ne aapko bhi sikhaaya."

"Haan."

He knew that much already. Vedant had said it when he first came, long before Rudra understood what else his father's life contained.

"Aapko kabhi laga, jitna seekhenge utna darr kam hoga?"

Vedant rested his arm before answering.

"Kabhi. Har cheez ka jawaab nahi mila."

He did not tell a story from his own past to compete with Ram's. Nor did he give the boy an account of the fight on somebody else's behalf.

"Mujhe lagta tha main galat seekh raha hoon," Rudra said. "Itne saal ke baad bhi har nayi cheez pe darr lagta tha."

"Darr aana galat seekhne ka saboot nahi."

The answer annoyed him. Too simple for the amount of work he had done trying to make it untrue.

"Toh kya seekha?"

Vedant did not point to the wrist or call the damaged body evidence of courage.

"Jo kiya hai, woh tum jaante ho. Ghat pe. Usse pehle bhi. Par usse tumhe har waqt taiyaar mehsoos hona zaroori nahi."

Rudra looked away.

He could remember being useful. He could not remember usefulness ever making him feel finished.

---

He wanted, suddenly, to hear something which had nothing to do with fighting.

"Woh college waala page?"

Vedant glanced at the bag, then asked whether he wanted it brought out. Rudra said yes, only to listen to a little of it. He did not reach for the bag with the braced hand.

The page came from the place where the receiving team had put it with his other things. Vedant held it without making Rudra look up into the light and read the heading first. Then a few ordinary lines.

Rudra had expected them to sound absurd.

They sounded like work he had missed.

That was worse in a smaller, more manageable way. He had missed work. Other people had continued attending, forgetting pens, quarrelling about how much a question could reasonably ask. The world had not spent every interval outside his bed waiting to decide what he was.

"Bas," he said after a little.

Vedant stopped at once.

"Baad mein."

The page went back into the bag, not into a promise that the boy would make up every lost day before anyone noticed he had been gone.

---

"Veer ko ek chhota message bhejna hai," Rudra said.

The next check came first. He answered it, rested as asked and had the phone placed near enough to record without holding it. Vedant would not send until he had heard his own words played back.

"Papa ne 2020 ki ek ladaai bataayi. Jeet gaye the, par darr phir bhi saath aaya."

Two sentences.

He listened. He had not included the name, the older massacre or an instruction to find something out. He asked for the message to go only to Veer through the existing contact, when his friend's team said it would not interrupt care.

Vedant checked the destination with him and sent it.

There was no demand for a reply tonight.

---

The man left at the handover instead of extending the conversation until his own pain made rest unavoidable.

Rudra watched the curtain settle behind him. Beyond it the night team was already working, knowing where the bag belonged and which movement still needed help. No one asked the boy to reward the handover by sleeping immediately.

He lay awake for a while.

His father had come home in 2020. This much was no longer an imagined ending to an imagined fight. He had come home, and a boy had been there, already learning how to keep his sore hands still at breakfast.

Rudra could feel the two histories approaching each other.

He did not know yet what his father would say when he asked where one had been allowed to enter the other.

---

Veer heard the message after the post's night check.

The volunteer put the supported phone where he could listen without reaching across the shoulder. Rudra's voice came out quieter than the noises in the passage, but clear enough.

Two sentences. A fight in 2020. A father who won and still came home afraid.

"Phir sunna hai?" the volunteer asked.

Veer shook his head, then answered aloud so the man did not have to interpret it.

"Nahi. Samajh aaya."

He looked at the phone after the voice stopped.

It would have been easy to send back that Ram was right to be frightened. The people at Manikarnika had given Veer enough reason to understand an adult lying awake. It would have been just as easy to say Rudra had never deserved what the fear had cost him.

One sentence would not have made the other untrue.

He did not need to send a judgement through a tired friend's phone tonight.

---

"Kal baat kar lenge," he recorded instead. "Abhi mere liye jaagna mat."

He listened, approved the destination and asked for it to be passed on through the contact without waking Rudra. The volunteer confirmed that it could wait until his friend was awake and the team agreed.

The phone went away from the edge.

Veer asked for help with the blanket before it caught the knee. While it was being moved he admitted that the shoulder had begun aching more in the last position. The volunteer called the nurse rather than deciding himself that more padding must be the answer.

The nurse checked, adjusted what needed adjusting and reminded him what he could do before the morning review. Nothing in the message from Prayagraj's other bedside had changed his own restrictions.

Veer thanked her.

Then, because she was still there and he had already begun being honest, he said he did not think he would sleep immediately.

"Theek," she said. "Dard badhe toh bataana."

No one asked him to turn concern into sleep on command.

---

He remembered Rudra at the college table, pretending a hurting hand was an inconvenience caused by everybody else's conversation.

Veer had known about the training. He had told Aadhya about it when she asked. Knowing had not given him a way to walk backwards into a courtyard and stand beside every younger version of his friend.

He could answer the person who was here now.

He could stop making that answer an instruction to be brave for him too.

The volunteer came back to say the reply had reached the receiving contact and would be held as requested. Then he left for his own rest after the handover, telling Veer who would be nearby.

Veer let him go.

He watched the light at the passage opening narrow when somebody drew the curtain, listened to the ordinary work beyond it and eventually fell asleep without one more message.

In Varanasi, Rukmini had already settled in the nearby room when Dadi came to check the arrangements for the night.

The shoulder needed the support. A small thing had been put on the wrong side of the bed, and Rukmini asked for it to be moved instead of twisting to retrieve it.

"Ab kuchh aur?" Dadi asked.

Rukmini looked towards the phone.

She wanted to check whether her son had slept. She wanted somebody to check whether her husband had slept. The want had the same familiar shape as care, but following it from one room to another could keep every person awake.

"Nahi," she said. "Subah."

Dadi nodded without congratulating her for resisting something so small and so difficult.

Dhruv left Ram only after the agreed handover.

The nurse knew the nearby family arrangement. The next shift had the actual clinical details, not a relative's reassurance that he always managed. His phone was placed out of the way of the care equipment, close enough for staff to help with it when needed.

Ram did not ask Dhruv to carry the account of 2020 to his son.

What he had said belonged to their conversation. What he remembered had not become a message merely because remembering it hurt.

He had once come home from the old fight and tried to wash the signs of it from his hands before the house could see how near the danger had been. He could remember the water running pink and then clear, could remember his own absurd irritation with a small stain which would not leave the skin beside a nail.

Clear water had not made him calm.

He did not have to search that memory for an excuse tonight.

He could let it be something he would need to answer for honestly when the boy asked again.

Rudra woke for a check, answered it and went back to sleep before the waiting message was mentioned. The receiving contact kept it where it would be available in the morning. In Mirzapur the girls' unfinished work stayed aside; nobody used a quiet night to demand one more page from tired eyes and hands.

There was no shared dream carrying the old fight between beds. No single room into which the families and friends had gathered without travelling.

"Kal baat kar lenge. Abhi mere liye jaagna mat."

He looked towards the grey light beside the curtain and smiled a little. Kal had become today without either friend having to keep watch over the change.

The clinicians were still deciding the next small steps by what his body could manage, not by how long he had been patient with the bed. His wrist and ankle remained protected.

"Us ladaai ki baat poochhni hai," Rudra said.

"Haan."

Ram told him a little more.

He had taken blows. The ground had broken badly underfoot. There had been no clean moment in which winning removed the hurt already done. He had stopped Adhira and come home alive, carrying the fear with the victory.

He did not recite every movement as though his son needed a combat lesson before he could understand what happened to a parent. Nor did the boy receive the whole private memory merely because the father gave him these parts of it.

"Haan."

In Varanasi, Ram kept his eyes on the phone.

"Pata hai."

He stopped because the last words had become difficult.

His father waited.

The nurse in Prayagraj checked his breathing and asked whether he needed to pause. He said yes. The line remained open while he took the rest of the answer from his own body, not from a count he could use to force the conversation onward.

"Main ek saal ka tha, Papa," he said. "Aap mujhe kiski ladaai ke liye taiyaar kar rahe the?"
