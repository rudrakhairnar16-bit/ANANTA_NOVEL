# CHAPTER NINETY-ONE — THE DAY HE HAD NOT CHOSEN

---

> *"There was no date hidden from us.*
> *There was a fear which kept moving the date away."*
> *— Rudra's later notebook, beside the question about telling*

---

"Koi din tay nahi kiya tha," Ram said.

Rudra stared at the phone.

"Socha tha, jab tum aur taiyaar ho jaaoge. Jab kuchh sambhaalna aasaan hoga. Phir ek aur wajah aa jaati thi rukne ki."

"Aur taiyaar ka matlab?"

His father heard the danger in giving another instruction as the answer.

"Mere darr ke hisaab se? Shaayad kabhi kaafi nahi hota."

Rudra had expected a date he had missed, an age, some achievement which would have opened a door he had spent his childhood trying to find by doing the wrong things better. There was no final exercise. Nobody had told the child what score would make him old enough to know his father's name because the man deciding had never settled on a score himself.

"Toh main peeche nahi reh gaya tha," he said.

"Nahi."

Ram took a careful breath.

"Tumhe apne baare mein bataana tha. Maine apne darr ka faisla tumhaari taiyaari ka naam de diya."

The boy's good hand stayed open on the blanket.

For years he had been asked to become sufficient for something. It was strange to hear that, in this one part of the life which had been kept from him, insufficiency had not belonged to him at all.

---

"Bachcha tha toh kam bolte," Rudra said. "Jitna samajh aata."

"Haan."

"Sab nahi samajhta toh poochhta."

"Haan."

The second answer was quieter. Ram did not offer ignorance of that possibility as a new defence. He had raised a child. He knew that children asked the same question badly and repeatedly, learned part of an answer and carried it around until there was room to learn more.

He had allowed repetition in almost everything except this.

"Mujhe khatre ka darr tha," he said. "Woh sach hai. Par tum sunke kya sochoge, mujhse kya poochhoge—uska bhi darr tha. Main usse hifazat ke andar rakhke bolta raha."

Rudra looked towards the nurse. She was close enough to see that he had heard, not close enough to choose what he ought to do with it.

"Ab jo bataaya, woh main sun paaya. Bura bhi lagaa."

"Pata hai."

"Dono hua."

"Haan."

His father could not change the earlier silence into wisdom merely because the truth hurt when it finally came. Rudra had hurt before knowing too. That hurt had been less convenient to name because nobody had given him the words for what was missing.

The nurse at Ram's end asked about tiredness.

He answered honestly. They had reached the limit of the conversation, not the limit of the son's right to ask. Ram said that plainly before they stopped.

"Jo poochha, uska jawaab badlega nahi. Din maine tay nahi kiya tha. Tumne koi mauka nahi khoya."

"Theek," Rudra said.

It did not mean forgiven. It meant heard.

---

After the call, Rudra let the next care interval happen without searching it for another thing he ought to have done earlier.

The supported ankle needed the small adjustment it had needed in the afternoon. The wrist ached in its own unremarkable way. He said so, waited for the check and accepted help. None of it made the answer less important. For once, importance did not demand that he refuse every other need until he had understood it properly.

He slept later, then woke when the ward changed around him.

The sentence came back in the dark: no day had been chosen.

He could be angry at the years without inventing the version of himself who would have been awarded the truth if only he had trained harder. That boy had already been asked to do enough.

Far away in Varanasi, Ram rested after the check.

Rukmini was following her own plan in the nearby room. Dadi had gone with her. Dhruv's period beside the bed had ended in a proper handover, and the next nurse did not need the private conversation repeated in order to know where he hurt.

He lay with a clean face and an unfinished answer still moving inside him.

The fear he had named to his son did not belong only to some old battlefield. There had also been fear of being looked at without the answers, without the certainty which he had made so much of his fatherhood depend on. He knew the life which had raised him. He knew the terrible outline of the life taken before it. Between those truths were things he could not remember and things he could not fill by wanting.

He had treated the gaps as a reason to keep the whole beginning closed.

His own childhood had not required that of him.

He remembered a wet evening at a doorway on Earth.

The rain had stopped. Water still fell from the edge of the shade, one slow drop finding the same dark place on the ground. He was a boy, old enough to be sent back for the clothes he had forgotten to bring in, small enough to resent being reminded that the rain had not arrived without warning.

He had brought them. One corner of a cloth dragged through the mud before he noticed. He lifted it, inspected the stain and tried to arrange the rest so the spoiled part would be found last.

It was found first.

The woman folding the dry clothes looked at him, then at the wet corner. She made the tired sound of somebody being handed a task which should already have been finished. He began explaining about the rain.

"Baarish toh sabne dekhi," she said. "Neeche tumne ghaseeta."

There was no good answer to that.

He took the cloth where she pointed and helped rinse the mud from the edge. It was ordinary work, slow enough for his irritation to lose its first sharpness. Afterwards she hung the cloth again under cover. He stayed by the doorway.

He could remember this much clearly: the smell of wet earth, the corner made darker by water, his own wish to say something which had nothing to do with the mistake everybody could see.

The exact order of the words had worn away. The feeling had not.

He had been thinking about the people from whom he had come.

---

He had known by then that he had a beginning elsewhere, and that his first parents had not been able to remain with him. He could not put the thought beside an actual remembered face. Sometimes that felt like another failure, as though he were being ungrateful to people whose absence he could not even picture correctly.

People passed outside after the rain. Somebody called to someone farther along. A child ran back when an adult called a second time.

He watched the child return.

The woman asked whether he was coming in.

He tried to ask whether it was wrong that he did not remember.

It took several attempts. The question kept sounding as though he wanted a better memory of the wet clothes, or permission to remain outside after being told to come in. At last she stopped folding and listened to the whole of it.

She did not supply him with a face.

She asked what he was missing. He could not give an answer which would fit into a useful description. There were people he had been told about. They belonged to him before the wet doorway, before the cup he could remember dropping, before he had learned how to be troublesome in this house. He wanted them to be real without having to make up the part he could not see.

She let the untidy explanation finish.

There was no great speech afterwards. Ram could not have repeated one honestly if somebody asked him now. He remembered her moving the dry clothes off the place where he could sit. He remembered being allowed to stay quiet beside her while she finished the work. He remembered the absence of an instruction to be grateful instead.

The boy noticed she had folded one dry cloth with the edges uneven. He pointed to it, pleased to have something he could correct in return. She opened it, made the corners meet and put it back on the pile. For a moment they were occupied with the same small problem. It was easier to remain beside her when neither had to be the person who knew everything.

He had still been expected to help with the cloth he had dirtied.

He had also been allowed to miss somebody.

The two things had occupied the same evening without either being used to erase the other.

---

In Varanasi, the ward's night sounds returned around him.

A trolley moved. Someone spoke at the duty station. The rain at the childhood doorway belonged to a day long before this bed, not to weather arriving mysteriously because he had remembered it.

Ram answered when the nurse checked him.

He did not reach into the memory for a voice which could tell him he had been a good father. The woman had listened to a boy who could not make his question come out right. Her patience was not a judgment handed across years in favour of everything the boy would one day do.

He could keep her kindness and be responsible for the kindness he had failed to give.

The thought stayed with him through the next stretch of rest.

---

Day fifty-five began after the actual night of checks and sleep.

Ram's review concerned the injured side, his breathing, the positions and activity the team had allowed. He reported the ache which had woken him and accepted help before the morning conversation with anyone else. His own learned healing was not an argument against needing contemporary care, and memory had not mended the damage beneath the dressing.

In Prayagraj, Rudra's night had been broken but quieter after the last check. The morning team heard that truth rather than a cheerful version prepared to show that the family conversation had done him good. The ward plan continued with the same limb protections. He rested after care before a short call was offered.

Both men wanted it.

When the line connected, Ram asked whether his son had the strength to hear a small remembered thing. No hidden route, no instruction, no test to make the previous answer sound more acceptable.

"Bachpan ki?" Rudra asked.

"Haan. Yaad na hone ke baare mein."

Rudra looked at the branch outside the window, then towards the nurse, who checked that he was comfortable before settling the stand.

"Boliye," he said.

Ram told him about the doorway.

The rain, the cloth, the part of the stain he had tried to hide beneath the clean folds. Rudra smiled at that before the account changed. His father did not pretend to remember every reply from the woman who had let him sit down. He remembered what he had been trying to ask, and that she had remained long enough to hear it.

"Unhe yaad nahi kar paa rahe the," Rudra said.

"Chehra nahi. Aawaaz nahi. Phir lagta tha, unka jo hai woh mujhse chhoot raha hai."

"Aapki galti toh nahi thi."

"Nahi thi. Samajhne mein waqt laga."

Rudra looked down at the good hand on the blanket. He had spent so long imagining his father already grown inside every story that the boy at the door still seemed to arrive from an angle he had not made room for.

A child could be loved and still miss the people he had never known. An adult could learn that and still become frightened by a different child's questions. The second fact made the first neither a lie nor an excuse.

"Ab bhi lagta hai?" Rudra asked.

"Kabhi. Par uske liye jinhone paala unse door hona zaroori nahi. Tab bhi nahi tha."

He stopped for breath and answered the nurse's quiet question before continuing.

"Mujhe dono sach rehne diye gaye the. Maine tumhaare liye bahut baar sirf ek jawaab rakha. Jo main theek samajhta tha."

The admission had a different weight with the wet doorway behind it. His father had not been born knowing only how to command. There had been room made for him, and he had sometimes failed to make it for another person.

Rudra could hear that without being required to decide the whole man before the call ended.

"Mujhe unke liye aapke jaisa dukh nahi aata," he said. "Main sochta hoon, bura lagta hai. Par aapke jaisa nahi."

"Aana bhi zaroori nahi. Tumhaara unse rishta apni jagah banega. Mere jaisa dard karke nahi."

The son breathed out slowly.

He did not have to borrow a memory or perform the correct grief before wanting to see where the people had lived. He could begin with what he actually knew and let the feeling be as unfinished as it was.

They ended after that, while both could still answer the check and say goodbye without making either nurse ask twice.

---

In the afternoon, Rukmini chose a short call of her own.

She had rested after the shoulder care. Dadi had gone to eat with somebody else available nearby, and Ram was not sitting beside her waiting to hear what their son would say. Rudra had slept since the morning conversation and wanted to speak.

"Papa ne bataaya, koi din tay nahi kiya tha," he said.

His mother listened.

"Main sochta tha bas koi cheez aur theek karni hogi. Phir tum log bataa doge."

"Tumhe aise sochna pada," she said. "Humne jagah nahi di poochhne ki."

He could hear a small noise beyond her voice, somebody moving something out of the way. She asked for the phone to be left where it was comfortable and returned to him.

"Ghar mein sab jhooth tha, aisa ab nahi lagta," he said.

The words surprised him with the relief they brought. They did not withdraw the anger. They stopped it from having to burn every ordinary thing in order to stay alive.

Rukmini did not seize them as a verdict in the household's favour.

"Pyaar sach tha," she said. "Jo nahi kiya tumhaare liye, woh bhi sach hai. Ek se doosra mitaa ke mat rakho."

He swallowed.

For a while they spoke about the shirt she had asked after earlier, the kind which could be managed without pulling his injured wrist through a tight sleeve. He said the current one was comfortable enough with help. She asked whether he wanted another familiar one when the ordinary arrangements made it possible, not a reason to send somebody on an urgent journey.

"Jo puraana hai," he said. "Jismein collar tedha rehta hai."

"Do hain."

"Toh achha waala tedha."

She laughed once, carefully.

"Bahut saaf bataaya tumne."

He tried describing the colour, failed to improve the directions and heard her ask someone to remind her later rather than reaching for the bag herself. The conversation could have a future containing a badly described shirt. It did not have to end with either of them explaining how much the future meant.

Before they stopped, she told him she wanted a quiet interval afterwards and would not be taking another call that evening unless care required it.

"Theek," he said. "Kal jab ho sake."

He did not hear the choice as the price of having asked something difficult.

Afterwards he rested too. The wrist still occupied its brace; the collar of the shirt still lay crooked beside an ordinary dressing. No hidden cover had returned to make this little piece of home safe from every danger. It was simply home being remembered without having to be declared innocent.

The night went on in the three cities with no one required to remain awake to prove that the calls had mattered.

On day fifty-six, Rudra's review again considered the breathing, sleep and what the previous chair meal had actually cost. The team allowed another short supported meal after rest, with the same full assistance and protected limbs. He asked for the bowl to be nearer before beginning this time.

The request took less courage when made before anything had gone wrong.

He ate slowly. There were mouthfuls between which he could look towards the window instead of planning the whole movement again. He still needed help towards the end, and the staff returned him to bed when the interval was finished. The easier part did not cancel the tired part. Both went into the account he gave afterwards.

At the post, Veer continued his own reviewed movement and rest. In Varanasi, Ram and Rukmini had separate care to get through before anybody proposed a conversation. In Mirzapur, the girls had their hand and eye checks before deciding whether they wanted the short call offered for later.

They did.

---

Rudra told them the bowl story.

He began by saying it was something his father had told him after their earlier conversation, not a scene he had somehow seen for himself. The child wanting to reach before he had asked. The person being helped saying stop. The healing which did some work and did not finish all of it. The floor which still needed cleaning afterwards.

Aadhya listened without reaching for the protected hand. Ira kept the uncovered eye closed for part of it. The helper had placed the phone so neither girl had to hold it or keep looking at a screen.

Then Rudra told them what he had asked about his own mornings, and the answer about fear being given more weight than his pain.

This was new for them. He left time for it instead of treating their earlier knowledge of Pataal as knowledge of every conversation which followed the name.

Aadhya spoke first.

"Rukna jaante the," she said.

"Haan."

He heard the anger before she found the next words.

"Phir tumhaare liye—"

"Maine bhi poochha."

"Pata hai. Tumhe hi poochhna pada."

For a moment he wanted to defend his father against the sound of a sentence he had said himself. The impulse frightened him. He waited with it long enough to hear the difference: she was angry that he had needed to do this work, not asking him to stop loving the person from whom the answer had come.

"Woh sirf meri choton ka hisaab nahi hain," he said at last.

Aadhya was quiet.

"Aur usse chot kam nahi hui," he added. "Dono bolna chahta hoon."

"Bol sakte ho," she said.

It came awkwardly, with some of her anger still in it. That made the answer easier to trust. She had not arranged her feelings into a lesson for him before he called.

---

He told them a little of the wet doorway too: the question about not remembering, the place made beside the folding clothes. Again he kept to the part his father had actually related. There was no family portrait in the memory, no object which had survived to identify the people in Pataal, no lost voice which Ira could be asked to find by looking harder.

"Unhe khud sab yaad nahi," he said. "Usse jo pata tha, woh bolna toh rukna nahi chahiye tha."

"Haan," Ira said. "Jo pata hai usko gap ke saath rehne dena padta hai. Gap ko jawaab bana dene se kuchh theek nahi hota."

Her own missing records had not filled while he listened to his father's account. She did not turn his answer into a promise about the answer she might someday receive. The real people she had chosen remained real; the things she did not know could remain things she wanted to know.

"Ab unse kya kahoge?" Aadhya asked.

"Abhi soch raha hoon. Sirf aur sawaal nahi. Apni bhi kuchh baat."

Ira opened the ordinary eye briefly, then closed it again. The interval had been enough.

"Humse taiyaar jawaab mat maangna," she said.

"Nahi maang raha."

"Achha. Humne ek paragraph khatam kiya hai. Zindagi ka nahi."

Aadhya laughed softly. Rudra smiled and let the helper begin ending the call before another careful sentence could keep Ira there longer than she wanted.

There had been no new promise of travel. No demand that either girl revise her conditions after hearing that Ram had once been a lonely child. They had listened to a friend, and he had heard their own limits without making them the conclusion of his family's story.

---

Afterwards the nurse asked whether Rudra wanted quiet.

He did.

She checked the position, left the ordinary call device where he could reach it and returned to the work outside the bed. The good hand rested. The injured wrist had not been asked to hold a pen so that an important conversation could leave a more impressive object behind.

He thought about what he wanted to say next.

His father had been the person giving the account, the son the person asking for what had been kept. That had needed to happen. It did not have to be the only shape in which they could speak for the rest of their lives.

Rudra could tell him something too.

Night passed between days fifty-six and fifty-seven.

The phones stayed quiet through the sleeping hours. In the morning, Rudra told the team which part of the seated meal had been easier and when help had still been needed. They checked the body which had done it, continued the ward plan and kept the limits on the injured limbs. There would be another assessment before any larger change, not a discharge hidden in the fact that lunch had gone a little better.

He rested after care.

When a family call was possible later, he asked for it without describing the request as urgent. At the other end, Ram had completed his review and was resting before speaking. Rukmini had her own plans for the interval, and nobody went to fetch her to approve what either man might say.

The stand was placed. The line connected.

For a few moments Rudra listened to the familiar voice saying his name.

Then, unexpectedly, he asked about the hinge.

---

"Kaunsi?" Ram said.

"Ghar waali. Jiske screw se aapka jhagda hua tha."

There was a pause.

"Usne shuru kiya tha."

Rudra smiled.

"Haan. Screw ne. Phir maine galat auzaar diya tha."

"Maine galat maanga tha."

He had not expected the correction to come so easily.

"Ab maan rahe ho?"

"Tab bhi tumhaari ma ne bola tha. Kaafi der tak."

The son remembered the laughter from the kitchen. It came with the light at the door, the screw which would not turn, his father's irritation at being watched while failing at something which ought to have taken a minute. None of those details had needed a grand origin in order to remain inside him.

"Pataal waali baat pehle din boli thi na," Rudra said. "Uske baad yahi yaad aa raha tha."

Ram did not answer immediately.

The boy had been thinking of him with a useless tool in his hand while the name Daavansh entered the family. It was such a small version of himself to be offered after all the terrible facts, and somehow harder to receive than an accusation. An accusation would have let him find the place where he was guilty and stand there. This required him to remain a whole person in the life of someone he had hurt.

"Woh bhi aap ho," Rudra said.

"Haan."

"Isliye bola. Koi baat mitaane ko nahi."

"Samajh raha hoon."

The nurse at Rudra's end checked whether he was comfortable enough to continue. He was, for a little. Ram waited until she had finished before asking about the meal.

Rudra gave the ordinary account, the easier mouthfuls and the help still needed. His father heard both. The call did not become a set of instructions for tomorrow's plate.

---

"Apni baat kehni hai aapse," Rudra said after a while.

"Kaho."

He looked towards the bag beside the bed. There had been a time when wanting to say something to his father meant choosing the safest moment and the shortest possible sentence, leaving out anything which might be heard as an argument with what had already been decided.

He could feel that old calculation begin.

He let himself stop it without having to make the next words perfect. His father had admitted there was no hidden exercise which would have earned the disclosure. There did not have to be a hidden exercise which earned this conversation either.

"Aapki baat suni hai," he said. "Jo bataaya. Uske baad mere andar jo hai, woh bhi sunna padega. Sirf meri samajh theek karne ko nahi."

At the far end, Ram moved the good hand on the blanket, then left it still.

He had wanted his son to understand. He could hear now how easily the desire became another task set for the boy: hear enough of the fear, see enough of the past and arrive at the correct tenderness. Understanding could happen without producing the relief the father wanted.

"Theek," he said.

Not a promise to be without fear. An answer to the actual request in front of him.

---

Rudra heard the word and waited for the correction which sometimes came after an agreement.

There was only the small sound of care continuing near his father's bed. A question from the nurse, Ram's answer, a pause while the support was made comfortable again. The son did not borrow the interruption to withdraw what he had said.

His father returned to the phone.

The names had been spoken. The infant had arrived in the account as an infant, the boy on Earth as someone who could remember wet clothes and a doorway, the adult as someone whose love and terrible choices belonged to the same life. Rudra did not have to turn that life into a single verdict before he could speak from his own.

Ram had spent years telling the child what would be required of him.

For once he left the next requirement unmade. He asked, and the question held no answer ready for the boy to repeat.

"Ab tum mujhse kya chaahte ho, beta?"
