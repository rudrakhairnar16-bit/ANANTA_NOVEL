# CHAPTER EIGHTY-ONE — THE NAME HE HAD KEPT

---

> *"I thought the new name would tell me which old memories to throw away.*
> *It did not."*
>
> *— Rudra's later notebook, first leaf of the family account*

---

"Pataal," Ram said.

The word reached Prayagraj without a change in the small hiss behind his voice. Rudra had expected something to happen to the line, to the light at his collar, to the whole ordinary arrangement by which his father could be far away and still speaking beside his ear.

The line stayed clear.

"Mera janm wahan hua tha. Mera Kul Asuran hai. Tummein jo mera hai, kavach use bhi chhupa raha tha."

Rudra looked towards Vedant. Not for a translation. To make sure somebody else had heard a sentence which could not be mistaken for another instruction about breathing.

The man remained in the chair beyond the nurse's working space.

"Aapka?" Rudra asked.

"Haan."

The word was worse in its simplicity than an account full of impossible things might have been. His father was not telling him about a shape which visited the body when it broke. He was telling him where that body had begun.

"Pataal ek Loka hai," Ram said. "Varanasi ke neeche ka koi kamra nahi. Main yahan paida nahi hua tha."

Rudra had spent years learning the limits of the courtyard. Now the man who had made him run them was saying that the courtyard had never contained the beginning of his father's life.

He kept looking at the phone.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini sat very still.

She had not moved the supported shoulder or reached across her husband's injured side. Her good hand rested against her knee. Dhruv had stopped at the chair; Dadi stood where she could see Ram without crowding the nurse.

The small gathering made no sound for the benefit of the boy at the other end. No one offered to explain Ram before he had finished speaking for himself.

"Mera ek aur naam hai," Ram said.

Rudra shut his eyes.

Of course there was. There would be a different name for the man who belonged to a place outside the world in which he had raised his son. For one awful instant Rudra wanted to refuse it, as if the refusal could keep at least the old voice intact.

Then he opened his eyes again.

"Boliye."

"Daavansh."

The nurse glanced at his face. Rudra answered her quiet check before asking the thing he could no longer keep behind it.

"Ram kaun hai?"

His father took time over the next breath.

"Main."

"Aur woh?"

"Woh bhi main."

---

Rudra could remember his father changing a door hinge.

Not a heroic memory. A screw which would not turn, the particular irritation with which Ram had asked him for a different tool and then found that the first one had been right after all. His mother laughing from the kitchen because the man had spent five minutes accusing a screw of bad character.

Daavansh did not fit into that memory.

Neither did he replace it. The voice on the phone was still the voice which had argued with the hinge and called Rudra before dawn and, much later, asked a nurse whether somebody had sent the next college page.

"Toh ghar mein—"

He stopped. The sentence was trying to become an accusation against every cup they had shared.

"Ghar mein jhooth tha?"

Ram did not answer quickly enough to make it feel rehearsed.

"Jo nahi bataya, woh chhupaya. Tumhara baap hona nahi. Tumhare saath rehna nahi."

Rudra heard the distinction. He was not ready to accept that his father could draw it wherever it hurt least.

"Mujhe farq kaise pata chalta?"

Across the two wards, nobody came to Ram's rescue.

"Nahi pata chal sakta tha," he said.

---

The nurse in Prayagraj touched the edge of the phone stand.

Rudra turned towards her. He was breathing faster, and she needed him to attend to the body which was listening as well as the words being heard. He let her check him, stayed within the support and did not make the question an excuse to sit up on the wrist.

At the other end, Ram's nurse spoke softly too.

The truth had reached two injured people. It had not made either of them able to hold an unlimited conversation.

"Baad mein baat karenge," Rukmini said.

Rudra felt the old anger lift its head. Later had always been the room in which they put the answers he was not supposed to touch.

His mother heard the silence after her words.

"Rukne ko bol rahi hoon. Bhoolne ko nahi."

He looked at the clock by the duty station. The hands had moved only a little since the call began.

"Phir phone hoga?"

"Team jab theek kahegi," she said. "Tumhari bhi. Unki bhi."

Ram spoke once more before the nurse helped end the call.

"Naam waapas nahi loonga, Rudra. Jo bola, wahi sach hai."

The line closed.

The phone stayed where it was, an ordinary dark rectangle, while the boy beside it tried to understand how his father could have come from somewhere else and still be the person whose silence hurt him most.

---

For a while Rudra wanted to hear the word again.

Not the whole call. Only Pataal, in the voice which had spent his childhood naming distances he was expected to cover. He wanted to find the instant at which the old voice became a stranger's, and could not.

The nurse finished the check. He told her the pressure had not suddenly become a different pain and answered the questions she needed. The dressing was attended as part of his care, not lifted to discover whether a new family name had changed the colours underneath.

It had not.

His wrist still lay in its brace. The supported ankle occupied the same inconvenient stretch of bed. He had imagined truth as something which might at least rearrange the pain into an order he could understand. His body had kept its own arrangements.

Vedant waited until the nurse had finished before speaking.

"Kuchh chahiye?"

Rudra almost asked for the mirror.

He knew what would be in it: his face, tired and badly slept, with nothing in the cheekbones which could tell him how to be the son of a place he had never seen.

"Abhi nahi."

---

The name on the patient's band was his ordinary name.

Rudra Vashisht.

He read it twice. The letters had been printed before the last part of the kavach failed, before Daavansh became a sound he knew. Neither the person who had printed them nor the boy wearing them had been trying to conceal a foreign birth. He had been born into this life, gone to its schools, learned where a particular step collected rain outside his college.

He could remember jumping that wet step with Veer and landing badly enough to lose a slipper.

Asuran, he thought, and the memory refused to become grander.

He had still gone back for the slipper.

---

"Kisi group mein mat bhejna," he said.

Vedant looked at him, then at the phone.

"Naam?"

"Jo unhone bataya. Sab. Abhi."

It was not a request to lie to the clinicians. Rudra made that part explicit. They needed the facts relevant to treating him, and he would answer them honestly. He did not want the account sent round the post as another version of the final, available for a stranger to improve while drinking tea.

Vedant put his own phone down.

"Tumse poochhe bina main kahaani nahi bhejoonga. Ilaaj ki baat team ko batayenge."

Rudra nodded.

The distinction felt small and fragile. The cover which had hidden his signature was gone; that did not mean the rest of his life had become public property.

If anyone beyond Earth could recognize him now, he could not choose what they noticed. Here, in this room, he could still decide whom to call by name.

---

He slept after the examination, more from exhaustion than a successful attempt to make peace with anything.

When he woke, the afternoon had moved along the curtain. Somebody had changed shifts. Vedant had handed over before resting his own forearm, and the nurse who answered the call button knew about the previous night without asking Rudra to perform it again.

He asked for the time and then whether there had been a message from home.

There had. His father was resting after care. His mother knew he was asleep. No one had withdrawn the words said in the morning or asked that the boy be told he had misunderstood.

Rudra let the nurse finish before looking away.

He had not known he was waiting for that particular betrayal until it failed to arrive.

---

His first anger had been almost clean: they knew, he did not, therefore every hurt belonged on the same side of the line.

Afternoon made it less convenient.

His father had concealed a birthplace. His father had also come to every ordinary place in Rudra's life carrying that concealment: the college gate, the chemist, the little repair shop where he would rather mend something twice than throw it away. Those memories did not prove him innocent. Their refusal to become false made the anger harder, not smaller.

Rudra did not want to be told that a frightened man could not help frightening a child.

He knew too much about being the child.

He did not want to discover that the man had never been frightened at all, either. That would make the years simpler in a way he no longer believed.

There were more things to ask than one tired voice could safely answer this afternoon.

For the first time, he could see that choosing the next question was not the same as surrendering the rest.

---

After his next review, the team allowed a short call if he still wanted one.

He asked for Veer.

The phone was set up without his wrist having to hold it. The post contact checked that Veer was awake and able to speak; another person stayed responsible for the work at the table.

Rudra watched the line connect. He could have sent a message with all the difficult nouns in it. Instead he waited for the familiar voice to say his name.

---

"Bol," Veer said.

He was on the supported bed at the post, with the phone propped where he could hear without making the slung shoulder take its weight. The volunteer had asked whether he wanted privacy and moved just far enough to give it without leaving him unable to call for help.

Rudra looked at the patient band once more.

"Papa ne bataya. Woh Pataal se hain."

Veer did not answer.

"Asuran Kul," Rudra added. "Unka naam Daavansh bhi hai."

He waited for something large enough to justify the silence.

What came was a quieter question.

"Unhone khud bola?"

"Haan. Abhi."

"Tu theek se sun paaya?"

Rudra told him where the conversation had taken place, who had been there and that the call had ended because both patients needed to stop. He was not describing a voice heard while his body shook in the dark. His father had said it, awake, on a clear line.

Veer let him reach the end of that account.

---

"Kuchh bol," Rudra said at last.

"Soch raha hoon."

"Itna?"

"Pehli baar dhang se kar raha hoon. Time lagega."

Rudra looked away from the phone. The old joke had arrived in the new room without asking what sort of blood it was allowed to speak to.

Veer heard the silence differently this time and did not fill it.

"Naam bada ajeeb lag raha hai," Rudra said.

"Unka?"

"Sab."

On his own bed, Veer moved the good fingers over the crease in his blanket. He had known the sound of Ram's training staff before he knew why an adult might be awake so early. He had known which bruises Rudra explained with a joke and which ones he would not let anybody see. A birthplace did not erase that knowledge. It also gave him no right to decide what his friend ought to do with it.

"Mujhe tu phone kar raha hai," he said. "Main tujhe hi sun raha hoon."

Rudra swallowed.

For a few seconds that was enough of an answer.

---

He told Veer about asking whether the house had been a lie.

The question sounded uglier outside his own head. He expected his friend to protect at least his mother from it, or point out that Ram had nearly died trying to get them out of the ghat.

Veer did neither.

"Jawaab diya?"

Rudra repeated the answer, carefully enough that it stayed his father's and did not become a kinder thing of his own making.

"Maan liya?"

"Pata nahi."

"Toh abhi pata nahi."

Rudra had never envied another person's certainty quite as much as he envied Veer's willingness not to counterfeit it.

"Mujhe lag raha hai main unse gussa bhi nahi ho sakta. Raat ko—"

He stopped before turning the night into a reason to surrender the years before it.

Veer waited.

"Ho sakta hoon," Rudra said, almost to himself.

"Haan. Baat bhi kar sakta hai. Dono."

It was not an instruction to forgive. The possibility of speaking without forgiving had simply not occurred to him with the same force.

---

At the table outside Veer's small space, somebody asked for the number of clean cups. The volunteer answered. Work continued beyond the edge of the conversation.

Rudra could hear it through the phone and felt an absurd relief that Pataal had not stopped a man from wanting tea in the proper number of cups.

"Kisi aur ko bataya?" Veer asked.

"Tujhe. Ward mein jo the unhone suna. Girls ko main bataaoonga."

"Theek. Main aage nahi bhej raha."

No oath followed. Veer did not make a private confidence into something whose protection required a ceremony between injured hands.

He asked whether Rudra wanted him to stay on the call or let him rest.

Rudra wanted both. He chose the part his body was asking for more clearly.

"Baad mein."

"Haan."

For once the word did not sound like a door being shut.

---

Veer left the phone propped up after the line ended.

The volunteer returned when called. Veer asked for the support to be checked before the next rest interval and answered honestly about the ribs. There was no request for a vehicle to take him to Rudra's bedside, no sudden new right to stand guard because he had learned a more frightening word.

"Sab theek?" the man asked.

Veer looked at him.

"Baat hui. Abhi utna."

The man accepted the limit and helped him settle.

In Prayagraj's monitored bay, Rudra had closed his eyes without trying to find a different face behind them. The name had crossed the distance to one friend. It had returned in the same friend's voice, without a verdict attached.

He could not yet decide how to speak to his father.

He no longer had to decide entirely alone.

---

In Varanasi, Ram tried to ask what Rudra had said after the call.

The question escaped before he could hear the impossible part of it. Rukmini had been in the same room as him. She had not travelled down the line to wait beside their son when it ended.

"Mujhe nahi pata," she said.

He looked at the phone, then away.

For years she had watched him turn ignorance into another task: go out, check a route, mend a joint, make the next morning begin early enough to outrun the thing he could not prevent. Here there was a hospital bed under him, a dressed injury at his chest and the small humiliating requirement to let another person bring water when he needed it.

The nurse asked whether he wanted some now.

He said yes.

Rukmini watched him drink after she had helped place the cup. The drinking took more of his attention than a healthy man would have thought possible. She waited until it was finished before speaking again.

"Usne suna. Poora suna, jitna tumne bola."

"Samjha?"

"Woh usse poochhna."

---

Ram had thought the hardest part would be saying the name.

The name had taken less effort than the question about the house.

He could remember Rudra following him round the courtyard with a tool too large for his small hand, asking why a door had to be hung before it could be shut properly. Ram had answered that question. He could remember the answer now, its needless length, the serious attention the child gave a practical explanation offered without a threat attached.

There had been other questions he had refused to hear in that tone.

He had made a habit of calling the refusal protection.

Rukmini did not have to guess where his thoughts had gone. She had lived in the house too.

"Ab usse tumhe samjhaane ka kaam mat dena," she said.

He looked at her.

"Jo chhupaya tha, tumne batana hai. Tumne kyun chhupaya, woh bhi. Usko tumhari taraf se achha jawaab dhoondh ke dena nahi hai."

The nurse adjusted the blanket clear of the dressed site. Ram waited through it instead of making pain the reason the conversation could not apply to him.

"Pata hai," he said afterwards.

Rukmini's good fingers moved over her own knee. The pulse at her wrist was visible for a moment, quick and ordinary. She put the hand flat until the trembling eased enough for her to lift the cup without spilling it.

Her husband had spoken. Their son was alive to hear. Neither fact made it easy to be the person who had known how long the words were waiting.

---

Dhruv returned from the passage with the current ward update.

Rudra had rested. He had asked to speak to Veer, and the short call had taken place with permission. No new deterioration had been reported in that account. Continued observation did not end because the family's conversation had changed subject.

Ram listened to the report without treating it as an answer to whether his son had forgiven him.

"Agli baar woh jab poochhe," he said, then stopped.

Rukmini let the sentence remain incomplete.

He began again.

"Main tayyar rahoonga. Jitna bol paaun, sach boloonga."

Dhruv put the phone where the nurse could reach it. He had his own arrangements to keep outside the room, and they named who would be present while he went rather than leaving his absence to surprise anyone.

Dadi brought the folded cloth for Rukmini's shoulder position. She placed it where it could be used without pulling the arm higher.

"Jawaab ki rehearsal mat karo dono," she said.

Rukmini looked up.

"Nahi toh uske sawaal ki jagah hi nahi bachegi."

For the first time that day, Ram's mouth changed a little without trying to become a reassuring smile.

He had been caught doing exactly that.

---

Towards evening, Rudra asked whether the clean shirt could be used.

The nurse checked what could be changed safely and helped him with another member of staff, keeping the wrist supported and leaving the collar dressing accessible. The front fastening let the cloth move without dragging his arm through a narrow sleeve. He did the small part she asked him to do, then stopped when the rest needed help.

The old shirt was folded away. Nothing else had been removed with it.

The colour at the collar stayed faint. No joined edge had returned to make the change of clothes feel like a new layer of protection.

Rudra asked for the page stand to remain lowered afterwards. He was not trying to use up the brief interval of feeling cleaner by proving he could study through it.

Vedant had returned after his handover and rest. He noticed the shirt, asked whether the movement had been comfortable and accepted the answer without turning it into a measure of obedience.

"Ghar pe message?" he asked.

Rudra considered sending only the small fact about the shirt. His mother would want that fact. It would also have made a good place to hide the questions he still had for his father.

"Baat karni hai," he said.

Vedant asked the team, then helped arrange the next short call after both patients' care allowed it.

The phone was not handed to Rudra as a reward for having behaved well that afternoon. He had asked to speak. For the moment, his body could manage a little more, and the people helping him made room for it.

---

This time Rudra asked for his father first.

Ram answered from the same ward, his voice rested but not suddenly strong. Rukmini was there. The nurse had placed the phone where neither of them had to hold it; in Prayagraj, Rudra's own team had kept the call within reach of help.

He told them that the shirt had been changed. His mother asked whether it had hurt at the wrist. He answered the actual question, and she let the little domestic report end without making it a substitute for the next one.

"Papa. Ram naam jhooth nahi tha, aapne bola."

"Haan."

"Par main kaise alag karoon? Kis baat pe bharosa karoon?"

Ram moved his good hand against the blanket. He could have listed things he had done for the boy. Every one of them would have been true. Every one would have sounded like a price being quoted for the right not to be questioned.

He left the hand where it was.

"Aaj jo bola, usse shuru kar sakte hain. Tum poochho. Main jo jaanta hoon, bataaoonga. Jahan nahi jaanta, nahi jaanta boloonga."

Rudra listened to the difference between that and the instructions with which their mornings had begun.

For once, his father had not chosen the first movement.

---

"Main aapka hi beta hoon?"

The question came out before he could decide whether it was stupid.

Ram's answer was immediate.

"Haan."

"Waisa beta. Sirf—"

He did not finish. He could hear how much the unfinished sentence might wound someone whose life was different from his own. That was not the thing he was trying to ask. He needed to know whether the man had described a bloodline which belonged to their bodies or a duty he had decided to pass on.

Ram understood.

"Mera janm jis Kul mein hua, woh tummein bhi hai. Main tumhara pita hoon, Rudra. Is hisse mein koi doosri kahaani nahi."

Rudra looked at the patient's band again, then stopped doing it.

The answer did not settle the other questions. It removed one fear he had not wanted to admit: that he might lose the right to be angry with this particular father because a revelation would take the father away first.

"Mujhe abhi bahut gussa hai," he said.

"Suna maine."

"Sirf aaj ki baat pe nahi."

Ram shut his eyes for the space of a breath.

"Woh bhi samajh raha hoon."

---

A wheel rattled in the Varanasi passage.

Rudra heard it through the speaker and remembered the stretcher wheels he had followed after Manikarnika, the hand which would not close round his fingers, the unreasonable conviction that if he could make the man open his eyes he would never again ask anything difficult of him.

He had meant that bargain while he was making it.

His father had lived long enough for it to become impossible to keep.

Being grateful for that life and angry at what it had done to his childhood occupied the same chest. He could no more choose one feeling to make the other false than he could choose which old injury deserved attention because a larger thing had happened through his skin.

"Aapko laga tha main nahi sambhaal paaunga?" he asked.

"Mujhe darr tha."

"Mujhe bhi tha. Mujhe naam bhi nahi pata tha kis cheez ka."

The line held the silence which followed. Rukmini did not reach across it to soften either voice.

"Tumhara darr maine kam nahi kiya," Ram said at last.

It was not the complete apology Rudra did not yet know whether he wanted. It was the first answer that evening which did not make the older man's fear the most important thing in the room.

He let it stay.

---

The nurse asked how tired he was.

He answered honestly. They paused, let him settle, and kept the next part brief. In Varanasi the phone moved a little as Ram's nurse checked that he could continue too. Neither son nor father tried to buy more words by saying the body was fine when it was not.

Rudra had a list in his head: what could hear him now, what the other colour meant, what his father had recognized before the others did. A list was not the same as knowing which question to ask a person rather than an instrument.

He thought of the hinge.

Of a man who could explain a screw for five minutes and leave the beginning of his own life unspoken for years.

"Aap yahan kaise aaye?"

Ram did not answer about combat.

"Bahut chhota tha."

Rudra waited.

"Main khud chal ke nahi aaya tha."

---

All afternoon he had been trying to put his father into another landscape without changing the man inside it.

He had supplied the familiar upright body, the measured feet, hands which knew what to take apart. Even his anger had imagined a man choosing a door, stepping through it and deciding to keep what lay on the other side from his son.

His father took that picture away with the next words.

"Main godh ka bachcha tha, Rudra. Varanasi ki zameen se nikla tha. Mujhe wahaan se uthaya gaya."

The nurse's hands moved quietly beside Rudra's bed. The floor beneath it remained the ordinary floor of a monitored ward.

Somewhere else in the same country, his father lay with the wound he still could not heal for himself, describing a time before he could even stand.

Rudra could no longer fit the account into a warrior's arrival. There was a child in it now, unable to choose a road or explain the place he had come from.

Someone had found that child.

"Kisne?" he asked.
