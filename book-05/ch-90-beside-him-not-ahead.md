# CHAPTER NINETY — BESIDE HIM, NOT AHEAD

---

> *"In the later telling, he agreed to stand between his friend and a world.*
> *What he actually asked for was a place beside him."*
> *— Ira's later register, correction beside the account of Veer's answer*

---

"Haan," Veer said. "Jaana chahta hoon. Par abhi nikalne ki haan mat sun lena."

Rudra heard the answer before he knew what to do with the relief.

At the post, the phone had been placed close enough for Veer to speak without lifting the bad shoulder. There was a nurse nearby, a supported knee beneath the sheet, a body which could not be volunteered into readiness by the voice coming out of it.

"Woh nahi sun raha," Rudra said.

"Achha. Kyunki abhi yahaan se paani lene jaaoon toh bhi aadhe log poochhenge kahaan."

Rudra smiled. The breath caught at the end of it, not painfully enough to hide or enlarge; he answered the nurse's glance and let it settle.

"Apne liye poochh raha hoon," he said. "Tumse pehle girne ka waada nahi."

For a moment Veer said nothing.

Then, "Pehle girna meri aadat thi. Kaam nahi."

The sentence had come out more quietly than either expected. Rudra did not help it become a joke.

"Pata hai," he said.

"Ab pata rahe."

---

Veer looked at the water cup which someone else had put within reach.

He had heard the word ruins only a little earlier. Until this call, Pataal had belonged to the account of a baby sent out of a massacre, a place whose terrible distance had made the known river and the ordinary roof over the post feel briefly insufficient. Now it also had a future tense: something a friend wanted to see.

He wanted to see it too.

The wanting was not simple enough to call bravery. He was frightened of what there might be, of how little they understood about getting there and returning, of finding that the part of himself which always moved first would move before the rest had been allowed to think. He was also curious. He wanted the place to become more than a word he stood guard around because it hurt Rudra.

"Mujhe bhi dekhna hai," he said. "Tumhaare papa ka janm jahaan hua. Jo bacha hai. Sirf tumhe akela na lage isliye nahi."

Rudra listened to that last part.

He had been preparing to accept a refusal. He had not prepared as carefully to hear a yes which belonged to another person's desire rather than the generosity of agreeing with his.

"Aur darr?" he asked.

"Woh bhi mera hai. Saath laaoonga. Chhupaa ke nahi."

Veer shifted his good hand, then left it resting. The helper asked whether the shoulder needed changing. It did. The friends waited while the pillow was moved and the position checked.

When Veer spoke again, the voice was less strained.

"Jab raasta, care, waapas aana—yeh sab samajh aayega, tab apna faisla phir boloonga. Abhi itna keh sakta hoon."

"Itna hi poochha tha," Rudra said.

He meant it now more completely than when he had asked.

---

There had been a time when the two of them could make a whole afternoon out of deciding which side of a college bench belonged to whom.

Veer remembered sitting first, spreading his books wider than he needed and refusing to recognize the hand which Rudra had put down to claim the remaining space. The quarrel had ended with both of them pushed towards the same edge while someone else used the room they were arguing over.

Neither had learned much from the incident. It had become a story they told differently whenever being right was easier than admitting they had both been idiots.

"Waise jagah rakh lena," Veer said.

"Kahaan?"

"Dimaag mein. Saath jaana chahta hoon bola hai. Har cheez ka jawaab mil gaya, yeh nahi."

"Bench pe bhi aise hi jagah lete the."

"Bench pe tumne kitaab rakhi thi. Khud nahi aaye the."

"Main paani lene gaya tha."

"Abhi bhi wahi bahaana."

Rudra laughed softly. This time the nurse asked him to stop talking for a moment, and he did. Veer could hear the short check, the answer that nothing had changed into a new pain, the instruction to rest after the call.

He waited without filling the interval.

The old argument had survived the larger conversation. That mattered more than either boy knew how to say. Friendship had not become a service which began only when somebody was in danger.

"Baad mein baat karenge," Rudra said when he could.

"Haan. Aur kisi ko bolna toh abhi waali baat bolna."

"Dekhna chaahte ho. Safar abhi tay nahi."

"Bas."

The helper at the post checked the time. Veer gave the ordinary goodbye first, then stopped his friend for one more thing.

"Papa se jo poochhna hai, poochh lena. Mere haan kehne se woh baat khatam nahi hoti."

"Kaunsi?"

"Jo abhi bhi unse poochhni hai. Tumhaari."

Rudra looked towards the edge of the phone stand.

Veer did not name a question for him. He had no access to all the spaces in the family calls, and no right to fill them because he had offered company on a road. He was only reminding the boy that a friend could be willing while a son's anger remained unfinished.

"Haan," Rudra said.

They ended the call there.

The rest of day fifty-two did not turn into preparations to leave.

The phone was put away. Rudra answered the later checks, ate what he could and slept before the next change of staff. When he woke in the night, the wish to tell someone Veer's answer arrived ahead of the patience required to wait until morning. He looked at the dark phone and let it stay dark.

There was no emergency in being glad.

By morning the feeling had become less like something he might lose unless he repeated it and more like company he could keep quietly. Someone else wanted to see the place. He did not have to begin carrying that person there from this bed.

The clinician heard about the previous afternoon's tiredness. The breathing had settled again with rest; the quieter periods were continuing through the actual observations, not merely through Rudra's recollection of them. His wrist and ankle were checked separately. He answered about the ache at night, about sleep, about what became uncomfortable when he had been sitting.

The day's permission was a small extension of the ward work already under way: after rest, a short part of a meal in the supported chair, with the staff doing the transfer and staying to see how he tolerated it. The equipment and limb supports would come with him. He was not to push through the wrist or put weight through the injured ankle. Getting into the chair himself was not included.

Rudra asked whether he could use the good hand for the spoon if the table was placed correctly.

They checked the arrangement before answering.

Yes, within that arrangement. If he tired, they would help.

He held on to the yes at its proper size.

---

The meal smelled better away from the pillow.

That was the first ridiculous thing he noticed once the transfer was finished, the ankle supported, the brace clear of the table edge and the nurse satisfied with the position. It was the same food. Nothing had been added to it while he was being helped across. Yet the steam had somewhere to go other than towards his face, and the bowl stood in front of him as though lunch were a thing which could happen at a table again.

He took the first small mouthful slowly.

The spoon shook a little in the uninjured hand. He had expected to feel insulted by that. Instead he was annoyed because some of the food slipped back before reaching his mouth.

"Thoda paas?" the helper asked.

He looked at the bowl rather than at the people watching.

"Haan. Bas itna."

They adjusted the table. He tried again.

For several mouthfuls it worked. There was a kind of pleasure in doing something which had an end as modest as eating it. No audience would remember the result. The next spoonful was not a test of whether he deserved the one before.

Then the effort began to spread, not a new sharp pain but the familiar loss of ease which made his shoulders want to tighten around everything else. He put the spoon down and said he was tired.

The nurse checked him. They ended the chair interval within the plan, helped him back with the same care and finished the meal where he could rest between mouthfuls.

He did not ask how much longer a stronger person would have lasted.

Later, when the ache had been checked and the body settled, he found himself thinking of Veer's water cup. The friend who wanted to come with him had let somebody move a pillow. Neither act had cancelled the wanting.

---

At the post, day fifty-three brought a review of Veer's own shoulder, ribs and knee before the usual supervised movement.

The new volunteer asked him to describe the comfortable position again. Veer did, less impatiently this time. The man listened, got one part wrong and waited while Veer corrected it. The nurse checked the support; the two ordinary attempts became the arrangement which worked.

During the permitted knee movement later, Veer wanted to compare the effort with yesterday. The therapist asked first where it hurt today.

He answered that question.

They kept the movement within what had been allowed, stopped when asked and let rest follow. There was no invisible preparation for a dangerous place hidden inside the repetitions. If a later journey became possible, the body going on it would need these people to know the truth now.

He thought about Rudra's invitation while the helper cleared the cup.

There would be days when wanting company would not be enough to make the knee follow. He had told his friend he wanted to go. He would have to keep telling the truth if the answer became harder to hold.

For now there was no ticket, no bag to pack, no reason to make the afternoon into a farewell.

He asked whether somebody could move the window curtain a little. The light had reached the pillow.

At the ward, Rudra had fallen asleep after the meal and the check. At the post, Veer lay in the easier shade and listened to a bicycle bell beyond the wall.

Both boys remained in Prayagraj. There was a distance between their beds which affection had not been required to pretend was gone.

Rudra told his mother about the meal in the afternoon.

The call had been offered after she finished her own care. He had slept; she had rested the shoulder. Dadi was near her, within the ordinary little arrangement of a family which could not be everywhere it wanted and still needed to know whether somebody had eaten.

"Table pe?" Rukmini asked.

"Haan. Thoda. Phir bed pe."

"Kya tha khaane mein?"

He began explaining the chair, the way the table had been moved, how the good hand shook towards the end.

"Woh suna," she said. "Khaane mein kya tha?"

He stopped, then told her.

She asked whether he had liked it. It took him a moment to understand that there was no other question beneath this one, no request to make the tired hand a lesson about patience or the half-finished meal an encouraging report which she could send to his father.

"Pehle achha laga," he said. "Phir khaane se zyaada spoon pe dhyaan tha."

"Hota hai."

Dadi asked to be heard for a moment. The helper adjusted the phone where Rukmini did not have to lean or hold it out.

"Achha lagaa toh khaane ko bhi bataa dena," she said. "Warna bechaara soch raha hoga saara kaam kursi ne kiya."

Rudra grinned.

"Kursi ki tareef nahi ki."

"Tabhi. Tum log kisi ki nahi karte. Khaana bhi aaya, kursi bhi aayi. Charcha spoon ki."

Rukmini's laugh was brief. She stopped before the shoulder pulled and accepted the small adjustment the helper offered. The nurse knew about it; the call waited while she was comfortable again.

When the phone settled, Rudra said, "Dadi, aapki bhi tareef kar denge."

"Meri chhodo. Main sunke kaam nahi karti."

The line brought them a few seconds of ordinary pleasure, awkward and unfinished in the way real laughter was when several people had injuries. Rudra did not try to hold it open after the allotted interval began to close.

Before they ended he told his mother the part of Veer's answer he had been allowed to tell.

"Woh bhi dekhna chahta hai. Abhi safar ki haan nahi. Jab samajh aayega, phir baat hogi."

"Achha," she said.

No list of four travellers appeared in her answer. She was glad he had been able to ask a friend. That was what she said.

---

In Varanasi, Ram had his face washed with help that afternoon.

It was undramatic work. The basin came, the towel was placed where it would not catch at the injured side, and he was asked whether the water was comfortable. He answered. The cloth passed over the stubble, behind the ear, along places which had once seemed beneath the notice of a man whose body could do so much more than lie in a bed and need washing.

When he tried to turn too far, the nurse stopped him and helped him find the easier angle.

He let her.

Afterwards, clean-faced and tired by an effort he had mostly received, he looked towards the chair beside the bed. Rukmini would be resting now. Dhruv's agreed visit was later, after the handover. There was no one whose presence required him to turn what he was thinking into advice.

He had heard through the consented care update that Rudra had managed part of a meal in the chair, then needed help and rest. He knew no more of the afternoon call than anyone had chosen to tell him. He did not know about Dadi defending the reputation of the food.

He could imagine his son trying to get the spoon to behave.

That image hurt in a way the clinical facts did not. He had spent years making a child's body repeat things until the motion became reliable, and now another adult had moved a table closer instead of asking the body to earn the distance.

He could learn from that without declaring every lesson he had given useless.

The useful parts were precisely why the rest needed looking at. It was too easy to hide a needless hurt inside an account of the hurt which had helped somebody survive.

He waited for the next check. When the nurse returned, he said the position was beginning to ache. She helped him change it before the ache became the only thing he could think about.

---

Rudra spent the later afternoon awake for a while, then asleep.

He had asked that a brief, accurate message about Veer's interest be offered to his father when convenient. Nothing urgent; no request for a travel decision. The receiving contact confirmed that Ram had heard it. No larger conversation was fitted between either man's care and rest merely because the answer felt important.

As night came, Rudra watched the window darken.

The afternoon had contained friends and food, his mother's ordinary questions and his grandmother's refusal to make a spoon the most interesting member of the family. He could keep all of it while wanting something more difficult from his father.

Company was not the answer to a question he had not yet asked.

The night between days fifty-three and fifty-four passed with the usual interruptions.

Rudra woke to one check from a dream about a classroom in which he could not find the right seat. He remembered nothing important from it by morning except the annoyance of everyone else already knowing where to put their bag. No voice supplied a forgotten country. He told the clinician how he had slept and let the small absurdity remain a dream.

The review used the previous day's actual chair interval: the tiredness, the recovery after it, the fact that he had not developed a new breathing problem while eating. The same protections remained on the wrist and ankle. Another short supported interval could be tried later if he was rested; yesterday's permission was not a licence for every repetition he wanted today.

He listened without searching the instructions for a hidden invitation to prove himself.

After breakfast, the nurse helped him settle the blanket clear of the supported foot. The new position lasted a little before he needed it changed. He asked, they adjusted it, and the morning continued without turning the request into a judgement about whether he was improving.

---

In Mirzapur, the girls finished the paragraph.

Not the whole piece of college work, not a triumphant return to examinations or a proof that either injured body had become ready for journeys. One paragraph, whose last sentence had been said three different ways across several days and remained wrong in a slightly different way each time.

Their separate reviews had kept Aadhya's hand protected and Ira's ordinary viewing intervals short. After rest, the helper read the existing words aloud. Aadhya listened with the hand settled. Ira closed the uncovered eye for part of the reading and opened it only when she wanted to check the line she was hearing.

They argued over a word.

Aadhya liked the first one. Ira said it made them sound much more certain than they were. Aadhya asked whether that was not the point of submitting college work. Ira said no, and the helper laughed before catching herself and asking which word to write.

They chose another.

When the paragraph was read back, Aadhya said, "Ab bas. Isse pyaar nahi hona humein. Poora karna tha."

Ira agreed to stop there.

The hand still ached. The covered eye remained covered. Completion had not repaired either injury or made the next page compulsory. The helper put the ordinary college sheet aside at their request, distinct from the private reminder Ira had dictated earlier about Ram's account. Nobody opened that reminder to settle an argument about an adjective.

Later, when Sitara checked on them, they asked for a short message to be offered to Rudra: the paragraph existed at last; he was not qualified to improve it.

There was no question about Pataal in the message. Their conditions had not expired while they were doing something else.

---

Rudra heard it after his own rest.

"Bahut achha," he said. "Mere naam pe mat bhej dena bas."

The contact asked whether that was the reply he wanted sent. He listened to it once, grinned and said yes. The exchange occupied less than a minute of somebody's work. It did not have to become a longer call to count.

Vedant came later, after his own forearm review and the handover which made the visit possible. He sat where he would not obstruct the nurse and asked whether the boy wanted company for a little while.

Rudra did.

For some minutes they discussed nothing larger than the completed paragraph. Vedant had not read it; he declined the proposed duty of deciding which student had contributed the least. Rudra accused him of protecting the other two from fair competition. Vedant asked which competition, since Rudra had yet to submit anything.

The answer was sufficiently accurate to end the prosecution.

They sat quietly after that.

Rudra could see the weariness in the man's face, the care with which he found a position for his own arm. He did not need another father waiting beside the bed to make sure he used the quiet correctly.

"Papa se baat karni hai," he said.

"Poochh lenge kab dono taraf theek rahega."

"Aapse unke liye jawaab nahi maang raha."

"Samjha."

Vedant did not add that he knew the man better, or that a son would understand when he was older. The nurse came past and asked Rudra about fatigue. He answered her first. When she was satisfied, the man in the chair asked whether he should stay until the call was arranged.

Rudra considered the offer. He was grateful for the company; that did not mean he wanted an audience for every question.

"Aap rest kar lo. Phone jab ho sake, bataa dena."

Vedant nodded. Before leaving, he checked that the ordinary help-call was reachable and told the nurse the visit was over.

Rudra watched the empty chair for a moment, then turned towards the window.

There was room to ask his father something without making the man beside him answer first.

The call became possible in the evening, after both men had rested.

Rudra had not used the waiting time to attempt another chair transfer. The day's plan remained with the team, not with the anticipation of having something impressive to tell his father. He was in bed when the stand was placed, with the wrist clear and the ankle supported. In Varanasi, Ram had finished the care which needed quiet; the nurse settled the phone before leaving it within hearing.

"Veer se poochha," Rudra began.

"Suna. Dekhna chahta hai. Baaki baat abhi tay nahi."

"Haan."

His father had received the answer at the size it was sent. Rudra could feel how much easier that made the first breath of the conversation.

"Tumhe kaisa laga?" Ram asked.

"Achha. Phir laga uski haan se sab aasaan nahi ho gaya."

"Nahi hua."

"Par achha laga."

"Woh bhi rehne do."

Rudra looked towards the window. Beyond the pane, the branch had become dark enough that he could distinguish it only where it crossed a lighter part of sky.

He told his father about part of the meal: how it smelled different at the table, how the bowl had been moved closer. Not an account of every mouthful. Only what had felt strange enough to keep.

Ram listened.

"Maine bahut baar doori badhaayi," he said after a while. "Jab tumse poochhna chahiye tha ki kitni door tak pahunch paa rahe ho."

The son let the sentence reach him without asking it to mean that every distance in his life had been cruel.

"Abhi table paas karwa li," he said.

"Achha kiya."

There was a little warmth in the answer. Not pride in endurance. Something quieter, almost relief that the boy had been able to ask for an easier arrangement and still finish some of his lunch.

---

Rudra had not meant to begin with food. He was glad he had.

It made the next words harder to say and less like words he had prepared for an enemy. The man at the other end had listened to the bowl and the spoon. He was also the man who had kept a whole beginning of their family outside the son's reach.

"Papa, main aapse naraaz hoon toh bhi aapki baat sunna chahta hoon," he said.

Ram answered without making him wait through an interpretation of what the anger ought to mean.

"Sunoonga main bhi."

"Abhi jo bataaya, usse pehle bhi main aapka beta tha. Sab baat samajh nahi aati, par poochh sakta tha."

The nurse at Ram's end came closer for a check. He answered her, accepted the small change she made to the support and asked for a moment before continuing. Rudra waited. The pause contained care, not a new excuse for the old silence.

When his father returned to the line, he said, "Poochh sakte the. Tumhe poochhne ka mauka kam diya."

Rudra pressed the good fingers flat against the blanket, then let them rest.

He remembered being told which exercise to do next, which foot to put down, how much longer the body had to last. Some of those instructions had kept him alive. None had told him how to ask why his father's face changed when a stranger said a thing he was not permitted to understand.

"Naam kaise poochhta jab pata hi nahi tha ki doosra bhi hai?" he said.

"Nahi poochh sakte the. Mujhe bataana tha."

The answer did not repair the years. It placed the work of speaking where it had belonged.

---

Ram looked at the phone.

He had imagined this part of the conversation often enough since the first call: the boy angry about the secrecy, the father trying to describe a danger without giving the anger another wound to carry. In his imagination he always found a better first sentence. Here, with a nurse near the bed and the familiar tired voice travelling from Prayagraj, there was no first sentence left to improve.

He could only answer the next one.

Rudra heard the quiet and did not rush to reassure him.

The kavach had hidden things which the son had not known to ask about. Its loss had made the questions unavoidable. Yet the need to ask had existed before the emergency, before the raw colours became visible to anybody in the room. A child had lived inside those missing explanations while the adults called the absence protection.

He had begun to accept that his father's fear was real. What he could not accept was fear becoming an answer which could never be questioned because the person asking was always declared too young, too injured, too unready or too much loved.

There would always have been another thing to prepare for.

His wrist remained in the brace. Nothing at the collar had joined into a hiding edge while they talked. The truth had not given him a stronger body in which to hear it, and he had heard it anyway.

He asked without raising his voice.

"Agar kavach nahi tootta, toh aap mujhe kab bataate?"
