# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED — WHERE HE CHOSE TO STAND

---

> *"His correction was not a new star.*
> *He changed the place from which he asked another person to look."*
> *— Ira's later register, conclusion of Dhruv's first account*

---

"Uski jagah se," Dhruv said.

Veer waited.

"Jahaan seekhne waala khada hota. Pehle wahaan se dekha. Sirf wahaan tak pahunchke nahi, wahaan se lautke bhi."

The answer was smaller than a new method with a name. It was not smaller than the difficulty it corrected. Dhruv had known the ground before the old learner came; knowing had not prevented him from teaching half of it as though the other half could be supplied by trust.

At the next lesson, he had begun beside the learner rather than at the place which made his own instruction easiest to give. They had looked at the starting ground, the turn and the view on returning before the exercise became a test of any one of those things. He had asked what could actually be seen and accepted an answer which did not sound like his own.

"Phir akela?" Veer asked.

"Jitna us waqt theek tha. Aur jab kehne ki zaroorat hui ki abhi nahi, toh uske liye alag se himmat jama karne ki zaroorat kam padi."

Not gone. Dhruv did not claim the correction had made every later learner fearless or every instruction complete. It had made one common mistake less likely to arrive dressed as the pupil's failure.

"Aasman ki baat badli?"

"Nahi. Pehle zameen ki baat chhootni kam hui."

The old teaching still belonged to its nights, its careful attention, its one learner at a time. This was not a new permission to practise it from a hospital bed. It was the account of a teacher learning that the person beneath a clear sky could still need a clearer beginning.

---

Dhruv looked towards the time and asked whether the rest of the answer could wait for the agreed listening.

Veer said it could. The immediate question had been answered. He did not need the teacher to remain until every implication had been converted into a sentence he could keep.

They ended while the shoulder was still settled.

During the night, Veer woke once to the sound of care being given in another room. He checked his own position before deciding he needed nothing changed just then. Later he did need help, and asked. The difference between those two answers mattered more than being the person who always said the same one.

He slept again.

---

On day eighty-six, the proposed gathering became a series of actual decisions.

Rudra's team reviewed the previous days' transfers and the continuing ankle and knee limits. The wrist would remain protected. The chair would take the distance to the room; there would be no extra stand for the sake of arriving as a less conspicuous patient. His breathing had stayed settled, but that was one part of the body, not permission for the others to be forgotten.

Veer's own examination and movement plan retained the aid, the help and the shorter effort if the knee objected. Aadhya's hands were attended to before she decided whether the listening interval fitted the day. Ira had her eye care and the choice of a shaded seat, ordinary right-eye use kept brief and comfortable, the left still protected.

They were asked about now, not yesterday's intention.

Each chose to come for the short account with the limits described. Nobody had to earn the seat by agreeing to a practical exercise afterwards.

---

This time Rudra asked where the chair could be placed before the others arrived.

A position near the window gave the better view of the small court below. It also asked him to keep turning if he wanted to see the person speaking. Another place gave less of the court and more of the room.

He chose the second.

The staff member moving the chair checked that the foot support remained clear, settled the position and asked whether the choice still felt right once he was actually in it. He said yes after looking, not before. The view could be smaller without the decision being a defeat.

When Veer came with the assistance and aid arranged, Rudra did not have to raise his voice to be heard. Aadhya could settle the hands without moving her chair out of somebody else's way. Ira found the shade she had requested and rested the ordinary eye once she knew where the people were.

Dhruv entered without the instruments of a night lesson.

The ring was on its cord. The hand which still needed care was not offered as evidence that he could now demonstrate whatever his story described. He sat with the four and asked where they wanted him, so no one had to spend the whole interval adapting to a position he had chosen alone.

Rudra moved his good hand towards an empty place, then stopped long enough to ask Ira whether the voice would be comfortable from there.

She listened to Dhruv answer and said it would.

Dhruv told the account from its beginning.

The three who had not heard it were not asked to understand from the last question Veer remembered. They heard about the familiar ground, the opening in the branches, the answer which had sounded complete to the man asking for it. They heard that the sky had stayed clear while the learner's next turn had become uncertain.

Rudra found himself watching where the teacher looked when he spoke of the mistake.

Not away from them. Not towards Veer as though the boy who already knew the story ought to supply proof of its meaning. Dhruv described what he had assumed, what the other person had eventually asked and what became visible when he went to stand beside him.

The remembered night had room for an ordinary error. It did not need to conceal a fatal one for the telling to matter.

"Aapne uski jagah se dekh liya," Ira said. "Usne aapki jagah se dekha?"

"Baad mein, jab dono taraf dekhna kaam ka tha. Pehle usse meri jagah pahunchne ko nahi kaha ki tab uski baat sunoonga."

Rudra looked down at the brace.

The answer did not make their positions identical. That would have been another kind of pretending. It meant the difference between them could be examined before the person with less freedom to move was required to remove it alone.

---

Aadhya asked what happened if the learner could describe the ground and still did not want to try.

Dhruv said the refusal would need hearing, not immediately improving into a more acceptable answer. There could be something he had failed to ask, a fear which needed time, a body unready for the task. There could also be a skill still needing work. He would not know which by deciding that reluctance always meant the same thing.

"Phir toh bahut der lag sakti hai," Veer said.

"Lagti hai. Jaldi samajh lene ka daawa karne se jo der baad mein lagti hai, woh bhi gino."

There was a dry humour in it which made Rudra smile. He had never heard a teacher offer patience as a way of taking the shorter road through an honest difficulty. Patience usually arrived as something the student was asked to have while the adult remained certain.

Here it was work the teacher could fail to do too.

"Galat jagah khade hoke sahi baat keh sakte hain," Dhruv said. "Par phir sahi baat bhi kaam nahi aati."

He did not ask them to turn the sentence into a rule which would answer every future situation. A clear instruction could be useful; an order given in danger could need acting on before there was time for a long conversation. This had been a teaching night, with time available, and he had nearly spent that time defending how obvious the answer seemed to him.

Rudra was glad the account kept its actual size.

---

Veer asked if the old learner had finally made the turn without help.

Dhruv answered about the later practice he had actually seen. The learner had learned more of that ground, needed less prompting there and still had things to ask. The success did not prove that a different place, a different night or a tired body would require nothing further.

There was no name given to the improvement. It was not a new kind of person emerging from the trees.

Rudra thought of the ward steps he had managed before the journey and the morning afterwards when the body had needed less asked of it. The earlier movement had been real. So had the later limit. He had wanted one to cancel the other because the account would then be easier to tell.

It was possible for learning to remain true without becoming a guarantee.

When Ira asked about keeping her own account, Dhruv agreed that she could record what he had chosen to tell. A later correction could stand with it. She did not have to spend the eye's rest taking down every word now.

The permission did not turn the memory into a record of the whole man. None of them had been given the life he had not described.

---

Rudra asked whether he could try a little more movement soon.

The question escaped into the quiet after the story. He had meant to leave it for the treating people. Hearing himself ask a teacher first showed him the old shortcut: if the desire sounded like learning, perhaps the answer could come from someone who understood why he wanted it.

Dhruv did understand.

He still did not give the permission.

"Unse poochho jo abhi tumhaara shareer dekh rahe hain," he said. "Main raasta bataane mein madad kar sakta hoon. Aaj kitna chalna hai, woh kahaani se nahi niklega."

Rudra nodded, then asked if the teacher would help him put the practical question clearly instead of answering it in their place.

He would.

When the interval ended, the four left with the assistance already arranged. There was no extra drill tucked into the way back. Rudra let the chair take the corridor and saved the question for the people who could examine the ankle before answering it.

The practical question reached the clinician and therapist that afternoon.

Rudra told them what he wanted, not what the story had made him think he ought to be able to do. They heard how the recent transfers had felt, where the knee pulled, how quickly the ankle tired and what the chair had allowed him to do without adding those efforts together.

There would be no extra attempt after the listening interval. They would review him the next morning, after rest, and decide from the examination and the night he actually had.

He disliked waiting less once the waiting had something inside it besides obedience.

He ate, received the care already due and slept. Once in the night he needed the leg's position changed. He said where it had begun to pull and let the help arrive before trying to solve it by moving the braced wrist into use.

---

On day eighty-seven, the review allowed the few protected steps he had managed before the first visit, with the same necessary assistance and no new freedom to load the wrist or abandon the ankle support.

The therapist showed where the effort would end and where he could sit sooner. His good hand had the permitted support. The chair was brought behind, not left across the room as an encouragement to finish the whole distance.

He rose with help.

For the first moments he listened too closely to the floor, as if the next instruction might arrive through the part of the body touching it. It remained a floor. The useful information came from the therapist, the support, the ankle's actual response and his willingness to say when that response changed.

At the turn he asked to pause.

They paused. The knee needed a smaller adjustment before the next movement. He sat in the chair brought for him rather than spend the remaining effort making the pause look unnecessary.

It had been a little more than he could manage comfortably after the outing. It was not the right to make the next journey longer.

He gave the account and rested.

---

The others had their own work, and it did not become four versions of the same improvement.

Veer practised the short aided movement his team allowed, with the shoulder protected and the seat placed before the turn. He reported the knee's response instead of using Rudra's steps as a distance to equal. His ribs still disliked some positions and the unexpected laugh; the shoulder was not available for lifting a friend merely because the legs had moved.

Aadhya's hand review allowed small ordinary uses where comfortable, not hard grip or rough contact. A light cloth could be moved; that did not restore the altered feeling in the fingers. She put it down when effort began changing the ease of the task. No leaf grew because the hands wanted a better answer about themselves.

Ira kept the left cover. Short comfortable use of the ordinary eye and the quiet afterwards remained part of care. She chose listening when looking was enough for the time being. The choice did not become evidence that another kind of sight had supplied what she had missed.

Their necessary functional updates could be shared as agreed. Their private responses did not have to become a group report.

---

That night Rudra slept badly.

There was no dramatic reason he could give the morning for it. The position had needed changing, the room had been noisy for a while and sleep had returned in pieces. On day eighty-eight he told the treating people before they considered another effort.

The examination did not become a trial of whether yesterday's progress had been honest. The plan was shortened. He did the necessary supported movement, received the care and let the chair take the rest. Nobody asked the account of the steps to be torn up.

He could still remember making them.

He could also need less asked of him today.

After food, he asked about a short seated time in the small courtyard if a later review found it suitable. He described the help it would need and asked for the others to be considered separately, not included inside his wish before they had been told.

The answer did not arrive as a booking. It arrived as questions about the route, shade, seating, return and the next care interval.

---

By the morning of day eighty-nine, they had slept, been seen again and heard the arrangements individually.

The distance to the court would be taken with assistance, not used as an exercise added to the morning. The chairs and supports would go where they were needed. Ira could stay in shade with spoken guidance and no demand to keep looking. Aadhya would not carry things in hands which still needed protecting. Veer's aid and place to sit would remain his, not equipment quietly reassigned because someone else wanted help.

The visit would be brief and end before the next care was due. If someone wanted to return sooner, that was part of the arrangement.

Each chose the short time described.

The ordinary staff who could help were available for that interval, not an entire afternoon which the story would forget to let them finish. The mentors would keep within their own hands' limits. Dhruv did not take the full chair's weight to prove the memory had made him a better kind of helper.

Rudra was brought through the door into air which smelled faintly of damp soil.

There was enough room to ask where he wanted to stop.

He chose a place from which he could see both the little tree and the way back to the door.

Not because the door threatened to disappear. It stood open, with a staff member carrying something through it and another patient waiting for room to pass. Rudra wanted the return inside the view without having it fill the view.

The chair was settled clear of the path.

Veer found the seat arranged for him and placed the aid before sitting. Aadhya rested the hands on the support she had brought. Ira chose the deeper shade, heard where the others were and kept the ordinary eye closed for a while after the change in light.

A bird landed on the low wall, found no food immediately available and inspected them with obvious professional disappointment.

"Isko bhi permission chahiye?" Veer asked.

"Isne tumse poochha hi nahi," Ira said.

"Isi liye itna fit hai."

Aadhya laughed, then warned him not to give the bird ideas about whose lunch had been easier to steal. Rudra watched it hop along the wall, unpersuaded by any of their claims on the space.

The small court did not become theirs because reaching it had required work.

---

A man watering the plants waited while a chair was moved clear of the nearer patch.

Aadhya thanked him. He said the water would find the patch later and went to the next one. The soil darkened under an ordinary pour. Leaves shifted where the water touched them and then settled under their own weight.

She looked without reaching.

Rudra remembered what she had told them about wanting a leaf in the hollow stone. Here the green had existed before they arrived, tended by people who did not need the four to explain why living things were worth watering. He was glad the place had work going on in it which belonged to someone else.

"Kaisa hai?" he asked Ira.

"Sunai achha de raha hai. Abhi dekhna nahi."

He said where the bird had gone and left the rest for when she wanted it. A description could be offered without becoming an obligation to receive every detail in exchange for being helped.

Veer said the knee was comfortable in this position. He did not stand to improve the sentence into a more impressive report.

For a little while the four were quiet together.

---

Rudra could see a strip of sky between the roof and the branches.

His father had once belonged to a place he could not remember. Rudra had now been there and returned with a view he could not complete. Both facts had become part of his life without reducing the life around them to an explanation for a future battle.

He had no fragment in his pocket to prove the connection. The two photographs remained on his phone, with the words which admitted what they could not show. He had not found the right to speak for the unknown people behind every broken front.

He had found that he wanted to know they had lived.

The wanting did not command the other three to remain beside him for every answer he might someday seek. They were here because they had chosen this short time in a courtyard, after care, with limits which were still visible if anybody cared to look.

Their presence was more particular than a promise to follow him anywhere.

He was beginning to understand why that made it worth more, not less.

---

When the agreed time was nearly over, Sitara asked how everyone felt about returning before the next work began in the court.

Ira was ready for the quieter light inside. Aadhya wanted the hands settled again. Veer needed the knee's position changed before the move. Rudra was tired enough to feel the temptation to call it something nobler than tiredness.

"Chalte hain," he said.

The staff helped with the actual move. Nobody handed Rudra's weight to Veer's shoulder or asked Dhruv's tender hand to do the missing work. The ordinary passage back indoors remained wide enough for other people because they waited where the turn required it.

There was a check of the supports afterwards, water, the care which had been due and the rest they had expected to need.

The small journey had happened.

It had not become another realm's permission merely because he had liked being outside.

---

Later, when both sides had rested and a short call fitted their care, he told his parents how the morning had gone.

His mother asked about the actual movement and whether he had reported the tiredness. His father wanted to know if the shorter day after poor sleep had been kept short. Rudra gave the answers without improving them into a steady upward line.

He asked about their day too.

Ram had his own care to describe. Rukmini had eaten and rested. Dadi had sent word that she wanted to hear his voice another time, not be woken to prove she had participated in every part of his return.

No one asked him to identify a house from the photographs again. No one offered a family verdict which made the difficult years harmless.

They spoke of the courtyard, the unhelpful bird and the work still being done outside the window. When it was time to stop, they stopped with more ordinary things left to say.

In the hour before the evening care, Aadhya asked whether Ira wanted company.

Ira did, for a little while, if she could listen without having to keep turning towards the voice. The rooms were near enough for the move to fit the interval already allowed. It was Aadhya who came, with the ordinary help needed, rather than making the girl with the tired eye travel to prove she wanted to be there.

They settled without making the room symmetrical.

Aadhya's hands had their support. Ira kept the left cover and the ordinary eye at rest. The light stayed low enough for comfort, not turned into a spiritual exercise because the two were used to being taught things in quiet rooms.

For a while they discussed the bird.

Ira thought Veer would have tried to train it if it had remained on the wall. Aadhya said he would first have needed to admit that the bird had a better idea of the way home.

They laughed softly. There was no prize for the laugh being free of every cost. Aadhya shifted the hands afterwards and let the support take them again.

---

Her next question was whether Ira wanted the things on the little table moved before the night.

"Pehle bataao kya kahaan hai," Ira said.

Aadhya described what she could see. The water, the call, a folded cloth, the small bowl which had been emptied and left farther back. Ira asked for the cloth to be nearer but wanted the call where it already was. Its familiar place mattered more than making a straighter row.

Aadhya did not try to do the moving herself. When Sitara came in, she passed on the request instead of turning it into a task for the hands to attempt in secret.

Sitara moved the cloth and checked the position with Ira. The bowl could be taken away. The water could remain.

It took less than a minute.

Aadhya found herself noticing how many possible acts of help had not been needed to finish the one which was. Nothing had to be polished into a lesson before the girls could use the table again.

---

Across the corridor, Rudra had put the phone aside.

The two photographs were still there. He did not open them for one last look simply because the day had made him feel he ought to know how a chapter of his life should end.

He had gone to the place his father had lost. He had returned without making it whole, without learning every name, without becoming less a son of the house which had raised him. The truths did not stand in a row with the least comfortable one hidden behind the rest.

He loved his father. Some of what his father had done had hurt him. People had been taken from that man before the son could know them. None of those statements had finished another.

Outside, a trolley passed, its wheel making the familiar small complaint at the join.

Rudra asked for the leg's position to be checked before sleep. The next day would begin with the body he actually had, not the one which an ending might have found more impressive.

He was tired. He was back. There were people he could ask to stay for a while and people he could let go home.

For this evening, those were not small things.

---

Sitara had a few minutes before the girls' separate care began.

Aadhya thanked her for moving only what Ira had asked. The gratitude sounded peculiar once spoken, as though ordinary attention had become an exceptional favour. She tried again, saying that she had liked being asked about things before they became arrangements she was supposed to be grateful for.

Sitara sat where Ira could hear without lifting the head.

"Har baar yaad nahi rehta," she said. "Jaldi hoti hai. Lagta hai poora kaam kar doon toh aasaan padega."

"Aapke baare mein socha nahi tha," Aadhya said.

"Mere saath bhi hota hai."

It was not a confession designed to make the girls reassure her. She let the answer stand without asking them to remember a dozen occasions on which her care had been useful.

The folded cloth lay where Ira wanted it. Aadhya looked from it to her own supported hands.

She had been trying to learn how to ask before helping. She had spent less time considering how much courage it could take to interrupt somebody who was already being kind.

The question she wanted was not about Sitara's power. It was not about who the woman had been before they knew her. Aadhya wanted to know whether the person who seemed so able to hear a small request had ever had to keep her own from disappearing beneath somebody else's good intention.

There was still time for a question, if not a whole account.

"Aapko kabhi kisi ki madad rokni padi hai—apne liye?"
