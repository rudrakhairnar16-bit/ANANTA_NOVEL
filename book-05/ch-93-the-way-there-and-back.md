# CHAPTER NINETY-THREE — THE WAY THERE AND BACK

---

> *"The first mark on the route was not an arrow pointing in.*
> *It was the place where someone had turned around and come back."*
> *— Ira's later register, before the first account written in Pataal*

---

"Dono taraf se dekhkar," Ram said. "Jahaan se jaayenge, wahin se laut bhi sakte hain—yeh pehle dekhna hoga."

Rudra watched the phone.

"Raasta hai?"

"Ek puraana kudrati guzar hai. Uski jaanch karaayenge. Dhruv aur Sitara se baat hogi. Tumhaare pahunchne se pehle unhe dekhna hoga ki aaj kis haal mein hai."

There was relief in hearing something other than strength named as the first requirement. There was also disappointment. A route which had to be examined was less like the road Rudra had been imagining than the answer which would finally let him stop imagining.

"Aap wahin se aaye the?"

"Uska daava nahi karoonga. Mujhe bachpan mein kaise bheja gaya, uska poora raasta yaad nahi. Yeh aaj jaane ki jaanch hai. Us din ka saboot nahi."

His father let the distinction remain plain.

The infant's arrival would not become a set of directions because the son now needed one. Nor would the broken machine at the ghat be made whole for their convenience. This was a passage to be inspected, not a wound to be forced open again.

"Aur mujhe?" Rudra asked.

"Tumhe apni team ko poora haal bataana hai. Kursi se aage kya mumkin hai, woh unke saath dekhna hoga. Dono kaam saath chal sakte hain. Ek se doosre ki haan nahi milti."

Ram did not promise to meet them at the entrance. His own treatment and movement remained unfinished work in Varanasi. He said that too, without making his inability to leave into something the boy needed to cure by asking less.

"Madad karoonga," he said. "Khud chalne ki baat abhi nahi keh raha."

"Samajh gaya."

They ended soon afterwards. Rudra was tired; his father answered the nurse's next question before resting. The road had entered the conversation without entering either injured body as permission.

---

On day sixty-one, Vedant brought the practical questions to the people treating Rudra.

Not a departure request disguised as an update. The boy wanted to understand what would have to change. He listened while the clinician went through what had actually happened over the recent days: quieter breathing, short supported meals, the corridor visit which had ended when he reported fatigue. Useful progress, and still a long way from managing a difficult journey without asking other people to invent strength for him.

The ankle was examined. So was the wrist. His left knee, which could so easily vanish from a summary concerned with the worst injury, received its own questions.

He answered about all of them.

For the present, the existing support and assistance continued. The next work would be controlled movement, tolerance and repeated review. He was not to stand simply because the possibility had been discussed. There would need to be a workable arrangement for the good hand and the help around him before any later attempt to load the injured leg.

"Aur doosri jagah?" he asked.

The clinician could discuss a body and the conditions it would need. An unfamiliar place could not be certified harmless by putting a medical signature beside its name. They would need honest information about the approach, the effort, the rest and how he could return for care. If the actual conditions exceeded what he could safely manage, a wish would remain a wish for longer.

Rudra listened through the whole answer.

Then he asked what he could begin today.

---

At the post, Veer had a separate version of that conversation.

His shoulder could not be volunteered to carry the other three. The knee would need its own progression; the ribs still made some efforts less simple than they looked from outside. He asked about supported walking and being a passenger, not whether he would be fit to stand in front of an unknown attack.

In Mirzapur, Aadhya asked what her recovering hands could manage through a long day with assistance. Ira asked about travel with the left eye still protected, the right eye's ordinary use and rest. Neither question became an agreement to whatever route somebody else later described.

Sitara heard what each chose to share. She did not need a private medical transcript to know that both answers would matter.

The practical account reached Vedant with their agreement. It contained limits, not four places reserved in a vehicle.

---

In Varanasi, Dhruv received the request after his own review.

The rope-burned hand had closed enough for the ordinary work allowed, though some pressure still hurt. He would not pretend that gripping a support or carrying a person was free merely because he could do other things with light. The care he had agreed to help with was handed over before he travelled.

Sitara could make time for an inspection when the girls' arrangements allowed it. They would go in stages, leave a receiving contact who knew when to expect them back and examine the passage as people who might need to bring tired companions through it.

Rudra heard that plan in the next permitted update.

Nobody asked him to bleed on a threshold to make it possible.

The next week had days in it, not a blank space through which Rudra could arrive repaired.

On sixty-two, the therapist worked through the movements which could be done with the limbs protected. On sixty-three, sitting cost more because he had slept badly; they shortened the interval and checked what hurt instead of treating the lost time as a debt. He had care through both nights, meals which sometimes required more help than he wanted and the unglamorous fatigue of using muscles which had spent too long waiting for permission.

By sixty-five, the easier parts were easier often enough to be useful. He could sit for more of a meal with the right arrangement. The wrist still needed its brace; the ankle still had not been offered the whole weight of him. The team examined both again before altering anything.

He began to understand why recovery could feel like repeating the same thing without being the same day. Yesterday's information mattered. It did not answer today's question on his behalf.

In the following days there were supervised trials, help with washing and dressing, nights in which he slept longer and a morning when he did not. The quieter breathing held through the actual checks. The marks where the kavach had failed were cared for as they healed; no new concealing edge grew over what had been exposed.

He was allowed to ask about standing before he was allowed to stand.

---

On day sixty-nine, after another examination and the preparation the therapist required, the answer changed.

A brief assisted stand, with only the newly permitted part of the load through the protected ankle, the left knee observed and the injured wrist kept out of the work. The good hand had a support placed where he could use it correctly. There were people positioned to help, and a chair behind him which nobody had described as failure.

Rudra looked at the floor.

It had been there through every family revelation, refusing to become less far away because he knew a name for the life beneath his own.

He followed the instructions.

The first lift was mostly other people's work. Then he was upright inside an arrangement of support, effort and fear so ordinary that it almost disappointed him. No great force entered the ankle. The knee felt strange. His head became light after the first interval.

"Baithna hai," he said.

They helped him sit before the words became a fall. The therapist checked him; the nurse stayed until the lightness settled. He answered where it hurt and where it did not.

He wanted to apologize for ending it.

Instead he asked whether he had put weight in the wrong place. They went through what had actually happened. The trial was over for now. A later attempt would depend on how he recovered from this one.

"Aaj ka kaam itna tha," the therapist said.

Rudra rested with the chair beneath him and believed the sentence by degrees.

Standing had not made him ready to leave. Being helped back down had not taken the progress away.

---

The later attempts belonged to later reviewed days.

By seventy-two, he could manage the controlled transfer more easily with help. By seventy-five, the clinician and therapist allowed a few supported steps in the ward within the prescribed load, the aid arranged for the good hand rather than pressed into the injured wrist. He tired, stopped and used the chair for the rest.

The following day was shorter. He had slept poorly and said so.

At the post, Veer was making his own progress: supervised short walking after the knee review, a shoulder still restricted even when it was no longer kept in precisely the earlier position, rib discomfort honestly reported when an effort pulled. He did not rehearse carrying Rudra as a way of finding out whether he could.

The girls' reviews in Mirzapur allowed longer ordinary activity with pauses. Aadhya's dressings could be reduced where healing permitted it, but the altered feeling in the fingers still required protection, and a useful hand was not a recovered grip. Ira's left eye stayed protected. The right tolerated more ordinary viewing in short stretches, then needed rest; the change belonged to recovery, not a new kind of sight.

Their agreed updates reached the others without turning four different bodies into one departure date.

---

While the patients were doing that work, the passage had been examined.

Dhruv travelled to Mirzapur after the Varanasi handover. On separate daylight visits, he and Sitara checked the approach beyond the ordinary road, the stone opening and the bends inside. They went through, stopped at the limited stretch they could examine properly and returned by the same way. The receiving contact knew when they were expected and heard them report back themselves.

The account came to Rudra before anyone asked him to decide.

There was width enough for assistance and the folding chair along the inspected line. A low uneven lip needed help; loose grit at one turn had to be watched. Beyond it was a broad place to turn and rest, not a licence to wander into every broken structure visible farther on. The return had worked in both directions during their checks. It would be checked again before the patients came.

"Tumhaari taaqat se khulne ka sawaal nahi," Dhruv told him in the supported call. "Hum guzre. Waapas aaye. Tumhaare bina."

The words took something heavy out of the journey.

Rudra would have to bring his body, its limits and his own decision. He would not have to arrive as the key.

On day seventy-seven, after the previous short day's fatigue had been heard rather than edited out, Rudra was assessed for regional transport.

The answer concerned a particular journey, not the whole future. He could travel to Mirzapur with the arranged assistance, the injured limbs supported, the planned pauses and the receiving care confirmed. He would not be a useful person for lifting bags or managing someone else's transfer. His current movement permissions, treatment and follow-up would travel with him in the handover.

The longer question about the passage would be reviewed there against the actual route account and how he tolerated the road.

Veer's team reached their own decision after checking the knee, ribs and shoulder. A vehicle seat which could be adjusted, help at either end and rest after arrival were conditions, not optional comforts. His report would go to the receiving people with him.

They did not put the two boys in an ordinary crowded vehicle and call the discomfort training.

Vedant had his forearm reviewed before accompanying them. He could coordinate and give the ordinary help allowed to him. He was not the sole person expected to lift a chair, drive, carry luggage and remain awake through everybody else's night. Two suitable transports were booked with drivers who knew the agreed run and receiving contacts. The costs were settled from what had actually been put aside for travel; keeping vehicles waiting indefinitely was not part of the arrangement.

Rudra listened to these things and felt, absurdly, more eager as the list became less heroic.

There was a journey being made for the body he had, rather than a body being demanded for the journey he wanted.

---

He told his parents before leaving.

Not a farewell from the edge of the world. A short chosen call, after their own care, with the ordinary information about where he would be received, who was travelling and when the next update should come. He told them the remaining limits too.

Rukmini asked whether the ankle would stay supported in the vehicle.

"Check karenge baithne ke baad bhi," he said. "Raaste mein bole toh rukenge."

"Tum bhi bolna."

"Haan."

She and Ram would remain in Varanasi. Their own recovery was progressing, but neither had been cleared for this journey, and neither asked a son to treat their remaining as abandonment. Dadi had her own work and rest beside them; she was not being packed into a vehicle because every person who loved the boy ought to witness him arrive.

"Pahunchke bataana," Ram said.

"Pehle rest karwaayenge. Phir."

"Wahi bataa dena. Pahunch gaye, rest kar rahe ho."

Rudra smiled.

He could send a small true message before he had the strength for a whole conversation.

---

They left on day seventy-eight after the final checks.

Veer saw Rudra properly before the vehicles moved, not through a little bright screen. His first impulse was to get up too quickly. The helper's hand and his own knee reminded him that seeing a friend did not change the transfer plan.

"Abhi bhi itna hi bekaar lagta hai," he said from the supported seat.

Rudra looked at the sling and the careful arrangement of the leg.

"Tumne toh poori kursi ko kapde pehnaa diye."

"Personal comment baad mein. Safar shuru hone do."

The laugh was small. The relief behind it was not.

They travelled with the planned assistance and pauses. Rudra watched Prayagraj move past the window and become road. There were shutters going up, a man arguing beside a loaded cart, cattle which required no interest in a patient's timetable to cross where they wanted. India remained stubbornly full of other people's mornings.

At one stop the ankle position needed changing. He said so before the ache became the only thing he could hear. They checked, adjusted it and gave him the planned rest. Veer's own vehicle stopped as agreed; the boys did not have to compare pain to decide which delay deserved the road.

By Mirzapur, Rudra was tired enough that arriving felt first like work: the receiving check, the careful transfer, the questions he needed to answer before anyone could put the bag down and leave him to sleep.

He answered them.

The handover included what had hurt on the road and what had eased with the stop. Nothing relevant was made private in the name of protecting the story of an exciting departure.

A brief accurate message went to Varanasi and to the girls: they had arrived, been received and would rest before any visit.

---

The next morning had a receiving review before a reunion.

The road had not produced a new breathing problem. The ankle and wrist still needed protection, the knee its limits, the ordinary help its place. Veer was checked separately. Both would have a quiet interval before the others came.

Rudra slept for part of it.

When he woke, the bag was where he had been told it would be. The phone was nearby. Beyond the unfamiliar door he heard a familiar voice complain that somebody had put the chair precisely where she had been told not to stand.

He knew Aadhya's irritation before he saw her.

For a moment, that was enough to make the new room feel less strange.

Aadhya came in with the hand protected and the expression of someone who had been patient for the last three minutes and considered that sufficient.

Ira followed with Sitara, the left eye still covered, the right turning briefly towards the light before she chose the nearer chair. Veer's seat was adjusted beside Rudra's. For a little while there was more arrangement than greeting: a support moved, a bag taken off the place where someone needed to sit, the ordinary delay before four people could look at one another without somebody else's elbow in the way.

Then Aadhya said, "Phone pe kam thake hue lagte the."

"Tum bhi zyaada shareef lagti thi," Rudra answered.

Ira sat down and closed the uncovered eye for a moment.

"Main toh dono jagah pareshaan hoon," she said. "Koi badlaav nahi."

They laughed, carefully and without grace.

Rudra wanted to reach for them all at once. Instead he asked for the chair a little nearer, then put the good hand where Aadhya could touch it lightly without using the fingers she still needed to protect. Ira's hand found his after Sitara described where it was. Veer watched and complained that he had travelled too, in case anyone was keeping an account.

"Tumne raste mein hi shuru kar diya tha," Rudra said.

"Toh credit pehle do."

The room was full of the people whose voices he had been living with. Their bodies made the distance they had crossed more real, not less. He could see the care still needed. They could see the raw colours at his collar without the old joined cover between them and what had been exposed.

No one asked him to make them flare.

---

Dhruv brought the latest route account when they were ready to hear it.

He and Sitara had checked the return again. They described the approach outside Mirzapur, the uneven lip in the passage and the clear turning place beyond it. They showed the ordinary sketch of the inspected line, with the turn marked where they would stop. It was a local working sketch, not a map of all the Lokas or a document which knew who had once lived in every ruin.

There were things they had not examined. Those stayed outside the proposed visit.

Rudra asked about the chair. Aadhya asked about coming back if somebody tired earlier. Ira asked who would give the spoken directions at the uneven edge, so three helpful voices would not tell her different things at once. Veer asked who would be doing the actual lifting where it was necessary.

The answer was not Veer.

Dhruv would help within what his hand could manage, with an able attendant who had agreed to the work and the planned return. Sitara would guide the line she had checked. Vedant would coordinate the Earth-side care and transport arrangements rather than pretend his recovering arm could be everywhere.

The route's existence did not depend on a boy holding a form. Nobody would have to keep a power alive while the others crossed.

---

There was another night before the next decisions.

On day eighty, the treating people considered the route account alongside each traveller's current function. They could advise what a short supported outing would require, what effort was not permitted and what changes meant returning immediately. They did not declare an unfamiliar realm harmless because experienced companions had walked its first stretch.

Rudra's breathing had remained settled through the road recovery. He could manage the limited transfers and protected steps he had practised; the chair would take the longer distance. The wrist would stay clear of lifting and propulsion. Veer had his own supports, walking limits and a place to sit, not a job carrying everyone else.

Aadhya's hands would remain protected from grip and rough contact. Ira would keep the eye cover, use the ordinary right eye only as comfortable and accept spoken guidance. Their care would not stop at the mouth of the passage. The short visit had to fit inside it.

The plans included water, the things each already needed for treatment, rest on the Earth side, a receiving contact, transport back and a return before the agreed care interval was due. The attendant's day and the transport had limits. Those were part of the plan, not people waiting forever beyond the edge of the story.

Each heard the facts before giving an answer.

"Is chhote daure ke liye haan," Aadhya said. "Aage ka aage."

Ira took longer. She asked one more question about the turn, heard Sitara answer and agreed to this visit with the guidance and rest described.

Veer said he wanted to come as himself, with the limits already given.

Rudra looked at them. These were different answers from the first invitations because they had actual information inside them now.

"Main bhi," he said.

He meant the short visit they had discussed, not every dangerous thing which might someday be done in its name.

The update to Varanasi went out that afternoon, before the last rest and the next night's checks.

Rudra spoke to his parents about what had actually been agreed: a short assisted visit, a limited inspected line and a return which had been walked and checked. He told them who would remain on the Earth side, where the receiving care would be and when they should expect the next update. There would be no need to pretend he could call from an unfamiliar place in order to make the arrangement feel safer than it was.

"Jo dekho, wahi bataana," Ram said. "Mere liye pehchaanne ki koshish mat karna."

"Pata hai."

His mother asked whether he wanted to speak to Dadi before she rested. He did. She told him to listen to the people doing the helping, then asked whether the shirt problem had been solved.

"Abhi jo hai wahi pehnoonga," he said.

"Toh theek. Saari duniya dekhke aana, collar hum baad mein seedha kar lenge."

He laughed softly. The collar could wait.

They ended while it was still an ordinary family call. The night which followed contained sleep, care and another morning check before anyone went to the vehicles.

---

On day eighty-one, Mirzapur moved past the window before the road narrowed towards the stone country.

The drive was shorter than the journey from Prayagraj, but it still had its own arrangements. Rudra's ankle remained supported, Veer had room to change position, and the girls were not asked to hold equipment in hands which needed protecting. The attendant checked the unloading place with Vedant before moving either chair.

They rested in the shade on the Earth side. Water was offered, symptoms asked about, the next care interval confirmed with the receiving contact. The drivers knew when the return was expected. Vedant would remain available there, with the practical means of getting someone back, not vanish into the passage because being present at every revelation sounded more important than the task he had accepted.

Dhruv and Sitara checked the short line once more. They went beyond the bend and came back themselves.

"Wahi haal hai," Sitara said. "Jahaan tak tay kiya, wahin tak."

Rudra looked towards the opening.

It was less grand than an entrance to a different world ought to have been. Stone, shade, the place where the ground lost the surface warmth of the road. No name waited above it. Nothing in the rock required the colour at his throat to identify itself.

He had been afraid of being commanded to belong.

Here, before the first step, he was asked whether the arrangement was still comfortable enough to continue.

"Haan," he said. "Abhi theek hai."

The others answered for themselves.

---

Sitara went ahead with Ira, describing the edge once and letting the other voices stay quiet. Aadhya followed within the space shown to her. Veer used his own support and did not turn the procession into a chance to prove he could walk without it.

At the low lip, Rudra made the brief assisted transfer they had planned. The attendant helped him stand within the permitted load while the empty folding chair was brought across. Dhruv helped with the chair rather than taking the boy's weight through the tender hand. They settled him again before the longer stretch.

The work was awkward and slow.

He could hear the scrape of an ordinary wheel, the attendant asking whether the ankle was clear, Aadhya saying that Ira needed a little room on the other side. No one held a transformation open. There was time to correct the position because no one had made haste the price of crossing.

The passage bent.

For a little while the light behind them narrowed and the air cooled against Rudra's face. He felt the old desire to look for a meaning inside every physical change. He could let a cooler place be cooler. He could tell the people around him if it became hard to breathe.

It did not.

At the next bend, Sitara stopped and waited until they had all reached the broad turning place she and Dhruv had described.

Rudra could see beyond her shoulder.

---

Pale light lay across broken stone.

Not a wall close enough for the eye to finish understanding. A road, or what had once allowed people to pass between buildings, continued away from the cleared place. There were fallen edges, gaps where roofs should have completed a shape, a dark rectangle which the eye wanted to make into a window until it found the open space behind it.

He was across.

The passage remained behind them, the checked way back still part of the visit. His body had arrived with its brace, its supported leg and the people whose help he could feel. Nothing had risen in his blood to explain the view for him.

Pataal was there without being remembered.

Rudra had spent days imagining the moment in which he would enter his father's world. Somewhere beneath the words, despite everything he had been told, his mind had kept the simplest shape of entering: a door, a room, somebody's shelter waiting on the other side.

There was a doorway ahead of him.

Nothing behind it was indoors.
