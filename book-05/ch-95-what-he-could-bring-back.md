# CHAPTER NINETY-FIVE — WHAT HE COULD BRING BACK

---

> *"He brought back two pictures. Neither was a home.*
> *For the rest, we had to let him speak."*
> *— Ira's later register, beside the first return from Pataal*

---

"Ki main yahaan aaya tha," Rudra said.

The answer sounded small after he had given it.

Ira waited. She was beside Sitara, ready for the way back, with the ordinary eye rested and no wish to make him produce a better thought quickly because the question had been hers.

Rudra looked at the unmarked piece in his good palm.

"Aur jo dekha," he added, then stopped.

The fragment could not show a street. It could not show the difference between a courtyard and a room which had lost its roof, the movement of grit inside a hollow, the way another person's voice became important when an eye needed rest. He could give it those meanings only by telling them.

The telling would have to happen anyway.

"Bas pakadke dikhaana aasaan lag raha hai," he said.

"Haan," Aadhya answered.

She had not asked him to be ashamed of the wanting. That made it easier to see what the wanting could not do.

He asked the attendant to help him put the fragment down clear of feet and wheels. It went beside other loose stone at the edge of the inspected line. He did not set it upright, make a mark around it or tell himself that the gesture had become a ceremony for people he could not name.

It was a little piece of grit removed from a support.

His palm was empty again.

"Do tasveerein hain," he said. "Baaki bolna padega."

---

They began the return.

The street changed when approached from the other side. A broken edge which had hidden the first doorway now revealed how close the next opening had been to it. Rudra had mistaken the first sight for something solitary because that was how it had entered his eye.

From here there were neighbours in the shapes, even without people visible between them.

He did not know which household had borrowed from which, who had failed to bring something back, who had disliked the dust being swept towards the shared way. He could let the possibility of those lives remain ordinary instead of dressing every unknown person for a final battle.

At the first resting place, he looked once more towards the roofless opening.

He did not feel that he had finished with it.

That was not a reason to stay until the body or the receiving people had to pay for his inability to find an ending.

"Abhi chal sakte ho?" Dhruv asked the group.

They answered separately. Ira wanted the same single voice for the uneven turn. Veer needed a short pause before the next stretch. Aadhya said the hand was comfortable enough if she did not use it for support. Rudra gave the actual answer about the ankle and tiredness.

The adjustments were made before they went on.

The way back remained part of what they were doing, not an admission that they had failed to go far enough.

---

For a little while Rudra tried to keep looking over his shoulder.

It pulled at the position which had been made comfortable. He noticed, stopped and asked whether they could turn the chair once at the broad place rather than make the whole return an effort to watch behind him.

There was room. The attendant turned him safely, waited through the look and brought the chair round again.

It was such a small accommodation that the feeling which followed embarrassed him. He had not needed to earn the last look by hiding where it hurt. Somebody had simply helped him have it without making it more dangerous.

The pale road lay in his mind after the actual road left the direct line of sight.

He could carry an incomplete view. He did not have to carry the stone to make it solid enough to count.

Ahead, Sitara described the next change in the ground. Ira listened and placed the foot where the sound led her, with her own consent and the help close enough to be useful. Veer waited until the space cleared. Dhruv checked the turn before the chair followed.

The movement had a rhythm without becoming a count which kept a power alive.

---

Near the low lip, a wheel stopped against a piece of loose grit.

The attendant stopped pushing at once. Rudra felt the chair resist and began moving the good hand towards the side as though he could solve it by helping the body lean. Then he saw where the injured wrist would have to go if the movement continued.

He left it clear.

"Ruko," he said, before the ordinary hesitation could become everybody trying to correct it at once.

Dhruv came to the wheel. Sitara kept the other three in the broad place behind the turn rather than send them round the obstruction. Veer had already started to rise farther than his knee liked; he stopped when the attendant said he had room to work.

They looked at what had actually caught.

It was a small problem, and they had brought enough ordinary help to let it remain one.

Dhruv found the grit caught where the wheel needed clear ground to turn. He moved it aside while the attendant kept the chair steady. They checked the position, eased the wheel back the little it needed and brought it straight again before trying the same stretch.

Nobody struck the stone. Nobody made the difficulty into a warning from the realm.

The chair moved.

At the lip, they paused for the planned transfer. Rudra was more tired than on the way in. He said so. They gave him rest, checked the support and asked whether the assisted movement still felt manageable within what had been allowed. He waited before answering; wanting to be out was not a reason to give the quick answer.

"Ab kar sakte hain," he said when ready.

The attendant helped him through the brief stand. The good hand found the support; the injured wrist stayed clear. Dhruv moved the empty chair with help rather than carrying a person's full weight through his tender hand. They settled Rudra before anyone began the next part.

He told them where the ankle needed easing. The change was made.

There was enough time to do that because they had started back before the last possible minute.

---

The light changed as they followed the final bend.

Earth did not announce itself with a voice. There was a warmer patch of air, the smell of ordinary dust and something growing beyond the stone, the familiar uneven sound of traffic farther away. The passage remained behind them as a route they had used, not an event which required shutting inside someone's body.

Sitara came out with Ira first and took her to the shaded resting place. Aadhya followed. Veer used the last stretch of his own permitted effort and sat where the chair had been kept ready on this side. He said the knee was aching before anyone asked him to stand again.

Rudra came through with the attendant.

Vedant was there.

Not appearing from nowhere, not returning from an unseen battle. He had remained with the transport and care arrangements he had promised to keep. He asked the ordinary questions while the boy was settled in the shade and let the answers come before asking what the place had been like.

Rudra told him about the tiredness, the supported stand at the outlook and the little ankle pressure which had eased after adjustment. Sitara gave the account of Ira's eye watering, the shelter and rest, the continuing comfort with the eye closed. Aadhya described her own hand. Veer repeated what the knee and shoulder needed.

The return had brought four patients back, not four clean pages on which a heroic outing could be written without the inconvenient parts.

Their receiving people would hear the same account.

---

For a while, no one asked about the ruins.

Water was offered. The things already needed for care were attended to within the arrangement, and anything requiring the treating team was kept for that actual review rather than answered by a mentor's confidence. Ira could remain in the shade with the ordinary eye rested. Aadhya did not have to use the hand to help hold somebody else's drink. Veer found the position which made the knee less intrusive.

Rudra looked towards the Earth-side opening.

It did not look large enough to contain the difference between the two views. He did not need to make it larger by deciding that every ordinary stone around it had acquired a secret name.

His phone was handed back from Sitara once the photographs had been checked as saved. He asked the attendant to put it where he could reach it without disturbing the wrist. The two images were still ordinary images, too flat in places and unable to include what the person taking them had not put in the frame.

They were his to describe, not his to improve into certainty.

Ira kept her own short recordings. She had not offered them to a group or a public archive merely by making them. When she wanted to hear them later, they could find a quiet interval and decide what to do next.

For now, all four needed rest more than an audience.

---

The brief arrival message went to Varanasi from the Earth side as agreed.

They had returned. They had reported the actual symptoms, were resting before transport and would speak after the receiving checks if both sides could manage a call. There was no claim that every body was unchanged and no alarming half-sentence left for a mother to complete alone.

The answer came back: message received; rest first.

Rudra listened to it and felt something in his chest settle which had nothing to do with a new power. Somebody knew he was back. He did not have to use the first safe moment proving what he had seen.

When the agreed rest was finished, the drivers and attendant checked the next move. There was help for the transfer, room for the supports and the ordinary road to receiving care. The people who had worked this part of the day would finish their agreed work and go to their own lives afterwards.

Rudra let himself be helped into the vehicle.

As Mirzapur's roads returned around him, he watched a man carrying vegetables argue with a driver about whose turn it was to move. The sound made him unexpectedly glad.

He had come back to a place which had not stopped needing ordinary things while he was away.

The receiving checks broke the group back into four separate accounts.

Rudra told the clinician about the stand at the outlook, the return fatigue, the pressure at the ankle cloth and the transfer over the lip. He included the moment when he had almost helped the stuck wheel with a movement the injured wrist should not make. He had stopped before putting the wrist into it; that was useful information too, not a reason to hide the impulse.

The limbs were examined. His breathing had remained settled through the outing and road return. The tiredness was real, and the plan for the rest of the day remained care, food and sleep, not a second test to see what the successful first visit had earned.

The existing protections stayed. The next activity would depend on the next review.

He was relieved and disappointed, which was becoming a more familiar pair of feelings than he had ever expected to live with.

Veer gave his own account of the shoulder, knee and ribs. The little rise towards helping the chair had not become a lift, and the knee's objection had been heard. He did not ask whether he could make up for the rest by carrying something later.

Aadhya's hands were checked as hands, not praised into recovery because she had managed to keep them out of the roughest work. The altered feeling still mattered. The cloth and protection were attended to where needed.

Ira told her treating people about the watering, what had changed, what had eased with shelter and rest and what she had chosen not to look at afterwards. They examined her, continued the necessary care and kept the left eye protected. Comfort after resting the ordinary eye did not mean either eye could be asked for more on demand.

Each person had gone to Pataal. Each still needed an individual answer about tomorrow.

---

Rudra was helped into the position which would let the ankle rest without pulling at the knee.

The room looked smaller after the view. It also looked wonderfully finished: a ceiling which kept being a ceiling, a door which closed into its frame, a floor somebody could clean without first deciding which pieces of a wall would fall if moved.

He lay looking up until the nurse asked whether the light was uncomfortable.

"Nahi," he said. "Bas dekh raha hoon."

She left the light where he wanted it and checked that the ordinary means of calling for help was within reach. There was no hidden condition that a person who had travelled so far ought to stop needing small things done for him.

He ate with the help and pauses he needed.

The first mouthful tasted strongly of something ordinary, and for a moment he felt guilty about being glad of it. Then he remembered the people whose lives he had been trying not to reduce to their deaths. They might have wanted a meal without being asked to explain how it honoured everybody who had ever gone hungry.

He could eat.

The thought was not a lesson received from the dead. It was a permission he could stop refusing the living body in front of him.

---

The second update to Varanasi carried the result of the receiving care.

All four had been seen. There were aches and tiredness, the eye symptoms had been reported and assessed, and the agreed rest was being followed. They had not been sent back into the passage to make the first trip look easier. A longer conversation could be offered after sleep, or the next morning if that was what both sides needed.

Rukmini asked for a short message from him when he could give it without being woken.

He recorded one before sleeping.

"Aa gaya hoon. Check ho gaya. Abhi thak gaya hoon, so raha hoon. Kal aaraam se baat karenge."

He listened once, decided it sounded exactly as tired as he was and let it be sent.

Her answer came before the phone was put aside: sleep; they would speak in the morning after care. Ram had heard the update too. No one required an account of the ruins that evening in order to believe he had returned.

Rudra let the phone go.

---

He slept for a while without an important dream.

When he woke, the daylight had changed. Someone had left water close enough for the permitted reach. The bag was in its ordinary place. The injured wrist remained inside the brace, and the first thing he wanted to do was scratch an awkward place he could not reach without moving it badly.

He asked for help.

Afterwards he lay smiling at the ridiculousness of that being the first problem after sleep. A world of ruins had entered his life, and the body had kept an itch available in case he became too impressed with his ability to think about it.

The nurse asked whether anything else was uncomfortable.

He answered, let her finish and waited until the rest interval was properly over before asking about the photographs.

There were two of them.

He remembered the street outside both frames. That was the part he would have to keep beside whatever he chose to send.

Later, when their separate care and rest allowed a short time together, the four looked and listened without turning the room into a meeting.

Sitara helped with the phone so that Rudra did not have to hold it in a position which tired the good hand. Ira chose not to spend her rested eye on the images. They described them while she listened, and the descriptions kept the difference between what was visible and what the person speaking remembered.

In the first photograph, the doorway looked almost complete.

Rudra had to say that aloud before he could explain the problem. The frame made the missing roof less obvious than it had been where he sat. A patch of light beyond the opening could be mistaken for a room farther back. Somebody who had never stood there might decide that the doorway protected an inside.

"Beech ka hissa dikh raha hai," Aadhya said. "Baaki ki jagah nahi."

The second photograph included more of the way. It gave a better sense of how one broken front followed another, though it could not show the turn which had hidden the first view on their return.

Veer asked to see that one again.

"Isme road hai," he said. "Sirf ghar nahi."

They still did not know whether that opening had belonged to a house. He corrected himself before anybody else had to.

Rudra was grateful without saying so. The corrections no longer felt like people taking the place away from him. They were helping him leave enough space inside the account for the place to have existed differently from his need for it.

---

Ira played the short recordings she chose to share with the people who had been there.

Her first voice sounded more certain to Rudra than its actual words were. He had heard those words made in the open air. In the quiet room, with the small speaker giving every hesitation an edge, the record seemed liable to become an account which somebody might later quote without the hesitation.

Ira stopped it herself.

"Likhenge toh correction saath mein," she said.

The courtyard that had become an unknown open area mattered. So did the difference between what she had looked at and what the others had described after her eye began watering. She left those qualifications attached, including the limits of the far view. She did not turn a difficult hour for the ordinary eye into a claim that another kind of sight had filled the gaps.

There would be time to put the account in order. Not all of it had to be completed that evening, and recording it had not created a promise to release every word to anyone who asked.

Rudra wanted the first return to remain something they could correct.

The wish felt less grand than wanting never to forget. It also felt more possible.

---

He asked whether there was anything they did not want him to give his parents from their part of the visit.

The question changed the room a little.

The family updates and care reports had already carried the symptoms which needed reporting. That did not make every private sentence said in a ruined street part of a family message. Veer did not offer his old conversations for Rudra to explain. Aadhya could speak about her own thoughts if and when she wanted. Ira's recordings remained hers; she was comfortable with him describing how they had kept correcting the account, not forwarding the files on her behalf.

He would tell what he had seen and what they agreed he could tell about being there together.

It was enough to make a beginning.

"Tumne kya socha, woh bhi bolna," Aadhya said.

"Woh toh clear nahi hai."

"Toh clear nahi hai."

She adjusted the protected hand on the support rather than reach across him. He looked at the space between them, close enough to be company without either person needing to prove it with a movement which would hurt.

"Ajeeb lagega," he said.

"Tumhari baat hai. Report nahi."

---

The evening ended before he became good at explaining any of it.

Veer needed the knee settled. Ira had used enough attention on listening and wanted the quiet which did not require following another person's voice. Aadhya went when it was time for her own care. The goodbyes were small and specific; the four had not failed to share a day because they could not remain in the same room until sleep.

Rudra lay with the phone put aside.

He had brought back no fragment to place in his father's hand. There were two photographs he could offer, neither a claim that he had found a home. There was the knowledge that an entire street could be larger than the family he had first looked for inside it.

He had not found an answer which ended the relationship.

That was different from finding nothing.

When the nurse came, he was ready to say where the ache was without worrying that it would be taken as a verdict on whether he should ever have gone.

The next morning could keep its own questions until he had slept.

By morning, the body had made its own account of the journey.

There was tiredness, less sharp after sleep but not gone. There were the familiar places which needed checking before he was moved. The day's care came before the call, and no one used the arrival of a new morning to take yesterday's protections away.

The four would each hear what could be asked of them next. There was no second trip hidden inside the fact that the first had ended safely.

Rudra asked for the call after breakfast and his checks, at the time his parents had said would work with theirs. A member of staff helped him settle the phone so the wrist could stay rested. He could stop if tired. He did not have to fill the interval merely because everybody had made room for it.

When the connection held, his mother was adjusting something outside the frame.

For a moment he could see only the edge of his father's shoulder and a patch of wall. Then the phone steadied and there were both their faces, his father supported comfortably, his mother looking at the screen rather than the small place where the camera wanted her eyes.

"So liye?" she asked.

"Haan."

"Khaana?"

"Woh bhi."

She studied the answer through the face which had given it. He had sent a message saying he was back; she could still need this small, unrecorded proof that the person who ate and slept had come back too.

"Tum logon ka?" he asked.

His father answered about the morning's care. His mother had eaten. Dadi had received the return message and could be spoken to later when she had rested. The lives on that side had not been suspended inside a single question about the passage.

Rudra was glad they had waited until morning.

---

He told them how he felt now, including where the outing still made itself known. His mother asked what the receiving people had said. He gave the answer without shortening it to the part which sounded like permission.

"Aaj aage ka review hoga," he said. "Kal waali haan har cheez ke liye nahi hai."

"Theek," Ram said.

It was not an invitation to argue for more. His father shifted slightly, found the better position and remained in it. Rudra saw the movement and did not turn it into a reason to conceal his own ache.

They could both need help during the same conversation.

"Do photo liye," he said after a pause. "Dikhaa sakta hoon. Par pehle bolna padega ki kya nahi dikh raha."

His mother nodded. His father waited.

Rudra found himself explaining that the pictures did not establish whose doorway it was. He had not found a family house. They had gone only as far as the inspected line, and the road continued beyond what they could see. The little part he had reached could not tell them how the whole realm stood.

He did not forward Ira's recordings. He did not borrow his friends' private words to make his own account sound less unfinished.

There was enough that belonged to his telling.

"Main aapke liye kuchh pehchaan ke nahi laaya," he said.

The sentence came out closer to an apology than he intended.

Ram looked at him for a moment. Whatever the sight of those photographs might ask of his father later, it had not yet been asked. Rudra did not have to decide the answer on his behalf.

"Woh tumhaara kaam nahi tha," Ram said.

---

His mother reached to steady the phone on their side. The picture tilted, found the wall again and returned to their faces. Nobody tried to make the interruption disappear. It gave Rudra time to put his good hand down instead of holding it ready for an object which was not there.

He could have brought the fragment. He could have made a story cling to its unmarked sides and asked his father to receive that story as though it had arrived already true.

There was no fragment between them now.

The doorway remained unknown. The threshold had been worn by feet he could not name. He had seen it from a chair, with a tired ankle and his friends beside him, and the sight did not become less his because another person would have seen more, or differently.

He had gone to a place his father had lost before he could remember it. He had not gone in order to give his father a childhood back.

On the screen, Ram let the explanation end. His mother's hand settled away from the phone.

Then his father asked for the part which neither photograph could bring into the room.

"Aur tum, beta—tumhe wahaan apne liye kya mila?"
