# CHAPTER EIGHTY-NINE — THE RIGHT TO STAY

---

> *"You were allowed to come.*
> *You were allowed to stay.*
> *The second sentence cost us more to mean."*
> *— Ira's later register, on the first invitations*

---

"Uske na kehne ke baad bhi use wahi jagah dena jo pehle thi," Ram said. "Sirf poochhte waqt nahi."

Rudra looked at the phone.

"Bura lage toh?"

"Lag sakta hai. Bura lagne ko uska kiya hua galat mat bana dena."

The father took the next breath before continuing. The nurse stayed within reach, the phone on its support.

"Maine tumhare saath bahut baar nahi kiya. Tumse wahi dohraane ko nahi kahoonga. Kisi ka jawaab sunna hai toh uske baad ki baat bhi uske liye bachaa ke rakhni padegi."

Rudra had wanted a sentence he could use correctly, a way to ask which would keep the answer from making either person smaller. His father was describing the less comfortable part: what he would do tomorrow if the sentence failed to obtain what he wanted today.

He would have to remain a friend after the asking.

---

"Aur jisko abhi pata hi na ho?"

"Usse abhi pata hone ka kaam mat do."

Rudra let the answer settle.

He did not know the route. There were no travel arrangements, no clinical clearance to leave, no honest account of the conditions in which a companion would have to make each later choice. An invitation to want to see a place could not become advance consent to every unknown between this bed and that place.

"Phir poori yaatra ki haan kaise maangoon?"

"Abhi jitna jaante ho, utna bolo. Jo nahi jaante, woh bhi. Baaki jab pata chale, tab phir poochhna."

The words were not a secret of how to make people willing. They left room for willingness to change when the facts did.

Rudra breathed out.

He could not make the ask painless by making it perfect. He could stop calling the other person's answer a mistake merely because it hurt.

---

"Mujhe Pataal dekhna hai," he said. "Par sabko leke hi dekhna hai, aisa nahi bolna chahta."

"Haan."

"Aapko bura nahi lagega agar koi yahin rehna chahe?"

Ram looked towards the ordinary things beside his bed: water, a folded cloth, the place where the phone would be put when the call ended.

"Mujhe yahaan logon ne zinda rakha," he said. "Yahaan rehne ko main chhota kaise boloon?"

He did not need to make Earth a refuge which counted only until a greater world became available. The people who raised him had not kept the infant alive so that every person after him could be commanded to leave their own work unfinished in proof of love.

"Jo saath aaye, uska apna bhi faisla hona chahiye," Ram added. "Tumhaara dukh uski poori zindagi nahi."

Rudra heard the sentence and let it hurt without arguing.

---

They ended the call soon afterwards.

The question had been answered. No father had decided the son's whole future, and no son had been given a promise that the friends he asked would find the unknown road easier because his reasons for wanting it were real.

He had the nurse move the stand away, answered the next check and rested.

In Varanasi, Ram let his own care interrupt the thoughts which followed. He had said something to the boy which he himself had not always known how to practise. Speaking it clearly did not make the past disappear, and he did not ask the next quiet interval to provide him with the relief of pretending it had.

Rukmini remained resting nearby. The people on duty knew the family arrangement. The evening went on without requiring her to return and approve the answer on anybody else's behalf.

---

Night passed between days forty-eight and forty-nine in the three separate cities.

There were checks, short awakenings, stretches of sleep and the ordinary handovers by which another person took responsibility for a while. No message went out recruiting anyone while the patients who might receive it were supposed to rest.

In the morning, Rudra's review kept the same protection on the injured limbs. The calmer breathing allowed the existing ward plan to continue, not a sudden discharge or an unsupervised attempt to stand. The short assisted movement of the previous day was useful information; it was not permission to repeat it however he pleased.

After breakfast and rest he asked whether there would be someone free to read a little of the college page later.

The answer, for the moment, was no. The helper had another task to finish first.

Rudra felt the small disappointment arrive, absurdly sharp after all the larger things he had been saying he could bear.

He did not turn it into an emergency.

---

For a while Rudra lay looking at the bag.

The page was in it. Nobody had refused to give it back, and nobody was making a point about how little his work mattered. Another patient needed the helper first. He understood that easily until the part of him which had been waiting to hear a few ordinary lines began treating the wait as a judgement.

He recognized the feeling without liking it.

When the nurse came for the check, he answered about the body rather than trying to make the college page one more reason she needed to remain. The wrist ached. The new position was easier. He did not hide one answer to be agreeable or enlarge the other to obtain an audience.

She checked what she needed to check.

The page waited.

---

Later the helper returned and asked whether he still wanted a little read aloud.

"Haan. Agar abhi waqt hai."

There was, for a short while.

Rudra listened to the heading and the first few lines again. They were the same lines. His attention was better today, then poorer; he lost the sense halfway through a sentence and asked for it once more without accusing the person reading of going too quickly.

After a little he said that was enough.

The page went back in the bag. The helper returned to the other work.

Nothing in the exchange would have looked like courage to somebody passing the bed. He had wanted a thing, been unable to have it immediately and still been allowed to want it when the time became possible. He could see how much of his old preparation had taught him to hear delay as a sign that he had asked incorrectly.

He did not have to pass that hearing on to a friend.

---

At the Prayagraj post, Veer learned that one of the volunteers would not be there the following day.

There was work at home. The man had arranged the handover and was telling him who would be nearby, not asking to be released from a debt. The care team knew. Nothing clinically necessary would depend on Veer persuading this one person to remain.

"Theek," Veer said.

Then he heard how short it sounded.

"Ghar ka kaam karo," he added. "Yahaan bataa diya hai."

The volunteer nodded and finished setting the water where it could be reached without the shoulder doing work it was not supposed to do.

Veer watched him.

He had got used to a particular way of asking whether the knee needed adjusting, to someone remembering the position before the next request. That familiarity mattered. It did not mean the man owed him the next morning too.

"Shukriya," he said before the volunteer went.

He meant the days already given, not an attempt to buy another one.

---

The next person asked where the support was most comfortable.

Veer gave the answer instead of saying that the other volunteer already knew. The nurse checked the shoulder when he said it was aching again and helped with the permitted position. His rib and knee limits had not left with the person whose questions he liked best.

Later he rested.

He knew about Ram's birth, the massacre, the authority responsible and the limited account of 2020 which Rudra had actually told him. He did not know that his friend had since asked to see Pataal or heard it described as ruins. Nobody at the post was reading the private family calls out to him between checks.

The next conversation would have to bring its own information.

For now there was a quieter interval, a supported shoulder and the sound of somebody new learning the ordinary work of the bed.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini asked for the later call to wait until after her care and rest.

The receiving contact told Rudra. She was not unwell in some newly hidden way; she was following the plan and had chosen not to squeeze another conversation into the time before it.

He asked for a brief message to be passed back: he understood, and she could speak when ready. He did not ask her to use the interval proving she had meant the things she said about wanting him to have a life.

The answer was a small thing he could do before any larger choice arrived.

When she was rested, she called at an agreed time and asked how the new bed was.

He told her about the view and the college page. She told him the shoulder still needed its support, that she had eaten and that Dadi had remembered where everything in the little bag belonged better than either of the people using it.

They ended with an ordinary goodbye.

No one had been punished for being unavailable earlier.

---

On day fifty, after the review and rest, Rudra asked the receiving contact to offer the girls a short call about Pataal.

Not an urgent call. Not news which Sitara had to put into two tired bodies before they had agreed to hear it. The answer came back later: both wanted to speak, and their team had allowed the interval after their own care.

He waited until the phone was steady on the stand.

At the other end, the helper placed it where neither girl had to hold it. Sitara remained near enough to help without making herself the person who would answer.

"Poochhna hai," Rudra said.

"Poochho," Aadhya answered.

He looked at the bag, at the ordinary page which had been unavailable when he wanted it and still there when help became free.

Then he asked.

"Jab sach mein jaana ho sake, tum dono mere saath Pataal dekhna chaahogi?"

---

The quiet which followed was not refusal yet.

He tried not to fill it with more reasons.

"Mujhe dekhna hai," Aadhya said at last. "Kya hua tha. Sirf naam sunke apni tasveer banaane se aage. Par main abhi safar ke liye haan bol doon, utna nahi pata."

"Kya?"

"Kaise jaayenge. Care kaise chalegi. Kya karne jaa rahe hain, aur kya nahi. Mera haath kal kya karega, woh bhi main aaj decide nahi kar sakti."

She was not asking him to produce a perfect world before anybody could leave. She was asking him not to make her interest carry facts he had not yet given her, some of which he did not yet have himself.

He heard the first part more loudly than the rest: she wanted to see it.

Then he made himself hear the conditions too.

She asked what would happen if someone needed to turn back, or stop before the others did. He had not thought beyond getting everyone there together. The way back was suddenly part of the journey too, a need he had left outside the picture because seeing the ruined place had seemed like the only thing with an ending. He could not answer by promising to be strong enough for everybody.

"Abhi plan nahi hai," he said. "Main bataaoonga jab pata hoga."

"Tab main apni baat phir boloongi."

"Haan."

---

Ira spoke after the pause.

"Meri abhi haan mat maan lena."

Rudra's good fingers tightened against the blanket.

Only for a moment. He let them ease before the wrist had to object.

"Theek," he said.

The word sounded thin to his own ears. He waited rather than making her repair its sound for him.

"Mujhe tumhari baat sunni hai," she continued. "Jagah ke baare mein bhi. Par jo abhi nahi pata, uske liye waada nahi kar sakti. Aur mera dekhna abhi kitna saath dega, uska jawaab bhi care ke baad hi hoga."

"Tumhe samjhaane ki zaroorat nahi—"

He stopped. She had chosen to tell him. Refusing the explanation could be another way of deciding what her answer ought to contain.

"Sun raha hoon," he said instead.

She finished in her own time. The left eye was still covered. The right could do only what it could, and wanting to know was not the same as being able to use it through a difficult journey.

He had not needed a reminder of the injury to respect the answer.

He listened to the reminder because it was hers to give.

---

"Dono ka abhi ka jawaab yahi hai," Aadhya said. "Jaanne mein dilchaspi hai. Safar ka waada nahi."

Rudra looked towards the quieter ward's window.

The wanting-to-know mattered. It did not entitle him to turn the answer into yes before anybody had used the word.

"Theek," he said again, and this time it had more of his voice in it. "Main tumhaari jagah khud nahi bhar loonga."

He could feel disappointment looking for something useful to do. Offer to take care of every need. Promise a route. Say he would make himself strong enough that neither girl would have to worry. Each promise would have moved an unknown onto a person who was supposed to trust him.

He left the promises unmade.

"Abhi jo poochha, woh kisi ko bhejne ki zaroorat nahi," he added. "Veer se bhi main khud baat karoonga."

"Haan," Ira said.

No one asked her to sign the refusal in a notebook or convert it into proof that she cared.

---

Before they ended, Aadhya asked about the college page.

It took Rudra a second to answer. The ordinary question had reached him while he was still braced for the next careful sentence about Pataal.

"Thoda suna. Phir bas."

"Matlab ab bhi hamaare paragraph pe hansne ka haq nahi mila."

He smiled despite himself.

"Woh haq toh bina padhe bhi hai."

"Bahut achha. Phir mat padhna."

The helper moved the phone a little when Ira asked; she had reached the end of the comfortable interval. Rudra heard the adjustment, asked no extra question and let the call finish.

Aadhya's hand still needed care. Ira's answer still had not become a promise. The joke had not been offered as a payment to make that easier for him to bear.

He lay back afterwards, relieved and disappointed at once.

Neither feeling required him to send another message correcting what the girls had said.

---

Day fifty-one began after another night of checks and sleep.

Rudra's review continued the ward plan. The quieter breathing had held; the injured wrist and ankle still needed protection, assistance and their own follow-up. He was not given a departure date, and he did not ask the therapist to make the next movement a demonstration for people who had not promised to travel with him.

After rest, he asked the contact to offer the girls an ordinary message when it would not interrupt care.

He recorded that the college page had still failed to make him intelligent, then listened to the line and asked for it to be sent. There was no question about whether either girl had reconsidered. No photograph of a lonely bed, no suggestion that disappointment had made his condition worse.

The receiving contact passed it to Sitara, who checked whether they wanted it played.

They did.

Aadhya sent back that the page had made no such promise. Ira asked for that sentence to be kept exactly as Aadhya said it instead of improved into a kinder one.

When Rudra heard the reply after his next rest, he laughed softly and let the conversation end there.

---

It mattered that the next words had been about the page.

The invitation remained unanswered in the larger sense. The girls had interest, conditions and no promise for the journey as yet. They had not become less close because those were the answers they could give.

Rudra could feel the relief in himself without pretending it made him generous. Keeping a friendship ordinary after asking something difficult was not a favour performed for the people who had failed to give the desired answer.

He would need to remember that if a later answer was simply no.

For now the day contained the small work he could actually do: answer the nurse, accept the help, rest before asking for another person to spend time with him. The phone stayed supported when used and out of the way when it was not.

He did not rehearse arguments for changing the girls' conditions.

---

In the permitted family call later, he told Ram he had asked them.

"Abhi kisi ne safar ka waada nahi kiya," he said.

His father let the sentence remain complete.

"Theek."

"Unke sawaal hain. Kuchh mere paas jawaab nahi."

"Jitna nahi pata, uski haan mat banaao."

Rudra looked at the bag. He could have passed the girls' exact words to his father, asked him to interpret whether they meant to come or wanted a reason not to. He could have made an elder's opinion into the answer he had been told not to write himself.

He gave no transcript.

"Baaki unse baat karoonga," he said.

"Haan."

The boundary did not need an argument to become real.

---

"Saath na aana, chhod dena nahi hota," Ram said after a while.

Rudra was quiet.

His father did not make that a rule excusing every person who had ever walked away. He was speaking about people asked to share a journey which still had unknown conditions, about a request whose answer did not become a moral offence because a boy had a terrible reason to want company.

"Mere maa-baap se alag hona maine nahi chuna," Ram said. "Uska matlab har alag rehna wahi kho jaana nahi. Yahaan jinhone paala, unka saath bhi sach tha. Tumhare dost yahaan rahen toh unki zindagi tumse kam nahi ho jaayegi."

The son looked towards the window.

He had heard the infant's survival first as a distance which would always need crossing. Now he could hear another part of the life which followed: people staying in one place long enough to raise a child, mend a wound, make the next meal possible.

A ruined origin did not make the places still living into waiting rooms.

He did not have to choose between knowing the loss and respecting what another person wanted to keep.

---

They ended while both still had strength to say goodbye properly.

In Varanasi, Rukmini was resting after her own care, with Dadi nearby and the team available. Ram did not ask for her to return so she could hear that he had answered well. The answer was not a performance owed to a household witness.

Dhruv came for the period he had agreed to, heard the clinical update from the right people and made time for his own rest when the handover was complete. None of the adults had been assigned a passage out of Earth by a son asking what he might want to see.

In Mirzapur, the girls' next care interval concerned their own symptoms and what each could manage. There was no suddenly recovered eye or hand to make a conditional answer unnecessary.

The three cities remained three cities through the following night.

---

On day fifty-two, Rudra rested after the review and the permitted assisted movement before asking about a call to Veer.

The post checked first. His friend wanted to hear from him; they would arrange a short interval after care, with the phone supported. No helper would have to abandon another patient because a conversation about the future had been described as urgent.

Rudra waited.

He knew what he wanted to ask now. He also knew how little of the journey had been decided, and how much of friendship would remain after the answer.

When the nurse placed the phone on the stand, he asked that it be moved a little closer, not into the injured hand.

The line connected.

---

"Haan, bol," Veer said.

Rudra listened to the familiar voice for a moment before beginning. The phone at the post had been settled on its support; a small movement and the answer to a nurse's question told him his friend was ready, not pretending the care around the bed had ceased to exist.

"Pehle ek baat bataani hai. Papa se Pataal dekhne ko kaha hai. Jab jaana ho sake."

Veer was quiet.

"Unhone kya kaha?"

"Taiyaari mein madad karenge. Abhi le chalne ka waada nahi. Khud jaa paayenge, woh bhi nahi bola."

He gave the limits with the first answer, before excitement could make the space between wanting and travelling disappear.

"Aur?"

"Bol rahe hain, ujaad hai. Khandhar. Unka bachpan jo yaad hai, yahaan ka hai. Wahaan jaake puraana ghar pehchaanne jaisa nahi."

This was new information for Veer. Rudra let him have time for it.

---

"Tu dekhna chahta hai," his friend said.

"Haan."

"Kya dhoondhne?"

Rudra looked at the branch in the upper corner of the window. In the days since he first saw it, he had stopped expecting every movement outside the glass to carry an answer about the world his father came from.

"Abhi jitna pata hai, usse aage sach mein jo hai," he said. "Kisi ko pehchaanne ka daava nahi. Koi cheez laake sab theek karne ka bhi nahi. Bas log the. Jo unke saath hua, usse nazar hataake nahi rakhna chahta."

He heard how easily the last part could become an accusation against somebody choosing not to go. He corrected the reach before making Veer do it for him.

"Main apni baat keh raha hoon. Dekhne ke liye jaana zaroori hai, aisa sabke liye nahi bol raha."

"Samajh raha hoon," Veer said.

The answer did not contain a promise of company. It did not need one before the friend could understand the wish.

---

Rudra told him a little of the other story too.

Ram learning beside the people who raised him, wanting to mend before knowing how to hear the person being helped. The spilled bowl. The cloth for the floor. The lesson about stopping, and the son's question about why a man who had learned it had not stopped for him.

He told Veer what Ram had actually answered: fear made larger than pain, not a claim that every morning had been necessary. It was the account a son could give of what he had been told, with spaces where he still had questions. The child at the bench did not become proof that the father had never hurt anyone; the father in the courtyard did not make the child's learning false.

Veer listened without deciding whether a better answer meant Rudra was required to stop being angry.

"Ab baat kar paa rahe ho," he said.

"Thodi. Har baar aasaan nahi."

"Haan."

---

There was a pause while the nurse at Rudra's end asked about fatigue.

He answered honestly and waited through the check before returning to the phone. At the post, Veer let his own helper make the small adjustment he needed instead of holding the shoulder tight until the conversation ended.

The two intervals belonged to the call. Neither friend had left the other by needing help.

"Abhi yahaan se nikalne ka kuchh tay nahi," Rudra said. "Team ka plan chal raha hai. Raasta mujhe pakka nahi pata. Jo aage pata chalega, tab phir baat karni padegi."

He did not sell uncertainty as something the two of them could settle by being brave enough together. They had survived too much actual damage to mistake a shared willingness for the means of keeping everybody alive.

Veer asked whether the girls knew about the wish.

"Haan. Poochha bhi. Abhi safar ka waada kisi ne nahi kiya. Apne sawaal hain. Jawaab milenge toh phir poochhoonga."

He did not repeat Ira's exact words or make Aadhya's interest sound like an agreement already secured.

He was not offering his friend an empty place which had to be filled to rescue the meaning of another person's answer.

---

Rudra could remember Veer beside him in all the ordinary places which had once seemed large enough to contain their future.

The college table. The ghat steps. An argument continued for so long that neither remembered which one had begun it. There had been more difficult places since then, bodies falling where friendship could not make the ground soft enough, and help given before there was time to decide what being grateful would cost.

He did not own the next act of courage because the earlier ones had been real.

The friend at the post had a life which was not an extension of the boy in this bed. It could remain joined to his without taking every road he wanted to take.

He felt the sadness of that possibility, and the relief beneath it. If company came, he wanted it to arrive from a person, not from the part of love which had been frightened into agreement.

---

The phone remained on its stand.

His wrist stayed inside the brace. The ankle had not moved from its support. There was no returned hiding edge at the collar, no promise that hearing the old story had made the next place safe or taught either boy a new way to survive it.

He had asked his father for help seeing Pataal. He had heard his mother's fear without making it a prohibition, heard his friends' conditions without calling them a failure.

Now there was only the asking he had spent days learning not to disguise as a duty.

He kept his voice ordinary.

"Veer," he said. "Jab jaana sach mein mumkin ho, mere saath Pataal dekhna chaahega?"
