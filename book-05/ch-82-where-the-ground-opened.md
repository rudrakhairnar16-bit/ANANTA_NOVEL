# CHAPTER EIGHTY-TWO — WHERE THE GROUND OPENED

---

> *"The first hand to lift him did not ask him to prove whose child he was.*
> *We learned to ask that question later, and called ourselves wiser."*
>
> *— a later private account, copied beside Ram's words about Varanasi*

---

"Vedic Kul ke logon ne," Ram said.

Rudra kept his gaze on the phone.

He had asked who found the child. His father had answered with another family, not a hidden name for the one he had already given.

"Unhone uthaya. Unhone paala."

The breathing support made a small sound beside Ram. The nurse waited where she could hear him without entering the conversation. He used the next breath to make the distinction plain.

"Janm Asuran Kul mein hua. Bachpan yahaan, unke beech beeta."

Rudra had seen his father tend wounds. He had watched him fail to heal the chest which now needed ordinary care. The word Vedic reached those memories before it reached anything he thought he knew about inherited power.

"Aapko yaad hai?" he asked.

"Uthaya jaana? Nahi."

For the first time since morning, the answer contained a limit which did not feel like a door being deliberately closed.

"Main godh ka bachcha tha. Zameen se nikalne ki baat mujhe bataayi gayi. Meri apni yaad mein tab tak ghar hai. Log hain. Main unke paas hoon."

No memory of an infant watching a road between worlds. No long account of a battle he could not have understood. The beginning had reached him through people old enough to have lifted him, not through a voice which woke inside his son tonight.

Rudra let the difference become real before asking anything else.

---

"Aapko apna maana?"

The question was meant to be practical. Food, a place to sleep, someone who knew whether the little body was getting ill.

It escaped with a child's need in it.

Ram heard that too.

"Haan."

"Asuran the, phir bhi?"

"Bachcha tha," Ram said. "Pehle woh dekha unhone."

In Varanasi, Rukmini looked at the man beside her. The name he had kept back was not the only part of his childhood she could hear returning to his voice. There was a different softness in the way he said the word for a child, a softness which had not always survived into the mornings he gave their own son.

She did not correct the memory on Rudra's behalf. She stayed long enough for Ram to be heard without being excused by it.

The nurse gave the small sign that they needed to stop.

Ram saw it.

"Aage bataaoonga," he said. "Jitna mera bachpan hai, woh main bata sakta hoon. Jo mujhe baad mein pata chala, usse alag."

Rudra said he understood the limit.

This time he did not have to pretend the answer had been nothing.

---

They ended the call and kept the rest the team had asked them to take.

The night in Prayagraj was staffed, interrupted by the necessary checks, not by another attempt to call the form or test whether a father could name a broken seal whole again. Rudra slept in uneven intervals. When he woke frightened, the nurse answered. He did not have to wait for Vedant's injured arm to become the only thing between him and the room.

By morning, a full night had passed since the family names were spoken, and more than a day since the kavach itself had gone.

Day thirty-eight began with the same supports and a fresh clinical review.

The chest discomfort had eased further. The wrist and ankle remained injuries with their own instructions. Continued observation and the care plan stayed in place; no travel permission arrived attached to a quieter night.

Rudra answered what hurt now, listened to what he could do and asked when another short call might be possible. The team allowed it after the review and rest, with help available and no obligation to keep talking if either patient tired.

He wanted to hear about the house which had existed in his father's memory before Rudra's own house began.

---

In Varanasi, Ram had woken before the next call without asking to be lifted out of bed.

His old habit offered its usual answer to a difficult morning: stand, do something with the hands, make what was wrong into a piece of work which would obey enough attention.

He looked at the dressed site by his chest and let the habit pass without mistaking it for permission.

Rukmini came after her own morning care, the shoulder supported. Dadi helped settle the cloth before the call was put where they could hear.

"Kahan se shuru karoon?" Ram asked when Rudra answered.

The boy looked at the curtain, at its lower edge which stopped just above the ordinary floor.

"Jahan se aapko yaad hai."

It was a small request. For Ram, who had spent so many years choosing the portion of his past which could be useful as a warning, it was almost a different way of being asked to speak.

---

The first place Ram could remember had a low shelf he could not reach.

Not the whole house. Only a few things had kept that early clearness: the shelf, an earthen pot with a dark damp ring beneath it, the drag of somebody's cloth at his cheek when he was lifted out of the way.

He could not honestly put them in order. The shelf might have belonged to a later room than the one in which he first learned to answer his name. Childhood had kept sensations more faithfully than dates.

"Paani maangta tha," he said into the phone. "Khud nahi le paata tha."

Rudra listened.

His father, who had made another person's dependence so difficult for so many years, could remember a life which began by asking to have a cup brought down.

---

The pot was cool even when the air in the room was not.

Ram remembered standing with both hands round the small cup, drinking too quickly and being told to slow down. He remembered spilling some across his shirt and then insisting he had not, though the wet place was travelling towards his stomach in full view.

A woman laughed. She was one of the people who raised him, and the laughter had the familiar impatience of someone who would have to change the shirt she had already changed once that morning.

"Paani ne kiya hoga," she said. "Tu toh bilkul sookha hai."

He looked down at the dark cloth, betrayed by the evidence on his own body.

She put out her hand. He gave her the cup.

The memory contained no great kindness which the district would have known how to announce. She had simply fetched another shirt, complained about the washing and let the child stay close while she finished what she was doing.

He had belonged to her day's work without having to justify the space he took in it.

---

There was another memory of broken clay.

He was bigger then, old enough to be sent across the room with something breakable and too impatient to understand that this was a kind of trust. He could not now recall whether he had caught his foot or turned too quickly. He remembered the sound, his empty hands and the wonderful hope that if he stayed very still the pieces might not quite be his fault.

The man who came to the doorway looked at the floor first.

"Hato. Nange pair mat."

Ram moved. Only after the sharp pieces were out of reach did the scolding begin.

He had wanted the scolding finished quickly. At some point he asked whether he was to go.

"Kahan?"

He could not answer. He knew only the old fact which had been told about him: he had been found. Something found might be put elsewhere if it proved too much trouble to keep.

The man looked at the little face above the broken pot.

"Yahin rehna hai. Isi liye sambhaalna seekh."

Then he gave him the small ordinary part of cleaning which would not cut his feet.

The child disliked that part almost as much as the fear which had come before it. He was hungry, ashamed and sure somebody else could have done the work faster.

He also remembered being called for food afterwards.

There had been no decision about whether he still belonged by the time the meal was served.

---

On the phone, Ram paused after telling Rudra about the pot.

Rukmini kept her eyes on him, not on a version of the past in which every person who had raised him must have been perfect. They had been a household. People got cross. Work went wrong. A child made himself impossible at precisely the moment someone needed him quiet.

"Unhe pareshan bhi karta tha," Ram said.

"Aap?" Rudra asked.

The surprise was so unguarded that his father heard the small opening inside it.

"Haan. Main bhi bachcha tha."

It ought not to have been new information. Rudra had never been given much with which to imagine it.

---

As he grew, the rooms became places where other people's pain arrived.

A member of the Vedic Kul could be family at breakfast and a person somebody urgently needed by afternoon. Cloth had to be clean. Water had to be carried. In that household, an adult would find a small task he could do without making the patient's hurt into his lesson.

He was sent for things before he was trusted to help with them.

Sometimes he disliked it. The person in pain took the attention he wanted, or the meal was late, or an adult sent him away just when he thought he was old enough to stay.

There were evenings when he was given the part no one would sing about: washing, fetching, waiting until the hand doing the work could be freed to take the cup.

Those evenings had not changed the place of his birth.

They had made another belonging real, one ordinary repetition at a time.

In Prayagraj, Rudra watched a nurse carrying clean things past the opening of his bay. He had come to the call wanting a map of another world. His father was telling him about the people who had first made this one large enough to keep him.

---

"Isliye aap zakhm—"

Rudra stopped before making the question into an accusation against the injury still keeping his father in bed.

Ram completed it for him.

"Vedic Kul se seekha. Jinhone paala, unhi se."

He was not describing another name for the combat force with which he had fought at the ghat. Rudra had watched those hands try two different kinds of work, and fail at more than one of them. Until now he had treated every thing his father could do as something a father simply arrived knowing.

"Asuran hone se nahi?"

"Nahi. Janm ka Kul alag. Yeh unke beech seekha."

Rudra looked at his own fingers.

He had not meant to ask the next question. It was there anyway, in the memory of an injured workman's arm and a grip which had been meant to stop a weapon, not hurt the person holding it.

"Mujhmein?"

Ram heard what the little word was trying to reach.

"Woh tumhein mujhse viraasat mein nahi mila."

The boy kept looking at the fingers.

The name Asuran had not caused them to become stronger overnight. Hearing Vedic did not give them a way to repair what strength had damaged either.

---

For one breath Rudra wanted to argue.

It seemed unjust that the man could have learned a way of helping and passed down the part which so easily broke things. The thought was childish, and painful enough that recognizing its childishness did not make it disappear.

Ram did not tell him to hide it.

"Maine jo seekha, woh sab tumhare khoon mein aa jaata, aisa nahi hua," he said.

"Toh aapko dekh ke—"

"Tumhare zakhm ko dekhna hoga. Tumhe kya ho raha hai, woh. Mera naam bataane se ilaaj tay nahi ho jaayega."

Rudra looked towards the nurse by the opening. She was helping another member of staff with something beyond his bed. She had not waited for Daavansh to become a word she knew before deciding that his ankle padding needed moving.

The old fantasies came back, stripped of their useful disguises: if only the force could be held differently, if only a better count could put back what a frightened hand had done.

He left his hand where it was.

This answer was about a life his father had lived, not a new exercise left for the son to attempt alone.

---

"Unhone aapko doosra bana diya?" he asked after a while.

The question surprised Ram.

"Kaise?"

"Asuran nahi rahe? Jab unke saath—"

"Asuran raha. Unka bhi raha."

The answer travelled the line without requiring either family to become a lesser one.

Rudra had heard enough of people using a Kul to tell somebody where to stand, whose road to take or which body they owed their support to. His father had just described something which would not fit into that demand: a child with one birth and a real place in another household.

"Dono sach?"

"Haan."

He did not call the word a solution to every question about bloodlines. It was enough that it was true of the life being discussed.

In Varanasi, Rukmini watched Ram lean back into his support when the nurse asked. The man who had been raised by healers still needed to let another person's judgment matter at his own bed.

---

Rudra wanted to ask why none of this had been said at the kitchen table.

The question was still there. It had not grown smaller because the story contained kindness.

He asked it.

Ram did not say that a child's ordinary happiness had required ignorance of the people who had made his father's happiness possible.

"Darr ko ghar se bahar rakhna chaaha," he said. "Bahut kuchh saath hi bahar rakh diya."

"Darr toh andar tha."

"Haan."

The small agreement was difficult to hear. Rudra had expected an argument he could push against. His father had left him with a true thing which did not tell him what to do with the hurt.

"Main abhi sab theek nahi bol sakta," he said.

"Maanga nahi."

Rudra listened closely to that answer before accepting it for the moment.

---

When the call ended for rest, he did not ask for another one at once.

The morning had given him more than a strange name. A place of birth, an account of being found, the beginning of remembered childhood and the source of a healing his own body had not inherited. His father had told him what he remembered and where memory stopped. For this morning, Rudra could listen without making the gaps disappear.

He told the clinician the family-history information which might matter and kept the uncertainties clear. The care plan was reviewed on what the team could actually assess; it did not become an invitation to demonstrate either inheritance.

Later, when the nurse helped move the blanket clear of the supported leg, Rudra thanked her before she had to ask what still hurt.

A person could be unable to heal and still answer when somebody needed to know where the pain was.

---

After the afternoon rest and check, the team allowed another short call. Rudra asked to speak to the girls.

The ward contacted Sitara first. She checked the girls' care interval and asked whether they wanted the call now. They did. A helper set the phone where Aadhya did not have to grip it and Ira could listen without using the ordinary eye for a long screen interval.

Rudra heard the two familiar voices before attempting the nouns.

He told them that his father had spoken, that he came from Pataal and had been born into the Asuran Kul. That Daavansh and Ram were the same person. He gave the rest slowly: an infant found in Varanasi, raised among the Vedic Kul, a childhood which belonged to Earth even though the birth did not.

Neither girl interrupted to make the story easier for him to finish.

"Isliye nahi bataaya tha kal," he said afterwards. "Khud samajh nahi aa raha tha."

"Ab aa raha hai?" Aadhya asked.

"Thoda. Phir nahi."

"Theek. Humein bhi time lagega."

She gave him the honest answer without making him comfort her for needing it.

---

There was a scrape of cloth near Aadhya's end of the line.

She asked the helper to move the support, waited while it was settled and came back without pretending the interruption had not happened. Her left hand still needed the prescribed care; the right fingertips had not recovered because the conversation was important.

"Bol," she said.

Rudra had spent the pause deciding whether the story ought to have made him more impressive. Hearing that one ordinary word undid the effort.

He told them about the broken pot.

A child who thought he might be sent away, then disliked being made to help clean the safe pieces because he was hungry. A boy who spilled water on his shirt and denied it while the stain spread.

"Unhone khud bataaya?" Aadhya asked.

"Haan."

She was quiet for a while.

"Aise kabhi socha nahi unko."

"Maine bhi nahi."

They let that be something they had discovered together rather than a reason to rush towards a kinder judgment of every morning which had come afterwards.

---

Ira had listened with the covering still in place.

"Unki yaad aur jo unhe bataaya gaya, alag rakha?" she asked.

Rudra told her how Ram had answered about being lifted from the ground. No memory claimed. A finding told to him, followed by a childhood he could speak about himself.

"Achha," she said.

The word was not a certificate that an ancient history had now been proved by a phone call. It was relief at hearing somebody resist a perfect story where a truthful one had edges.

Rudra knew the difference well enough not to ask her to make it more flattering.

"Likhegi?"

"Tu chahe toh chhota sa. Tere liye. Bhejna kisi ko nahi."

He thought about it. Then he said yes to a few plain lines, the names and the distinction about childhood memory, with no drawing of the signature and no claim to a route.

The helper took the short dictation rather than making Ira strain the eye to write through a tiring interval. The note stayed private. A spoken account had acquired a careful reminder, not a new ancestral document which could answer more than its speaker had said.

---

"Vedic unka Kul nahi tha?" Aadhya asked.

"Janm ka nahi. Unke log the."

Ira's face turned towards the speaker.

"Toh unka tha bhi."

Rudra thought of the way his father had said both answers were true.

"Haan."

She did not need to supply a missing file about herself to understand that much. Sarokaar had become real through people who knew her and work she had chosen to do. Its reality did not depend on pretending every question about birth had been answered.

For a moment she wanted to say all of that.

Then she heard the tiredness in Rudra's voice and kept only the part which belonged in this conversation.

"Dono ko rakhne ki jagah ho sakti hai. Ek ko jhooth bolna zaroori nahi."

Rudra looked at the phone as if there might be a little more space around it than before.

"Mujhe abhi Ram naam sunna achha lagta hai," he admitted. "Pata nahi iska matlab—"

"Wahi naam suna hai tune saari zindagi," Aadhya said. "Usko chhod ke sunna shuru karna zaroori hai kya?"

He had not been given a new order. He had been released from one he had made for himself.

---

When he tired, he said so.

They ended before the call became something he had to complete for their benefit. Sitara kept the next clinical contact where the helper could find it and let the girls rest too.

The private note remained with the few facts actually spoken. No old dream had been recruited to authenticate Pataal; no absent childhood paper had suddenly provided the same answer for Ira.

In Prayagraj, Rudra asked whether the nurse could put the phone back beside the charger. He left the college page lowered and the hand open on the sheet.

He had told the story to people who mattered without being required to know all of it first.

That evening there would be another question for his father. For the moment, there was a little room in which the answers already given could stop sounding like separate alarms.

---

Rukmini heard about the private note during the evening call.

Rudra told her before the account could grow a second life in somebody else's version. He had asked for the girls to be told. A few names and limits had been written for him, not sent to a group or turned into a public claim.

"Tumne chaaha tha?" she asked.

"Haan."

"Toh theek."

She did not ask to read it before he could decide whether it said what he meant.

The phone remained supported. Ram had finished the care which needed quiet before joining the short call; the nurse was close enough to hear if speaking became too much. In Prayagraj, Rudra had rested since the afternoon. The shirt sat comfortably round the accessible dressing, and the wrist and ankle remained where the team had put them.

The day had been quieter than the night of the shatter.

It had not restored the thing which shattered. At the collar, the faint gold was still part of the exposed signature, not a border joining round it.

---

Rudra told his father what the girls had heard.

He wanted to make sure he had not joined the pieces wrongly while trying to say them aloud. Born in one Kul. Found as an infant in Varanasi. Raised by the Vedic people. Childhood memories of Earth, the finding itself told to him.

Ram confirmed the account without adding an impressive detail to reward being listened to.

"Unke beech rehke bhi Daavansh the?" Rudra asked.

"Haan. Ram bhi tha."

The boy looked towards the edge of the curtain. He could remember thinking that a name ought to sort the memories into true and false. Instead he had been given more people whose lives would not fit into that division.

"Unhe bura nahi lagta tha? Aapka doosra naam?"

Ram considered the question before answering.

"Mera wahaan janm hua tha. Unke ghar rehne se woh baat badli nahi. Mujhe unka hone ke liye apne janm ko jhooth bolna nahi pada."

He did not turn one household's life into a promise about every person who would hear the name now.

Rudra did not ask him to.

---

In Varanasi, the blanket had slipped a little at Ram's good side.

He began to pull it back, stopped when it tugged at a sore place and let the nurse help. The phone caught the little ordinary exchange. Rudra heard his father say thank you before coming back to the question.

For some reason that made it easier to speak.

"Mujhe abhi lagta hai main kisi ka naam galat bol doonga. Kisi ek ko chhota kar doonga."

"Kiska?"

"Jo aapke the. Dono taraf."

There was a pause at the far end.

Ram had prepared himself for questions about strength, the world below, the things which might be able to recognize a signature no longer hidden. He had not prepared for his son worrying that remembering one family might wrong the other.

"Mujhe paalne walon ko kam mat samajhna," he said.

"Nahi samajh raha."

"Aur jahan se main aaya, unko bhi sirf ek naam mat rehne dena."

Rudra heard the change. Until then, Asuran had been a difficult word about himself. His father had just used it to leave room for other people.

He waited, without bringing a pen or a diagram into that room.

---

The remembered household had become easier to imagine.

A cold pot. A shirt. Somebody cross enough to scold a child and careful enough to move the sharp pieces first. He could hear how a life was made there, not by a single rescue which demanded gratitude forever but by being kept through days when the rescued child was inconvenient.

The beginning of that life still contained a missing part.

A child had arrived from somewhere.

Not a warrior with a declared reason for exile. A body too small to choose whether to remain in one world or appear in another, to keep one name or answer to a new one.

"Jo pehle the," Rudra said. "Aapko janm dene waale."

Ram kept his eyes on the phone.

"Unhone aapko—"

The word left him unwillingly.

"Chhod diya tha?"

---

The nurse at Ram's bed settled the blanket and stepped back.

He could hear the young voice waiting in another ward. Yesterday that voice had asked whether he was still the same father. Today it had found a way to ask about the people who existed before he could become one.

He did not have their lifting hands in his own memory. He would not invent the touch now because the boy needed something gentle with which to imagine them.

He knew what the separation had been for.

Rukmini stayed beside him. The good hand on her lap did not move towards the phone to finish his sentence or make the answer less painful in advance.

"Nahi," Ram said.

In Prayagraj, Rudra watched the screen, though there was no picture on it which could supply the missing faces.

"Unhone mujhe pheka nahi tha," his father said. "Unhone mujhe bheja tha."

The distinction was a door opening onto a darkness quite different from abandonment.

Rudra waited to learn what a parent had been trying to send a child away from.
