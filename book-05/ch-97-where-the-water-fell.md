# CHAPTER NINETY-SEVEN — WHERE THE WATER FELL

---

> *"He did not begin with the lesson he taught.*
> *He began with the water he put in the wrong place."*
> *— Ira's later register, opening of Vedant's account*

---

"Jab main galat jagah dekh raha tha," Vedant said.

He took his hand from the door and came back far enough to ask whether Rudra wanted the short answer now or the rest when he had rested. There was no penalty hidden in choosing later.

"Abhi bataaiye. Thoda," Rudra said.

Vedant drew up a chair. He placed his own forearm comfortably before beginning. The unfinished account of the journey stayed beside them; he did not turn it over to draw a diagram which would make a story into the next exercise.

"Tumhaare pitaji se seekh raha tha tab ki baat hai. Paani chalaa leta tha. Kisne dekha, kaise dikha, uska hisaab kaafi tha. Paani kiske liye tha, woh chhoot gaya."

Rudra looked at the water by the bed, then at him.

"Koi ghaayal tha?"

"Nahi. Pyaasa tha. Aur main bahut prabhaavit karna chaahta tha."

There was a wryness in the answer which kept the memory from dressing itself for a battle it had never been. No one had died because the younger Vedant wanted to be impressive. The mistake was allowed to matter without being given a body to justify its place in the telling.

---

There had been a basin of water and an ordinary metal tumbler.

Vedant remembered the shine on one side of the tumbler more clearly than the rest of the place. It had stood near the things Ram was washing after food. There was wet stone where the washing had been done, dry ground a little farther off, a cloth which had been folded and put out of the splash.

Ram asked him to fill the tumbler.

Vedant could already move water from the basin. He was not remembering the discovery of a gift or the day his teacher had given him one. He was remembering the less flattering interval after he could do something, when he wanted every use of it to testify that he could do it.

He brought up a narrow curve.

The water held together above the rim, caught the light and went where he intended. He remembered being pleased before it had arrived. The pleasure made him lengthen the path a little. There was room for a smoother turn, a cleaner fall, a last small flourish before the stream reached the cup.

Ram was still occupied with what he had been washing.

Vedant had decided that did not matter. In another moment Ram would see. The cup was there. The water was there. The skill was his.

It had seemed an almost complete account.

---

"Aap dekh rahe the ki unhone dekha ya nahi?" Rudra asked.

"Haan. Aur soch raha tha main paani dekh raha hoon."

Vedant stopped to move the chair a little. He did it with the unhurt hand, without making the movement part of the remembered performance. Rudra watched the present man settle again and found it easier to imagine that a younger version had once wanted praise badly enough to mistake wanting for attention.

He had known Vedant could be wrong about an ankle's timing on a page. That was a small correction between two people who agreed on what the correction was for. He had not often thought of the mentor before the assurance had settled into him, at a time when the opinion of the person watching could crowd out the actual work.

"Phir?" he asked.

"Unhone haath badhaaya."

---

Ram reached for the tumbler before the stream arrived.

There was nothing deceptive about the movement. He had asked for a drink. He was taking the cup he intended to drink from. Its position changed as an object changes when the person who needs it begins to use it.

Vedant's elegant curve went to the place where it had been.

The first water struck stone. A little splashed Ram's trouser hem. Vedant saw the miss, felt the hot stupidity of it and tried to correct the fall without first letting the stream stop.

That was the part he remembered most clearly: not the spill, but the wish to repair it quickly enough that the other man would never have to consider the mistake complete.

He pulled the curve round.

Now there was more water in motion near a person whose attention had been on receiving a drink. The younger Vedant could still tell himself he was fixing the first error. The water at his feet had already made another fact of the ground.

Ram set the tumbler down, away from the spreading wet.

"Roko," he said.

The command was not difficult to understand. It was difficult to obey before the demonstration looked right.

Vedant stopped the stream.

Some of it fell back into the basin. Some had already fallen elsewhere and remained there. The distinction had irritated him, though he would not have admitted it then. If he could gather the spill, make one clean movement and finish with the tumbler full, the attempt might still be remembered as a correction accomplished rather than an error seen.

He asked to try again.

Ram looked at the wet stone between them.

"Pehle yeh."

Vedant looked too. He had thought he was waiting to be told how to improve the curve. There was no curve now. There was a place where someone could put a foot without knowing that the ground had changed.

"Utha deta hoon," he said.

He meant the water, with the same skill which had put it there.

Ram asked how the arm felt.

The question was unwelcome because it had an answer. Holding the narrow stream while trying to correct it had left an ache which Vedant was pretending belonged only to concentration. He could still make another attempt. He was less certain that he could explain why another attempt was necessary before anyone was allowed to want a drink.

He answered about the ache.

Ram put the cloth down within reach.

It was a cloth. Not a sign that water had rejected him, not a lesser instrument awarded to a disappointing student. It had been folded before either of them knew there would be a spill. Somebody had expected that wet ground might need drying.

The younger Vedant hated how ordinary the answer was.

---

"Aapko laga sazaa hai?" Rudra asked.

"Laga tha."

"Thi?"

Vedant considered the question rather than borrowing certainty from the fact that he was older now.

"Us din? Nahi. Paani gira tha. Raasta sookhna tha. Par mujhe lag raha tha meri vidya chheen li."

He glanced towards the present glass. There was no water moving around it. Rudra did not find that lack disappointing. The room had enough things which needed placing carefully without a demonstration being added to them.

"Gilaas toh bhar sakte the," the boy said.

"Haath se."

The answer surprised him into a smile.

Vedant smiled back, briefly. The younger man in the memory had taken longer to discover the possibility.

---

Ram filled the tumbler from the basin without asking the water to do anything remarkable.

He drank, then put it where a hand could reach it again. The person who had asked for water had received it. That part of the task was finished, though it did nothing to repair Vedant's feeling about the attempt.

Vedant dried the wet place. The cloth did not make every edge dry at once. He had to look at the ground instead of at the man whose opinion he wanted to regain. He found a thin run of water behind the basin and another near the place where his own foot had been.

There had been more of the spill than his first ashamed glance had admitted.

Ram moved the washing away from the remaining damp. He did not stand over the work reciting what the younger man ought to understand. That absence of a speech was almost more uncomfortable. Vedant could not prove he was learning by repeating the right words while failing to see the next wet patch.

When the way was dry enough to use, they left it alone.

There was no immediate second performance.

---

"Bas?" Rudra asked.

"Us waqt bas. Haath aaraam maang raha tha. Unka kaam bhi tha."

The fact had the ungenerous shape which real life sometimes gave to a lesson. The teacher did not exist only to wait beside the student's unfinished feeling. There had been food to clear away, washing to finish, people whose day was not an audience for the young man's progress.

"Agle din phir mauka mila?"

"Mila. Par us tak main jo soch raha tha, woh bhi kahaani ka hissa hai."

Vedant looked at him, not at the clock alone.

Rudra had begun holding his head in the position which made listening seem easier while the rest of him tired underneath it. He noticed when the mentor asked about comfort. The ankle wanted easing. The room, which had briefly contained the wet stone of another day, contained his own supported legs again.

"Yahin rokte hain," he said.

Vedant did not make the unspoken remainder precious by refusing to leave. He helped him call the staff member needed for the adjustment and waited until help had come. Then he left the boy to rest.

The old spill did not continue spreading because the account had been paused.

Rudra lay looking at the ceiling. He had asked when a teacher learned to stop. He had heard about a student who had first wanted very much not to.

It was an answer. The part he had not expected was how familiar that student had felt.

After the rest, the nurse helped him change into a clean shirt without making the protected arm do the work of getting through it.

The collar sat badly on one side. Rudra saw it in the dark face of the phone when it was put back within reach and thought of Dadi's promise to deal with it after he had seen the world. He could have asked for it to be straightened. For the moment he left it.

There were other things he wanted to put in order first.

He asked Sitara, when she had a free interval later, to carry a question to Vedant: could the account he had just heard be shared with the other three, or did he want it kept between them?

The answer returned with her. Vedant was willing to tell them himself at a time which fitted their care. Rudra did not have to become the sole keeper of a teacher's remembered embarrassment. Nor did he have to turn it into common property before asking.

He put the phone down.

He was less settled by the story than he had expected.

---

When Aadhya came for a brief visit after her own rest, she noticed him looking at the collar.

"Dadi ko mat bhejna," she said. "Photo."

"Woh bina photo ke jaan lengi."

"Phir bachne ka kya fayda."

She sat where the hands could remain supported. The joke left the room a little easier without requiring the thing underneath it to become easy too.

Rudra told her that he had heard something about his father as a teacher. Vedant would tell the actual account himself if they wanted to hear. He was not going to repeat it while the teller was absent.

What he wanted to say was about the discomfort it had left in him.

"Achhi baat thi," he said. "Sunke achha bhi laga. Phir gussa aaya ki achha kyun laga."

Aadhya did not answer at once. Her ordinary expression made room for an answer which had not been rehearsed around the correct way to comfort him.

"Achha laga toh jo bura laga tha, woh kat gaya?"

"Nahi."

"Toh?"

He looked towards the window. The little question was not an instruction to stop feeling the difficulty. It asked whether he was trying to make one true thing cancel another because carrying both was untidy.

"Lagta hai kisi ko choose karna padega," he said. "Kaunse waale Papa sahi hain."

"Dono mein se koi photo nahi hai na. Crop karke rakh do."

He laughed once, unwillingly, then went quiet.

The father who could give one student a useful lesson did not prove that the son had been wrong about the costs of his own childhood. The childhood did not mean every useful thing learned from the father had to be declared false. It was a harder arrangement than choosing one man and throwing the other version away.

He did not need to solve it while Aadhya's hands waited for their next treatment.

"Theek hai," he said. "Abhi dono."

"Abhi jitna hai."

She did not reach to fix the collar. After a while he asked the nurse for help with it, and Aadhya looked away to give the small task its privacy.

---

Later he returned to his own account of Pataal.

Not the account of the assisted movement. That had a remaining sentence which could wait until the next reading with Vedant. This was the part he wanted kept beside the photographs: what he could say about having been there, without demanding the pictures say it for him.

A short quiet interval was available. Sitara helped him put the words down and stopped when he needed the support changed. The account did not have to be long enough to justify the work of making it.

He named what was known. A threshold, worn smooth. An opening without a roof behind it. A way between broken fronts. The sound of air passing through hollow stone. Four friends who had taken different things from the same short line.

He left the unknowns in place.

He had not identified a household, found an heirloom or learned the view beyond the inspected turn. He could not say how every person in the realm had lived or where every survivor might be. Those absences did not have to be disguised as mysteries the stone had promised him a later right to solve.

Then he dictated what he had almost omitted.

"Mera Kul Asuran hai. Isliye yahaan aana mere liye alag tha. Par yahaan jo log rahe, woh sirf meri kahaani ke liye nahi rahe the."

Sitara read it back without making the word Kul louder than the rest.

The line did not command a future battle. It did not certify that he was now a different sort of body, cured of fear and injury by finding a word to stand behind. It was a fact of relation and an attempt to leave other lives their own size.

He asked for one final sentence beneath it.

"Main wahaan gaya tha. Laut aaya hoon. Rishta wahin chhodke nahi aaya."

When it was read to him, he did not ask for it to sound grander.

He asked for his name to be put with it: Rudra Vashisht. The name he had answered to before he knew where his father's family had come from. He did not have to leave that name on the Earth side of a passage in order to make the relation honest.

Sitara added it without a title. No handwriting was demanded from the braced wrist. Hearing his name beside the imperfect account was enough to make him responsible for having said it, not an authority on all the lives it could not describe.

Ira heard the account during a later quiet interval which she chose for listening.

Sitara read it with Rudra's permission. The two photographs stayed on his phone; Ira did not need to look at them again to certify that she had been present. She asked for the sentence about the return to be read once more.

Rudra watched the ordinary eye remain closed against the light. He was learning not to take the absence of a visible response as an absence of attention.

"Rakhna hai?" he asked.

"Tumhaare liye?"

"Apne paas. Mere naam se. Jo maine kaha."

She said she would keep a copy with his permission, beside her own qualified notes, not as a correction which replaced them. She would ask before passing it farther. Her name on a register would not make his words more certain than they were.

Then she asked why the chair had almost disappeared from his account.

He looked down at it.

The first reaction was irritation. He had spent the visit refusing to make the body less injured for the sake of being there. Must the first thing anyone learned about his relationship to a realm be how he had been helped across a lip?

Ira did not say it must.

"Bas kahaan se dekha, woh tumhaari baat ka hissa hai," she said. "Maine jahaan nahi dekha, woh meri baat ka hissa hai. Dono ko chhupaana kyun?"

He sat with the objection until it no longer sounded like pity.

They added that he had viewed the short line from the assisted chair, with the brief supported stand at the outlook. Not a medical inventory. A position in the world from which the seeing had happened.

The addition did not make the threshold less worn or the unknown lives smaller. It made the boy telling the account less likely to vanish behind a version of himself who had needed nothing.

---

Veer came when his own care allowed, in time to hear Rudra say that the account was finished for now.

"Bas?" he asked.

"Haan. Aaj ke liye."

"Achha hai. Socha ab poori sadak ka tender niklega."

Ira smiled without opening the eye. Rudra made a rude gesture with the good hand which remained safely brief and close to his lap.

The joke held a little of the relief he had not found a serious way to admit. Nothing had to be commissioned because they had reached the place. No next expedition had to be promised so the first could count. A relationship with lost people was not a contract for four recovering bodies to undertake every task left undone.

There would be questions later. They had not run out of things to learn. But the unknown beyond the turn could remain unknown through a meal, a treatment, a night.

Veer moved the aid closer before the knee became uncomfortable enough to make the reaching awkward. Ira said she wanted quiet soon. Rudra asked Sitara whether the copy could be made later rather than using the rest of the interval merely because a phone was available.

It could.

The three left the account where it was.

---

Before the next rest, Rudra asked whether the others wanted to hear Vedant's story when a suitable time could be found.

He gave only what he had permission to offer: an ordinary incident from when the mentor was learning from Ram, not a promised secret about any of their bloodlines. There had been water, a mistake and a useful reason to stop.

Veer wanted to hear it. Ira did too, if listening could be fitted around the eye's rest rather than made its replacement. Aadhya's answer came through Sitara after she was asked separately: yes, after her own care, without turning it into a practical test for the hands.

Those were not four people agreeing to a lesson because a name had appeared above them in a family chart.

They were different people choosing a question they could bring to a teacher.

Rudra realized he had imagined the story of a mentor as something received once one had become strong enough to deserve the rest of the training. This one had begun with a cup which the mentor failed to fill. There had been no demand that he stand unaided to hear the answer.

He wanted to know more about how a person learned to live after being seen making a small mistake, before every mistake had an audience large enough to turn it into a legend.

He could ask.

---

The account went to his parents later with his own permission and a plain message: this was as much as he could put into words now. Ira received the private copy he had offered her when she was ready to keep it. Neither sending included a request to make it public.

Rudra listened to the last sentence once more before the phone was put away.

He had gone. He had returned. The connection had not been left behind.

The words did not close Pataal around his father's past or his own new experience. They closed the need to keep proving that the first visit had actually happened.

For the first time since the journey began, he could let an account be finished without mistaking it for the end of the place.

In Varanasi, the story of the light was still being improved by people who had not agreed on what they had seen.

At a tea stall, a man said the golden colour meant the boy could no longer be hurt. Another asked why, if light settled such matters, their lane still needed a mason. The first said he was speaking of bigger things. The second pointed out that the hole outside his shop was big enough.

Someone remembered the tournament differently from both of them. Someone else supplied a result which belonged to another bout. The cups emptied while the accounts grew more assured.

A woman collecting tea for the people repairing a shutter waited through the argument and asked for the change.

The stallholder gave it to her. One coin had rolled beneath a bench; a child fetched it before the talk could make the missing amount symbolic.

There had been damage. There had been a tournament. People had seen an alarming colour and found explanations with more confidence than evidence. At that stall, none of the speaking men had a first-hand account of the recovering boy's present day.

The mason still needed paying. The shutter still needed its last hinge. The city kept a little of the light in its mouth and went on with work which light had not finished for it.

---

Elsewhere in Varanasi, Rukmini read her son's account after her own care.

She left the screen on the sentence about returning. Ram was resting, and she did not wake him to make the moment complete. The copy would be there when he wanted to read it.

For a while her fingers rested against her own pulse. She had not known, before he went, how often she would want an update he could not safely send from wherever he was. Now there were words made after his return, his chosen words, not a promise delivered to quiet her before leaving.

She let the phone rest beside her.

When Ram stirred later, she asked if he wanted the message now or after water. He asked for water first. She called for the help they needed, and the account waited without being lost.

---

In Mirzapur, Vedant came back for the short reading they had left unfinished.

Rudra had eaten. The support had been checked, and there was time before the next rest. The mentor asked whether that time was still available for this. Rudra said yes.

They finished the sentence about the last look.

The chair had been turned at the broad place. Rudra had not been expected to keep twisting to look behind him. The little change had let him see without spending a movement the injured body could not comfortably give.

Vedant read the whole practical account once more. It did not become an invitation to repeat the trip tomorrow. Rudra heard the parts which belonged to the people doing the assisting as well as the patient being assisted. He said it was accurate now.

The pen stopped moving.

For a moment both sheets lay beside each other: the plain account of needed help, and the boy's attempt to say what the place had meant. Neither could do the other's work. He was glad he had not tried to keep only the more beautiful one.

"Baaki log sunna chaahte hain," he told Vedant.

"Kal apne-apne kaam ke baad dekh lenge. Jitni der theek ho."

"Kahaani?"

"Kahaani. Haath se kuchh karwaa nahi rahe."

There was warmth in the answer. It left room for a roomful of students who were not ready to perform.

Rudra looked at the hands which had corrected the page. He had learned a little about the person before they became a mentor's hands in his mind. Not a hidden origin, not the answer to every old question about the man, but a younger person's wish to make an error disappear before anybody could see how much wanting had helped make it.

He recognized the wish.

---

"Aapko tab laga tha ki unhone jaldi rok diya?" he asked.

"Haan," Vedant said. "Baad mein jo samajh aaya, woh us waqt nahi aaya tha."

The honesty kept the younger man in the story alive. He had not stood on the wet ground already possessing the wisdom of the man who could tell it now. He had been cross, ashamed, eager to try again before the arm's ache became part of the account.

Rudra did not have to become a grateful student before he was permitted to say that stopping hurt his pride.

He looked from the finished line to the mentor.

The man had listened when the ankle's timing was wrong. He had left a sentence unfinished because the boy was tired, then returned when there was room to finish it. These were things Rudra had seen, not qualities he had to accept because someone older had been appointed above him.

But a useful story was not a promise that its teller could never repeat the mistake in another form. The student who wanted a beautiful result could grow into a teacher who wanted one too.

Rudra asked the question which remained when admiration had stopped doing the work of judgment.

"Aur aap galat ho rahe hon, toh aapko kaun rokega?"
