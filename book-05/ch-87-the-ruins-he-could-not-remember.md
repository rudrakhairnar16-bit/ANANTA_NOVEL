# CHAPTER EIGHTY-SEVEN — THE RUINS HE COULD NOT REMEMBER

---

> *"There were places I inherited only as an absence.*
> *Seeing them would not make me remember them."*
>
> *— Rudra's later notes, before the first journey*

---

"Haan," Ram said. "Wahaan jaane ki taiyaari mein tumhaari madad karoonga. Abhi tumhe le chalne ka waada nahi kar sakta."

Rudra looked at the phone.

He had prepared himself to argue for the wish. The answer left him with the unfamiliar work of explaining it without having to defend his right to want it first.

"Abhi nahi bol raha," he said.

"Suna maine."

Ram took the next breath carefully. At the far end, the nurse checked his position before letting him continue.

"Tumhara ilaaj. Raasta. Wahaan ke haalaat. Sab dekhna padega. Main khud saath chal paaoonga, iska bhi abhi daava nahi karoonga. Par sawaal ko galat bolke band nahi karoonga."

The answer had boundaries which belonged to real things. Not a test of whether the son could become strong enough to stop asking. Not a date hidden behind an order to be patient.

Rudra let the first tightness leave his good hand.

"Wahaan kya milega?"

His father's silence was brief.

"Ujaad," he said. "Bahut kuchh khandhar hai. Jis duniya se mujhe bheja gaya, use bachaa ke nahi rakha gaya."

---

Rudra had known about the killing.

He had not understood how much of his own picture still depended on an intact doorway, a place in which someone could stand and say this is where the child belonged. His mind had been keeping a house ready while his father spoke of people who had not survived to bring its child back.

"Aapka ghar?"

"Main tumhe apne bachpan ke kamre nahi dikha sakta," Ram said. "Mera yaad waala bachpan yahaan hai. Jo wahaan hua, baad mein jaana."

He did not claim a remembered route from one doorstep to another. Nor did he give the boy an address which would turn a whole ruined place into a promise about one particular wall.

"Toh jaake—"

Rudra stopped.

What had he been expecting to do? Recognize a window? Find that his body knew the turn into a lane it had never walked? He had already refused the borrowed faces of the dead. The borrowed house was harder to release because it had seemed such a modest thing to want.

His father waited.

"Phir bhi dekhna hai," Rudra said.

"Haan."

The second yes was not a permission repeated. It was a man hearing what remained of a wish after the comforting part had been taken out of it.

---

"Mujhe kuchh yaad nahi aa jaayega wahaan," Rudra said.

He meant it as a question, then heard that it was not one.

He had never lived there. His father's beginnings did not give his own mind a childhood beneath another sky.

"Tum dekhoge," Ram said. "Jo tumhaare saamne hoga. Yaad hone ka naatak nahi karna padega."

A ruined place would not have to recognize him for the destruction to have been real. His grandfather and grandmother would not become less his people if he could stand beside what was left and feel nothing more useful than the ordinary ignorance of a grandson who had never met them.

He had been frightened of failing even that.

The discovery made him angry in a quiet, tired way. He had found another examination in a place where nobody had yet asked him to perform.

"Main samajhna chahta hoon," he said.

"Samajhne ke liye apne ko yaad dilaana zaroori nahi jo kabhi dekha hi nahi."

---

They ended because the teams asked them to end.

The request had been answered. The unknowns had not been disguised as a journey already arranged. Ram would help with the wish; neither injured man had promised to carry the other out of care because the story had reached a point which felt like a departure.

Rudra asked for the phone to be lowered and told the nurse he was tired. The ankle support was checked before the night settled into its quieter work.

He slept more deeply in the first interval than he expected.

Later he woke thinking of the house he had made up. There had been a courtyard in it, of course. His own imagination had supplied the same square of open sky he had spent years wanting to leave, then placed it in a world he knew almost nothing about.

He let that courtyard go.

Beyond the curtain somebody was speaking softly to a patient who had been awake longer than he had. The voice repeated an ordinary instruction until it was understood, with no anger at the time understanding took.

Rudra listened until he could sleep again.

---

Day forty-two began after a staffed night, not after an unseen journey.

The morning review took the chair trial into account. His chest symptoms had remained quieter; the team still wanted observation and the same protection for the injured wrist and ankle. Another assisted trial would depend on their assessment later, not on his father's answer about Pataal.

After rest, Rudra asked whether he could speak to his mother.

She should hear the wish from him, not from a warning passed around the family before he had found the words for it himself.

---

Rukmini was ready for the call after her own morning care.

The phone stood on the table beside her supported shoulder. Dadi had helped place the water within reach and then moved far enough away to leave the conversation to the two people having it. Ram was resting in the ward, not listening from the next chair.

"Ma," Rudra said. "Maine Papa se Pataal dekhne ko bola. Jab jaana ho sake."

There was a little quiet at the other end.

He waited without improving the sentence into something safer.

"Tum dekhna chaahte ho," she said.

"Haan."

"Woh kya bole?"

He gave her the answer, including the limits. Help with preparing, no departure now, no certainty that Ram could go with him. A ruined place, not a house kept ready for his arrival.

"Mujhe aapse khud kehna tha," he finished.

Rukmini looked at the water. The fingers of her good hand had tightened without moving towards it.

"Achha kiya," she said.

---

"Aapko bura laga?"

"Darr laga."

The honesty was immediate enough to hurt.

"Phir?"

"Phir tumhari baat sun rahi hoon."

She did not deny the fear so he would believe her generous. Nor did she put it in his hands as something he had to cure before he could want anything for himself.

"Main ghar bhoolne ko nahi—"

"Pata hai," she said, then stopped herself. "Tum bolo."

Rudra looked towards the opening of the bay. Somebody carried a tray past it and disappeared into the next ordinary part of the morning.

"Mujhe lagta tha, jo jaanna hai woh sab mere andar se nikalna chahiye. Taaqat hai toh samajh bhi aa jaayega. Ab Papa bol rahe hain, mainne dekha hi nahi. Mujhe kuchh yaad nahi ho sakta."

His voice had grown rougher.

"Phir bhi jaana hai. Kya bacha hai, dekhna hai."

Rukmini let the words reach their own end.

"Ghar yaad rakhne ke liye har doosri jagah se aankh band karna zaroori nahi," she said.

---

She asked whether he imagined leaving as soon as he could reach a chair.

"Nahi. Team ne abhi chalne ko bhi nahi bola."

"Aur tumhare saath jo log honge?"

"Abhi kisi ko kaha nahi."

"Unse poochhna. Unki chot. Unka mann. Sirf tumhara faisla hoga toh unka saath nahi hoga."

Rudra heard the distinction without yet knowing how difficult asking would feel.

He could imagine Veer saying yes before he had finished, could imagine making that quick yes into proof that no one needed any more time. His mother was asking him to leave room for the answer which would cost him more to hear.

"Aapko bhi nahi bolna padega ki chalo," he said.

"Maine apni taraf se kaha hi nahi."

He smiled at the dry precision.

She did too, though neither could see it. She wanted her son alive and near; the wish was not made false by refusing to turn nearness into the condition on which she would love him.

---

He told her about the college page.

Not the whole work. Only that Vedant had read a little and he had wanted it to stop, then wanted to know it would still be there later. Rukmini asked whether the page had been put back in the bag instead of left where somebody might clear it away with a tray.

"Haan, Ma."

"Theek."

"Bas itna? Padhne ko nahi bolengi?"

"Padhoge jab padh paaoge. Kaagaz sambhaalne ko bola hai. Tumse aaj poora college nahi sambhalwaa rahi."

The laugh surprised him. It pulled a little at the chest; he stopped, told the nurse when she checked and let her make sure nothing new had changed before they continued.

Rukmini waited without talking over the check.

"Aap bhi rest karna," he said afterwards.

"Karoongi."

He had heard adults use the future tense as a place to hide things they had no intention of doing. Today she told him when, and who would help her settle.

He believed that small part because it could happen.

---

They ended before either had to make the other ask twice.

Dadi came back to help with the support. Rukmini told her that Rudra wanted to see the place his father came from one day. Not the details of their private call, and not a journey the family had secretly arranged without asking anyone else.

"Abhi?" Dadi asked.

"Nahi. Jab ho sake."

Dadi nodded, adjusted the thing which had been slipping and asked whether Rukmini wanted the water moved closer.

There was comfort in that order of work. The great distance could remain a great distance for the moment. A glass was in the wrong place; somebody could fix that now.

Rukmini drank, then rested as she had said she would.

---

In Mirzapur, Ira was trying to listen to Aadhya read without helping.

It was more difficult than reading had once been. Aadhya would stop at the end of a sentence to decide whether she liked it, and Ira would already be preparing a correction to something which had not been a question. Twice she caught herself. The third time she interrupted, and Aadhya stopped altogether.

"Ab bol hi do."

"Kuchh nahi."

"Tumhaara kuchh nahi bahut bolta hai."

Ira smiled with her eyes closed. The ordinary right eye had reached the limit of what she wanted to ask from it before the next rest; the left remained covered. She could hear the paper being turned without needing to turn the sound into a different kind of sight.

"Woh line chhoti karo," she admitted.

"Meri line hai."

"Isliye poochh rahi hoon."

"Poochha kab?"

They were still arguing over that when Sitara came with the proposed call.

Rudra wanted to tell them a little more of the family account. Had enough rest passed? Did they want it? Aadhya glanced at Ira rather than answering for both. Ira said yes. The team confirmed the short interval, and the paper was put aside.

---

Rudra asked first whether he had already told them about the fight six years before his birth.

"Nahi," Ira said. "Uski baat nahi hui."

He was glad she answered plainly. The days had begun putting information into different small conversations, and he did not want a friend left guessing because he remembered saying a thing somewhere.

He told them what Ram had actually said: the fight with Adhira, the defeat, the injuries, coming home without leaving fear outside. The encounter had happened years before his training, which began when he was one. Knowing the order did not make every lesson or every hurt necessary.

He had no detailed scene to give them. His father's remembered struggle had not played itself inside the call.

Aadhya asked whether the fear explained what Rudra had lived with.

"Kuchh samajh aa raha hai," he said. "Iska matlab jo hua sab zaroori tha, woh nahi."

"Haan."

She did not make him argue the distinction again to earn her agreement.

---

Then he told them he wanted to see Pataal.

The phone stayed on its support while he spoke. Ira's hand lay still; no notebook opened, no mirror supplied a view of the place being named.

"Papa bol rahe hain, khandhar hai," he said. "Unka bachpan yahaan yaad hai. Wahaan ka ghar yaad karke dikhaane jaisa nahi."

Aadhya was quiet for a moment.

"Phir bhi dekhna hai?"

"Haan."

"Theek."

The simplicity annoyed him before he understood it.

"Bas theek?"

"Tu chahta hai main abhi bataa doon wahaan jaake tujhe kya mehsoos hoga?"

He looked towards the curtain.

"Nahi."

"Mujhe bhi nahi pata."

A laugh escaped him, smaller and safer than the one in the morning. He let it end there.

She was not leaving him alone with the wish. She was refusing to furnish it with a response neither of them could honestly promise.

---

"Mainne abhi koi safar tay nahi kiya," he said. "Raasta bhi nahi. Team ne yahaan se nikalne ko nahi bola."

Sitara had remained within call distance without taking the conversation over. When Aadhya needed the hand moved, she helped and waited for the girl's answer before letting the talk continue.

Rudra heard the pause and did not apologize for it on his own behalf.

These were bodies with their own histories of the last weeks, not a waiting set of companions placed beside his father's story until he called them in.

"Tum dono se bhi baat karni hai," he said. "Par pehle khud samajh loon main kya poochh raha hoon."

"Hum apna jawaab de denge jab poochhoge," Ira said. "Hamaari taraf se pehle mat likh lena."

He could hear the smile in it. He could also hear that she meant what she said.

"Nahi likhoonga."

---

After the call, Aadhya asked whether Ira wanted the paragraph read once more.

"Abhi nahi."

"Theek."

The girl beside her had a real belonging she had chosen. She also had questions about her birth for which the empty places in a record were still only empty places. Ram's story had not quietly filled them, and wanting to hear it did not make Ira a person who existed only to keep somebody else's past straight.

Aadhya put the paper down within the helper's reach and asked about the next rest interval instead of recruiting her friend into another attempt at work.

Sitara gave them the answer from the care plan, not from how much determination she thought two nineteen-year-olds ought to have.

In Prayagraj, Rudra had the stand moved away and lay back, tired from having said the same history in his own words.

The girls knew the part he had chosen to tell them now.

He did not have to keep talking until it became a story simple enough for all three of them to feel the same thing about it.

---

There was no departure on the morning of day forty-three.

There was a check before the next assisted movement, another adjustment to the support beneath the ankle and a warning not to confuse a quieter chest with permission to push through the braced wrist. Rudra listened without treating each limit as a postponement invented by somebody who disliked where he wanted to go.

He sat with help again. This time he noticed the moment before tiredness became difficult to admit.

He told the therapist then.

The return to bed was ordinary enough that no one else in the bay stopped what they were doing. He found that easier to bear than praise would have been. He had not climbed out of pain by becoming exceptional. He had said what was happening, and the people working with him had been able to use the answer.

Later, when the team checked whether anything had grown worse, he did not use the success of the morning to improve his reply.

The wrist still ached. The ankle still needed its protection.

They checked both.

---

The next night he slept poorly.

Day forty-four's trial was shorter. He hated that more than he expected, the small reduction feeling like something being taken back, until the therapist asked whether he wanted the care plan to answer yesterday's body or today's.

"Aaj ka," he said, with no grace at all.

"Toh aaj ka bataao."

He did.

Nobody called the shorter interval defeat. Nobody promised that wanting Pataal badly enough would make the following morning longer. He had seen enough real costs to know that a body did not become honest merely because its owner had learned the correct words about being patient.

He was still learning to use them when he was disappointed.

The phone spent most of the afternoon away from his face. The agreed contacts received the care update he permitted; no one turned it into an order for another round of difficult conversations while he was tired.

In the evening he listened to a little ordinary news from Veer, not another verdict on the family history. His friend had his own review and his own rest to get through. They ended before either needed to pretend otherwise.

---

In Varanasi, Ram began measuring the day less often by whether he had been useful to somebody else.

It was not a peaceful habit to acquire. He would wake with an instruction already prepared, find the instruction had no proper destination and have to remain with the fear underneath it instead of making it into a task.

The nurse asked about his pain. He answered. The site was checked. He accepted help with the position which made breathing easier and did not offer a demonstration of how well he could control discomfort.

Rukmini came after her own care, stayed for the period she could manage and left before the shoulder had to insist. Dhruv's agreed visits and the family's nearby arrangement did not replace the shifts of people whose work continued through the night.

Once, Ram began saying he ought to be doing something.

Dadi looked at the cup he had not finished.

"Woh karo," she said.

He looked at her, then at the cup. Rukmini laughed quietly, and for a moment the room contained an irritation so familiar that no one had to explain how they belonged to one another.

Ram drank with help.

The world did not become safe while he did it. He had still needed water.

---

At the tournament ground in Prayagraj, a worker pressed a boot beside the repaired length and watched the edge give.

The organizer had come hoping to reopen that part of the ground. He crouched, saw the movement and straightened without asking the man to stamp a little less hard the next time.

"Abhi nahi," he told the people waiting at the rope.

Somebody complained that the fighting had finished days ago. It had. That did not make the repair hold. The worker wiped the tool, put it aside and asked about the hours already done; the answer concerned payment, not how much more risk the ground could be made to carry before another claim arrived.

The organizer checked those hours and paid what was due for that work. It reduced what remained in the reserve. The other unresolved claims did not vanish because this one could be met.

Beyond the rope, two people retold the final score differently and were corrected by a third. None of them knew what had been said in a family call at a hospital, and the ground's slow repair supplied no answer about anybody's blood.

The damaged length stayed closed.

---

By day forty-five, the quieter stretches in Rudra's breathing had held through further observation.

The team had reduced support only as the checks allowed, watching the periods without it rather than announcing that it had become unnecessary forever. He remained in the hospital. The same injured limbs still set limits on transfers, and a supported chair inside the ward was still the extent of the attempt, not permission to walk out through the receiving desk.

The days had contained actual sleep, care and repeated individual reviews. Nothing had healed in a missing interval because a future journey needed people ready.

When he was rested enough for a short call, he asked for his father.

Not to test whether the answer about Pataal had survived three uneventful days. It had. He had begun to notice another part of the story, one which had been there since Ram said who raised him and which the massacre had temporarily pushed to the edge.

The people in Varanasi had taught the child who arrived.

They had taught him how to mend.

---

At the other end, Ram heard his son ask whether he wanted to talk about something quieter.

"Kya?"

"Jinhone aapko paala. Unke yahaan seekhne ki baat."

Ram's good hand lay open beside the blanket. A small crease from the sheet ran across the palm and disappeared when he moved the fingers.

He could remember those fingers shorter, clumsier, reaching to help before they knew what helping would require.

"Poochho," he said.

---

"Unhone aapko badalne ko kaha tha?" Rudra asked. "Jo aap paida hue the, usse alag banne ko?"

Ram looked at his open hand.

"Asuran hona chhodke nahi paala mujhe," he said. "Aur jo unse seekha, woh mera nahi hua, aisa bhi nahi. Dono the."

The answer did not divide him into a dangerous child and a respectable skill. Rudra had begun to understand how quickly a story could be made to do that: keep the part which mended, put the part which fought somewhere outside the household and call the arrangement belonging.

His father was refusing the division.

"Chhota tha," Ram said. "Kaam dekhna chahta tha. Haath badha deta tha jahaan abhi poochhna chahiye tha."

Rudra smiled a little.

He had trouble imagining the man clumsy, though the broken pot in the earlier memory should already have taught him better. A child's reach came into the account, eager and inconvenient, not the arrival of a fighter whose whole life had been waiting to turn useful.

---

"Daant padti thi?"

"Haan."

"Phir?"

"Phir kaam samjhaate the. Kya karna hai. Kya abhi nahi karna. Kab sirf paas baithna hai."

Ram moved the fingers again, remembering the effort it once took to keep them out of the way.

"Jiska dard hai, uski baat pehle. Mujhe kya karna achha lagega, woh baad mein."

Rudra lay quiet.

The nurse outside the bay answered a question. Somebody had asked to wait before being moved, and the answer was a small adjustment to the work, made without any great declaration about kindness.

He could hear his father describing something which happened in ordinary care all around him.

"Yeh unhone sikhaaya?"

"Haan. Baar-baar. Main jaldi karna chahta tha."

No named state had appeared in the answer. No secret instruction which the son could apply to make the wrist inside its brace stop hurting. His father was talking about the child who learned beside other people, not offering a power in return for understanding his past.

---

Ram could remember the impatience more clearly than he could remember how long each lesson took.

He had wanted the result. A person comfortable, a wound no longer pulling everybody's attention towards it, the pleased moment when somebody could say that what he had done helped. The people raising him had made him remain through the slower parts too.

He gave his son only the part he could honestly give now: not every face or every day, but the repeated work of learning to ask before deciding what another body could bear.

The rest would need room to be told properly.

Rudra did not hear that as a new name being withheld. He had the important part already. The man who trained him had not grown up with only one language for pain.

He had learned another.

---

For a moment the son wanted to set the two men apart.

The child at a Vedic household's work, being made to wait. The father in the courtyard, unable to let his own child stop. If they could remain different people, one history would not have to make the other harder to bear.

They were the same person.

That was what Ram had said when the second name first arrived. Ram and Daavansh. The boy who had been sent away and the man who had stayed. The hands which had learned to mend were the hands which had made a frightened child believe that crying meant another part of him was not ready.

Rudra looked down at the support beneath his wrist.

He had wanted the childhood story to make his father easier to understand. It had. Understanding had not moved in the direction he expected.

It meant the man had once been taught how to hear somebody asking him to stop.

---

The phone was still steady on the stand.

Rudra did not reach for it, did not use the good hand to hide the part of his face he could feel changing. In Varanasi, Ram's nurse stayed close enough to help him. His mother's rest did not have to be broken so she could stand between the next question and its destination.

He knew the call could not go on indefinitely. The team had given them this short interval after actual care and sleep, not a whole evening in which the boy could make the past yield an answer by exhausting the man who carried it.

But he could ask one thing plainly.

He did not need to decide in advance whether the answer would make him kinder or angrier. Nor did he have to pretend the people who raised his father had taught him nothing except how to survive.

"Papa," he said. "Aapko jab rukna aata tha, mere liye kyun nahi ruke?"
