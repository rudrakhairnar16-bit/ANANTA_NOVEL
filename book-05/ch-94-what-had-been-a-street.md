# CHAPTER NINETY-FOUR — WHAT HAD BEEN A STREET

---

> *"We wrote courtyard. Then crossed it out.*
> *An absent roof is not an open sky chosen by the people beneath it."*
> *— Ira's later register, correction to the first Pataal entry*

---

The lintel had survived the roof.

Rudra needed a moment to understand the shape. The doorway still divided a wall; it could no longer divide inside from out. Pale light reached a strip of floor which his eye kept putting beneath a ceiling, and behind that strip there was fallen stone, the jagged end of another wall, more light.

He stayed in the chair.

The attendant asked whether the ankle was comfortable. Rudra answered before letting the view become too important for the question. Dhruv stood beside the inspected line. Sitara waited until Ira had found the level place with the guidance she had been given.

Then they were quiet together.

There were no faces in the doorway for Rudra to mistake for people he had inherited the right to remember. There was a shadow beneath one side of the lintel, a line where the lower stone had worn smoother than the rough break above it. It was harder to look at the smooth part.

A break could belong to a terrible moment.

Wear required people to have lived before it.

---

"Aangan tha?" Veer asked.

He had found his own place to sit, keeping the knee in the position which worked. He was looking beyond the doorway at the space between the broken walls.

Aadhya followed his gaze.

"Chhat gayi bhi toh aisa dikhega," she said.

They looked again.

The space had the wrong certainty when named. Courtyard made it sound as though somebody had chosen to let the weather in, arranged a household around an opening to light. A missing room might have the same outline after enough of it fell away.

"Pata nahi," Rudra said.

It was the first account he could honestly give of something in his father's world.

The admission did not send him back through the passage. He could remain here without improving the facts until they were worthy of the distance he had crossed.

Ira asked for a description of the upper edge. Aadhya gave it slowly, one part at a time, while Ira looked only as long as the ordinary eye was comfortable. Aadhya did not turn the guess into a verdict to make the other girl less dependent on words.

"Toh abhi khula hua hissa bolte hain," Ira said. "Aangan kaise tha, woh nahi pata."

Nobody objected.

---

The broad place near the passage gave them room to rest and turn the chair. Beyond it, the inspected line continued a little along what appeared to have been a street. Dhruv showed the part which could be used and the fallen edge which was outside the plan.

They would not go through the doorway. Nobody had examined the structure well enough to put a person beneath that surviving lintel merely because it made a powerful first picture.

Rudra was glad not to be asked to choose between looking brave and looking properly.

From the safe side he could still see the line at the foot of the wall, lower than the threshold, where a shallow channel followed the street. Aadhya noticed it too.

"Paani nikalta hoga," she said.

"Ho sakta hai," Dhruv answered. "Jaisa dikh raha hai, utna hi likhna."

It might have carried water. He did not know who had cleared it, how often it filled or what the people inside had called it. The visible shape offered a possible use, not a memory complete with voices.

Rudra watched a little grit move along the channel in the air.

Somebody might once have been annoyed by that channel, by the work of cleaning it, by someone else putting the swept dirt exactly where it would wash back. He could imagine an ordinary quarrel without claiming to have recovered one.

That was new too: the ruined place making room for an irritation instead of a battle.

---

Ira asked whether she could make a short voice note on her phone.

Her own choice, not a task assigned because the other three wanted their first sight turned into evidence. Sitara checked whether she wanted the phone placed rather than held. She did. The attendant helped settle it once Rudra's chair was secure and everybody had a place to wait.

Ira listened to the recording begin.

"Pehla rukne ka hissa," she said. "Raaste ke paas. Ek darwaaza, uske peeche khula hua floor aur tooti deewaarein. Chhat thi ya nahi—abhi tay nahi kar sakte."

She stopped, heard the last part played back and asked to leave it exactly that way.

There was no name for the family who had used the doorway. No claim that the pale patch on the floor was proof of a particular day. The record contained the place where they were standing and the edge of what they could say about it.

Rudra listened to her voice coming from the small ordinary speaker.

The first account made here did not need an ancient alphabet to matter. It needed the courage to leave a sentence unfinished where knowledge ended, rather than where somebody hoped a better story would begin.

They moved only along the line which had been checked.

Sitara went first, naming an uneven place for Ira before either foot reached it. The attendant brought Rudra's chair slowly over the sound middle, with Dhruv watching the edge where fallen grit had narrowed it. Veer used the aid he had been allowed and kept the knee out of the turn which would have hurt. Aadhya stayed where she could see the ground instead of pushing a wheel with her protected hands.

The distance was small.

From the chair, it felt large enough to change the shape of the first view. What had looked like one broken front became several places divided from one another: a threshold set farther back, a narrow space between walls, another opening beyond the first. The road had joined lives which now appeared separate only because the things between them had fallen.

Rudra watched a patch of floor disappear behind an edge of stone.

There might have been another room there. There might have been a place where somebody put things they were tired of moving. He did not know. The wanting to know was almost painful in its ordinariness.

He had expected the scale of power to meet him first.

Instead there were corners in which people might once have lost ordinary objects and blamed somebody else for putting them away.

---

At the next resting place, Dhruv checked the level stone before Veer sat. The attendant secured Rudra's chair. They gave Ira time to close the uncovered eye and let the descriptions she had heard become a place she could hold without continuing to look.

Rudra asked whether this part had belonged to his father's people.

Dhruv heard the size of the question and answered the part he could.

"Pataal hai. Asuran Kul ka janm yahaan se juda hai. Par is dehleez pe kaun rehta tha, main naam nahi de sakta. Har ghar ka hisaab humaare paas nahi."

The answer had no little locked box waiting at the end of it. There was not a name Dhruv intended to release after the boy endured one more thing. He did not know the household.

Rudra let the unknown household remain.

It was harder than he had expected. If this could become his grandparents' doorway, he could tell his father something shaped like a gift. If it could not, he had crossed all this distance to look at somebody else's loss.

The second thought stopped him.

Somebody else's.

As though that made it small.

He looked again at the smooth lower stone and felt ashamed, then tried not to make the shame the most interesting thing in front of the ruined house. He was not required to identify the people as his own before their having lived here mattered.

---

"Mera Kul Asuran hai," he said.

The words were quiet. They reached the people beside the chair and the ordinary air around them.

Nothing answered from beneath the stone.

He had said it before, in the account of what his father had told him. Here it felt less like repeating a family fact and more like allowing the fact to occupy his own voice in a place which had existed without him.

"Main yahaan paida nahi hua," he added. "Par isse mera kuchh juda hai."

Aadhya looked at him.

"Haan."

She did not tell him what the connection was supposed to make him do next. Veer rested the good hand on his own leg and let the boy have the sentence without turning it into a pledge. Ira listened with the ordinary eye still closed.

Rudra could be related to a place without recognizing it. He could belong to a lineage without being appointed the explanation for every ruin in its world.

The knowledge did not lighten the ankle. It changed the way he was able to look past it.

For the first time since arriving, he stopped searching the doorway for a resemblance to the courtyard in Varanasi.

---

He asked whether they could take one photograph from where they were resting. The attendant passed his phone to Sitara.

Not of the whole group arranged as though it had conquered a view. He wanted the doorway and the open floor, without someone standing in the broken space to give it scale.

Sitara asked which edge he wanted included. He pointed with the good hand from the chair, keeping the wrist which needed protection out of the movement. She took the picture from the inspected line and showed it without asking Ira to strain towards the screen.

It was a poor photograph in the way the first honest ones often were. The pale light flattened some edges. A shadow looked deeper than the place had looked to the eye. The frame cut off the part of the road which made the doorway seem less alone.

"Bas itna aayega?" Rudra asked.

"Is tasveer mein," Sitara said.

He nodded.

He would have to tell his father what stood outside it. A photograph could remember what had been put inside the frame; it could not do the work of admitting what the person holding it had failed to know.

They kept the picture. Nobody improved it into a plan of the lost house.

A sound came through the broken opening above the channel.

All four turned towards it before the sound had finished. Low, then thinner, with a rough catch at the end: close enough to the shape of a voice to make the body begin listening for words.

There were none.

A little dust lifted from the hollow beneath the surviving stone. The sound changed as the air moved through it again. Sitara watched the grit, pointed out the opening and waited through the next change before naming what they could actually hear.

"Hawa. Is khaali hisse se."

Rudra let out the breath he had begun holding.

The explanation did not make the place cheerful. It made it a little more terrible in an ordinary way: stone which had once kept air out now giving it a shape like somebody trying to speak. The people were not required to return as voices for their absence to be heard.

Veer looked towards the broken edge, then down at his own hand.

"Har aawaaz ka jawaab dene ki aadat chhodni padegi," he said.

"Pehle sun toh lo kya hai," Aadhya answered.

"Wahi toh keh raha hoon."

The brief irritation helped. They were four tired people startling at a noise, not four chosen interpreters being addressed by the dead.

---

Ira asked them to wait.

Not because she had seen something beyond their sight. The uncovered eye was watering in the moving air, and the shapes had become less comfortable to hold apart. She said exactly that instead of letting the quiet around her be mistaken for a discovery.

Sitara came nearer, asked about pain and the change in what she could ordinarily see, and followed the care arrangement they had brought. They gave her shelter from the moving air and a place to rest the eye. If the symptoms did not settle as expected, the visit would end. No part of the ruined street was important enough to negotiate with that condition.

Rudra waited.

He wanted to ask whether Ira was all right, then asked once and listened to the answer instead of making her provide reassurance again for every worried face.

"Aankh band karke theek lag raha hai," she said after the rest. "Abhi dekhna nahi. Sun sakti hoon."

She chose to remain seated for the next interval, with Sitara beside her and the way back close. That choice had room inside the limited plan. It was not permission for the others to leave her behind while they pursued a more impressive view.

They stayed together.

The ordinary eye had given them what it could. Nobody asked the covered one to make up the difference.

---

Aadhya looked at the ground between her feet.

A shallow hollow in a fallen piece of stone had gathered a pinch of dust. For one ridiculous instant she wanted something green there. A small thing, nothing grand. A leaf would give the gaze somewhere to go other than towards the next broken edge.

She knew that wanting.

There were times when making a thing live had felt like the only answer she possessed to somebody else's pain. Here the impulse was almost an impatience with the view, a desire to put a kinder ending in it before she had finished looking.

Her hands lay protected in her lap. The altered sensation in the fingers had not changed because the need in front of her seemed larger.

She did not press a palm to the stone.

"Kya?" Rudra asked.

She had been looking down for a while.

"Soch rahi thi kuchh ugaa doon. Phir laga main kya theek karne chali hoon."

He followed her gaze to the little hollow.

"Main bhi aate waqt soch raha tha kuchh lekar jaaoonga," he said. "Pata nahi kya. Bas haath mein ho."

Aadhya nodded.

Neither desire was wicked. Neither became a reason to ask an injured hand for more than it had been allowed, or to call a changed patch of ground the repair of what had happened here.

For now she could sit beside her friend while he looked. She could tell Ira where the air was moving. She could ask for help when her own hand became uncomfortable.

She did those things.

---

When the next little interval ended, Dhruv asked how each was managing.

Veer needed the knee position changed before standing again. Rudra said the ankle support was beginning to press at one place. Aadhya wanted a moment before moving. Ira kept the ordinary eye rested and said she could continue with guidance if the others still wanted the last short part of the inspected line.

They made the adjustments first.

The place was not being taken away while they rested. No gate was closing because they had failed to be strong enough on the first attempt. The return time remained real, and it would come sooner if care needed it, but it was not a supernatural contest with the ruins.

Rudra drank a little water and felt it arrive as water, cool and ordinary in a mouth which had been waiting to taste something more important.

He looked towards the turn where their permitted line ended.

There was enough time to see that last view and come back, if the checked path and their bodies remained as they were. That was the whole offer.

He accepted its size.

The last stretch turned beside a wall too low to hide what lay beyond it once they reached the corner.

There was no climb. Sitara and Dhruv had included this level edge in the line they had checked, and the attendant kept the chair where the surface remained broad enough. Ira took the spoken turn, eyes rested, with Aadhya describing the change in the view only after Sitara had finished directing the feet.

Rudra looked past the end of the wall.

The street did not end with the houses they had seen.

More broken fronts lay beyond the first ones. The pale light found one edge, lost another, reached a farther line which might have been a wall or several walls standing together. There were gaps too wide for him to understand from here, places where the eye tried to finish a building and found another depth instead.

He could not see the end of the damage from this narrow view.

That was not the same as knowing the whole realm. It was enough to make the little house he had been trying to place inside his mind suddenly too small to hold the loss he was looking at.

He had come with the story of a family.

The view contained the ordinary problem of how many families could live beside one another without any one child ever needing to know all their names.

---

Veer reached the resting place and sat before asking anything.

He had once thought of protecting as something which began when a danger appeared in front of him. Here the damage occupied the space after all the moments in which someone might have tried. He could not put his body between the broken walls and what had already happened to them.

He did not know where to put the familiar readiness in his shoulders.

The shoulder which still hurt answered first. He let it settle into the permitted position, told Dhruv what needed changing and took the help offered. It was an answer he could actually use now.

Beside him, Aadhya described the farther view for Ira. She kept the uncertain parts uncertain. She did not count houses or declare the visible emptiness a census of everyone who had ever lived in Pataal.

Ira asked where the line of the nearest street disappeared.

Aadhya told her.

"Uske aage?"

"Yahaan se saaf nahi."

"Wahi rehne do phir."

The words were gentle. Not every distance needed to be conquered by another person's eyes before Ira was allowed to know where she stood.

Rudra listened to the description and felt how much of arriving had been the work of other people making the view possible without pretending to make it complete.

---

He asked whether he could stand briefly in the arrangement already allowed, with help.

The attendant checked the position first. There was space, the same protected load and a sound support for the good hand. The chair remained behind him. Dhruv watched the line rather than asking the injured wrist to do one unnecessary thing in the name of belonging.

Rudra rose with assistance.

The ankle felt the permitted weight through the protection; the knee required attention. He gave the body that attention before looking beyond it. He was not planting his feet as an owner, and no one asked him to kneel.

The altered height changed very little in the distance.

It changed the nearest threshold. From the chair it had been an edge; now he could see the worn strip leading up to it, the little irregularities which a person coming home might once have learned without ever thinking of them as knowledge.

He thought of the wet step outside college. Of the turn by the familiar door. Of feet learning the small dangers of a place until someone could be half asleep and still come through without stumbling.

People had known this ground that way.

He had not.

"Baithna hai," he said when the effort began to spread.

They helped him down. He reported the ache, waited through the check and let the support be made easier. No new injury had been demanded to prove the feeling real.

---

For a while he wanted the view to tell him what his father had looked like before Earth.

The desire arrived as it had in the ward: a face, a room, something particular enough for love to fasten on without having to reach through the whole terrible distance. Then he remembered the infant in the account. There was no childhood walk through this street for him to recover from his father's memory.

There could have been other children here.

The thought did not supply a procession of imagined last moments. Rudra refused that easy cruelty to people whose lives he did not know. He could look at the thresholds without inventing who had fallen on them.

He let the place remain larger than his own need for a picture.

When Ira asked what he would want included in a second photograph, he answered differently from before.

"Raasta bhi," he said. "Sirf ek ghar nahi."

Sitara took the wider view from where they had stopped. It still ended at the frame. He knew that now and kept it anyway.

Dhruv said it was time to begin the return while they could still make it without hurry.

The ordinary words changed the view. A moment earlier Rudra had been looking as though he could remain until the missing part became visible. Now the broken street would have to go on existing while he was somewhere else.

He wanted another minute.

He said so. Dhruv looked at the group, the way they were resting, the time left within the arrangement, and said they could have a short one here before turning. If anyone needed to leave sooner, they would. The answer had no bargain hidden in it: the minute was not being exchanged for a longer walk back.

Rudra stayed seated.

He looked at the farther line of walls and tried to stop fixing it in his mind like a photograph which would fail if he blinked. There would be things he forgot. Forgetting the exact shape of a broken edge would not make the people less real. He could write later what he actually remembered, with the gaps left in it.

Ira asked for one addition to her voice note before the phone was put away.

"Doosre rukne ke hisse se aur tooti imaaratein dikhti hain," she dictated. "Jahaan tak hum gaye, usse aage bhi. Poora hissa dekha nahi."

She had heard the descriptions and checked the wording with the people who had given them. The account named its source instead of borrowing an eye which had been resting.

Then she asked to stop recording.

---

A small piece of grit had worked into the edge of the cloth supporting Rudra's ankle.

The attendant noticed it while making the position ready for the return. He lifted it clear before settling the cloth again. Rudra said the support was easier and watched the little rough piece of stone in the man's hand.

"Dena zara," he said.

The attendant put it in the good palm once the chair was secure.

It was unmarked. A broken piece, no bigger than something he might have shaken from a shoe on Earth, grey on one face and rougher along the broken edge. There was no way of telling when that had happened. Nothing in it named the wall it might have come from.

Rudra held it loosely.

The weight was almost nothing.

After all the effort of crossing, there was a terrible convenience in that. A thing which could go into a pocket. A thing he could put before his father and say, there, I went. It existed outside what either of us had been told.

He knew it could prove no more than that somebody had carried a stone. He could have picked one up beside the road to Mirzapur and given it the same importance with his voice.

Knowing this did not immediately loosen the wanting.

---

Aadhya noticed the hand.

"Kya hai?"

"Pathar. Support mein aa gaya tha."

She looked, then looked back towards the street. There was nothing in her expression which could decide for him whether a loose fragment belonged in a pocket. She had wanted a leaf in the dust a little earlier. Wanting a small thing to carry more than it could was not an error she had earned the right to find amusing.

"Rakhna chaahte ho?" she asked.

"Papa ko dikhaaoon soch raha tha."

"Tasveerein bhi hain."

"Haan."

The answer was not enough for what he was trying to say. A picture was light caught in a device. This could touch another hand. The difference felt important even while he knew it did not make the grey fragment an heirloom or an answer about the people who had died.

He remembered his father's instruction before leaving: tell what you see; do not try to recognize it for me.

He had seen so much and understood so little of it. The stone offered the relief of looking certain.

---

Sitara helped Ira prepare to stand with the guidance she had chosen. Veer checked the position of his aid before rising. The attendant waited by Rudra's chair. The way back had not changed into a test while the boy looked at his palm.

There would be water on the Earth side, care due at an ordinary time, a message to people who would worry until it came. The ruins would not become less ruined because he remained one minute longer than the body could manage.

He could leave.

He could also bring something which he had no right to call a recovered piece of his father's childhood. He knew that already. What he had not settled was why the almost weightless thing still felt easier to carry than the view.

Ira heard the conversation about the fragment. She kept the ordinary eye rested and asked from beside Sitara, not from some sight which reached through the closed lid.

"Isse unhe kya bataaoge?"
