# CHAPTER 12 — THE INTERROGATION

It came for Rudra at dawn, the way everything in this house came: through the courtyard, on a count.

He had slept in two ransacked fragments — the first till midnight, then nothing, then a leaden too-late second sleep from which his father's voice removed him at five forty-five with the traditional economy of the garrison: not a call, only the sound below of the staff being taken down off its pegs, the ironwood haft ringing once against the courtyard flagstone as it settled into waiting hands.

He dressed. He taped. He went down, wrapped knuckles hidden up his chewed sleeves, heart already on mark.

The sky over the lane was the colour of skimmed milk. In the courtyard Ram stood with his back to the neem, the staff upright beside him like a visiting pandit, and he watched Rudra come down the steps the way clocks watch men approach them, all the while doing one unprecedented thing: nothing at all. No warm-up. No walking of the courtyard's perimeter. No inventory. Standing. Waiting.

Inside, the kitchen's single diya still burned from earlier — stood and lit at five by hands that needed the fire to exist for the rituals to have somewhere to be returned to. Rukmini had spent those forty minutes before dawn not cooking. She had stood at the window with her eyes shut and her thumbs together and re-laid the boy's joinery, knot by knot, knowing that she was caulking a ship with the sea aboard already: eight fractures where yesterday's count had been cheating at four, one of them long as a finger now, shaped like nothing she could un-see — the crack had forked; cracks fork when they have discovered the building's original fault-lines. She had laid down position twenty-eight four more times and had the same snag-thread feeling, worse, and around position thirty-three — where the old weave *looped* eastward through the heart — she discovered that the seam there had *already chosen its first re-route*: the light inside her son was finding new borders to the east while she still laboured at the lost old ones —

She had finished anyway. Masons finish.

Now, with the pressure-cooker's first amicable hiss answering the morning below her like an all-clear, she washed her hands with all sixteen motions and dried them on the towel from last night — folds memorized, silent as bread — and went to stand at the inner door's curtain, two rooms from the courtyard, a wall away from her whole remaining life. She did not trust herself to go outside. She did not trust herself not to.

"Staff nahi chahiye aaj," came Ram's voice, flattened, beyond the wall. "Haath se karenge. Paanch minute mein shuru."

She put her forehead against the cool of the doorframe and listened, unmoving, proprietor and hostage of the same house.

So there was to be no session at all. There was to be a *fight.* In the courtyard, Rudra's throat went dry with a dryness older than yesterday; he said "Theek hai" softly, set his feet, and got up onto the balls of them.

They began, as all father-son wars begin, without a whistle — and with every instrument in the house, every diya and tap and pressure-valve, standing witness at attention, inventorying the noise of it into the family's permanent, private accounts.

---

It was not a beating. Everyone will tell you those are different and everyone is wrong: in the courtyard of a silent man, abuse and education wear the same leather gloves. The truth was that it began properly — drill-proper, a spar like the aarti began per schedule — and it disintegrated.

Before it disintegrated it was, for nearly eleven minutes, the most complete conversation they had ever had.

Sparring is the family's real dining table, and this morning they ate enormous helpings. Ram opened with the old counting-gate drills: palm-heel probes that demanded the lota-parry and got it, twice, crisp, earlier than the beat — the boy was *ahead*, today of all days, ahead of tempo the way a hundred-metre man slightly beats the gun — and Ram escalated by increments he no longer permitted himself to call cruelty: inside-reap, hip-turn, both hooks, then the close-range shiver-work his son had never weathered full-strength, and Rudra absorbed three clean bursts without breaking the count, breath low, eyes wide — wicked with discovery — and for two exchanged seconds the courtyard was what it had dedicated two decades of mornings to: two adepts, speaking perfectly in a tongue made entirely of their hands, the son finally marginally quicker than the father and the father finding, for the first time, that he could not catch up—

It disintegrated at eleven minutes, on a low inside leg-block of Ram's that came in harder than the day's protocol — pressed Rudra's forearms down and inward with the weight of thirty years of withheld mornings — and Rudra, answering, sheeted one elbow up across the opening: textbook; and on the passing of elbow past his father's temple the two men froze, because in that passing the air *changed quality around Rudra's elbow* — seasoned suddenly, gaspingly warm, like a bakery door had opened inside the courtyard at shoulder height — and a thin thread of gold-red came drifting off the boy's forearm-burn scars — three fading seams — into the morning.

They both saw it. The thread unspooled a hand's length, wound around nothing, and folded back into the skin.

"Ruko," said Ram.

Rudra stopped. That had always been the contract; the rule of every drill since the broken-stone runs was that a command halts a body the way a brake halts a cart: instantly, totally, on faith. Rudra stood with his back half-turned and his raised elbow getting heavier in the air, and for the first time in his life he did not want to turn around. And then he did turn around, because he was his mother's son too, all the way down.

Ram was standing perfectly still at the courtyard center. His chest moved as chests move after the word "archery," and not otherwise.

"Yeh kal bhi tha," he said.

It came out of him flat as a nail being indexed. Not a question.

Rudra considered, for the remainder of one breath, the armory of his prepared lies — *dhund; bulb; tea-stall ka photoflash; lecture mein sab dekh rahe the* — and his mouth, which had never once lied successfully inside this quadrangle, voted by absence.

"Haan," he said.

"Usse pehle bhi." Again flat. He had it all sequenced. Rudra could see the inventory: Ram had arrived at the interrogation with the account-books already balanced —

"Ghat pe?" said the father. "Veer ke saath?" — the little drop-pause of arithmetic — "…pillar?"

Rudra did not answer by nodding or by lying. He breathed, once, deeply. So Ram read to him, in a low voice, the charge-sheet of a man who had spent the night not sleeping:

"Raat ko main gaya tha. Do baje. Dashashwamedh." Flat — no theatre in it, which made it worse — all theatre belongs to people still hoping to be believed; Ram had abandoned even the wanting of belief. "Khambha dekha maine. Chunar ka. Aisa pathar jo hamare purane ghaton ke neeche dabbe-nakshon ka kaam kar sakta hai, baarish mein bhadega nahi, chirag se bharega nahi — *aisa.* Toota woh pillar stone-dust mein nahi toota, Rudra — *slice* hua. Chopper se todti hui cheez ki tarah. Aur neeche, teen kadam peeche, ek nishaan tha. Mitthaa hua pair ka nishaan nahi — jo cheez uthti hai toh wo dhool ko seedha khol deti hai — main woh nishaan jaanta hoon. Chaalis saal pehle —" he caught the sentence with his teeth, re-sentenced it: "—main jaanta hoon. Jhooth mat bolna ki tum ladke bheed mein the aur patthar gir gaya. Pillar. Chunar. *Slice*. Aur tum dono ke chehre pe subah — jitna maaza aata hai, utna *sannata.*"

The syllables fell, even and laid, like dressed stones. Long after, it was the total lack of raised voice that Rudra found impossible to forget. His father stood there without his staff — an old turtled paleness rising from under the collar of his vest — and read the city aloud like a walking ancestors' stone.

"Kal ka akhbaar," Ram said, "maine andar moda tha subah. Usi mein photo tha ghat ka. Tumhare peeche ek cheez hai Rudra jo tumne aaj subah apni balaiyon se jo chahe tape barrel kar li ho — mere andar bhi boli aayi hai wo. Maine saari umar us cheez ko ghar se bahar rakha; tum usse — *kheench* rahe ho, apni or, jaise kanjar-kushti mein kunda." His voice dropped a last rung. "Kisine kaha tha mujhse — woh jo ghat pe thi tumhare saath, ladki, lambe baalon wali — usne jo kuch bhi lagaya haath, woh *seedha tumhe dhoond rahi thi*. Coil. Cheeni-miiti light ki. Aaye hain Rudra. Aa chuke hain. Aur tumhe — unki light mein — tum — mera beta — ko *jala hua* paya jaata hai."

He waited then. His chest moved once. And Rudra — the son, the defendant, the boy of the broken-stone years — whose whole front had been assembled on the shelf of *one kal-ki-ladai karne wala beta* — understood, suddenly and without pride or shame, that the man before him was not angry: he was *petrified*, and had been petrified for nineteen years, and had learned to hold the posture of stone so constantly that the stone had begun passing for the man.

Rudra breathed, once, deeply, and the seam under his tape answered the morning's fine cool light with a glow restrained to below the visible by one strip of skin; he waited.

Only in the small of his back, dripping down the spine, came an understanding of how long this conversation had been waiting for both of them.

"Do din," said Ram at last, the iron voice gone into something more broken than anger — something *procedural*, the tone of a mahant reading out the list of maintenance-works for a condemned temple — "do din se is ghar ki deewarein bata rahi hain. Do din. Main apne kamre mein let kar *sunte* aa raha hoon. Ek maa apne bete ke andar ki cheez ko roz seedha karti hai, subah chaubis ghante, aur ek baap apne kamre se ginta hai ki aaj ki phati hui lakeerein *kal se do zyada hain.*" He stopped. His jaw set into the continuity of a man who realized he was already saying too much and would therefore say all of it. "Ungliyon pe gin sakta hoon, Rudra. Kavach ki phati lakeerein. Kal subah chaar thi. Aaj subah *saat* hain."

The word landed in the courtyard like a staff on stone: *kavach.* Rudra had never in his life heard either of them say the word. They had needed forty names for nineteen years of silence and they had never needed one, and now his father, standing alone in the rosy-flecked dawn without his staff, let the word out of the bag without asking permission from nineteen years of policy.

"I don't know what that is," Rudra said, in English, not hostile but suspicious, out of words, child-quickness, already apologizing to the morning for what he was about to say next—

"Jhooth," said Ram.

"—haan theek hai, jhooth, *jaanta hoon main kya hai* —" and the nineteen years that followed had scratches and were dry and unlubricated: "—jaanta hoon ki andar kuch hai kyun ki ROZ subah yahan khada hoke usi ko dabaane ki training ho rahi hai, aur main *pooch bhi nahi sakta* kyunki yeh ghar pe poochne pe rishtey tootte hain, jawab pe nahin!"

The wall-lizard, overhead between the living-room rafters, stopped ticking.

"Main jawab de raha hoon," said Ram — quiet, cyclonic. "Poore jawab ke liye tumhe pehle woh cheez bujhani padegi jo tum log gaye khinch kar laaye ho. Ghat ke peeche. Aadhi raat. Do naukri-walon ki jeep ke saamne." His eyes left his own thought and simply, entirely, terrible in their precision, fastened on his son: "Tum jaante ho un logon ko? Laal topi nahi lagi thi unko, Rudra. Laal topi nahi lagi thi *kyunki unhone tumhe dekh rakha tha.*"

"Appa—"

"Fifteen years," his father said, and all the grey mechanics in his voice broke loose and bared the raw steel underneath. "Main tumhare saath kya nahi kiya? Main tumse dosti kar sakta tha. Main tumhe akele chhod sakta tha. Maine tumhari hadiyan todi hain is aangan ke patharon pe, roz, subah, mazboot wali dubaara — not because I enjoyed it—" —he stopped, re-registered the past tense he had admitted —

"I didn't break your bones for fifteen years so you could let this power eat you alive."

The courtyard breathed.

"Maybe I didn't ask you to break my bones."

It was as if the sentence had made the sun come up while all these years the sun had been someone else's rumour.

Ram's face crumbled.

It crumbled exactly the way things in him would crumble — strictly privately, deep in the load-bearing rooms — with one tremor passing through the lower jaw and eyes unchanged, the face of a man being handed back his own entire curriculum as a bill his son was refusing to ever pay, and it lasted one second, one entire second that Rudra would replay for years without being able to remain present for it during the replays —

And then it hardened.

Set. *Re-sealed* — the same inner masonry his wife performed with thumbs and counted knots, executed in one stiff muscular summary. The jaw-set acquired its twenty-year policy. The eyes, two dim furnaces too far gone for mere anger, came up, and stepped back away from him:

"Toh phir samajh, Rudra. Agar todna tumhara faisla nahi tha — toh *bachana* bhi tumhara faisla nahi. Jab tak tum mere ghar mein ho, tum iss cheez ko khule mein nahin nikaloge. Din mein. Raat mein. College mein. Ghat pe. **Nahin.** Us pillar ke saamne, apne hi doston ke saamne, police ke saamne, un teen aadmiyon ke saamne — kabhi nahi. Mere saamne nahi. Apni maa ke saamne *bhi nahi.*" His voice did not rise. It *lowered*, which was infinitely worse. "Kyunki ye jo cheez hai — ise dekhne wale aayenge — ye vardan nahin hai, beta." He stopped carefully, the way he stopped his staff before striking. "Ye dhabba hai. Dhabba. Tujhe pata nahi kis waade ki jageer hai tu. Main jaanta hoon. Aur jeete ji, zamaane ko pata nahin lagne dunga."

"Dhabba," Rudra repeated, level, bloodless. "Keh rahe ho — mujhpe dhabba —"

And that was when the courtyard's back door opened.

---

Rukmini had not watched the whole thing. Years later, assembled in a room of sons-in-law Rudra would claim that she'd heard it all through two closed doors; and only she, who knew that the house's walls had been enlisted into service years before the boys could spell, would narrow her eyes listening to that boast. She arrived, in any case, precisely at the crest — the curve of the fight's unobtainable apex — with an expression on her face that Rudra had never seen on it: the expression of a mason who turns a corridor corner to find both her husband and her son standing *inside* the wall she has been quietly taking down at dawn for nineteen years, their hands black with mortar.

In one hand she held a steel bowl of breakfast — halwa someone's condolence-sweet, halwa from Mr. Srinivas' stall delivered nineteen minutes ago. She set it on the courtyard's washing-stone with a sound of metal settling.

The men of her house did not look at each other. Two silhouettes with arms hanging empty with the spent ferocious knowledge of what an unfolded night in the world did to the morning.

"Chalo," she said. Not *don't fight.* Not *enough.* Just: *chalo.*

Neither of them moved.

"Rudra." She turned halfway to the courtyard tap and said, over her shoulder, at the courtyard wall, in the easy everyday machinery voice: "Upar jaa. Nahale. Tere notes cushion ke andar hain, maine theek kar diye."

He stood looking at her. Then at his father's back — the incomprehensible changed country of it, the north of his father he'd been born too late to emigrate to. Then he walked past — tapped his father's arm very briefly, two fingers, neither contact nor absence, his mother's finger-tap — and went in, and up.

Mother and son heard, ten seconds later, the water going on behind the closed bathroom door, high and cold and deliberate.

***

Rukmini took the water lota off the tap. Turned it down. Filled the brass bowl for the birds. When her hands were done tidying they went to the small of her back and stayed there, like hands standing outside a dispensary.

"Uske saamne," she said, at last, with the tender uninflected flatness in which this household kept its Holy Articles, "kabhi phir se yeh mat kehna. Dhabba-wala."

Ram did not face her. He faced the courtyard-wall, or the lane beyond it, or something beyond the lane whose headcount was rising every night, at eleven, across the city's ringed drains. He said: "Sach hai."

"Sach kehne ke tarike hote hain." She poured. The water said its small hallmarked song onto old brick. "Pandrah saal tu ise todh-sikha raha tha. Ye bachcha tumse aaj tak ladta nahi aaya — poochta rahaa. Aur tum dono ab jhooth bolte ho ek dusre se, aur dono ko bilkul theek pata hai." She looked up. She was not crying; a manufacturer of tears does not have the budget. She was taking inventory, arranging his face along the dawn, memorizing which regret had stayed and which had emigrated. "Jaante ho — jab kisi ko mitti lagti hai na, toh mitti zyada dikhayi deti hai."

When he did not speak — when it was quite clear that all the fortifications of his day were sand-construction — she acquitted him of the helplessness by changing the subject to the only one she had left:

"Mela aa raha hai." Pause. "Dev Deepawali. Teen din. Nau ghanton wala." She came up beside him with the empty brass bowl under her arm like a commander's folded paraphernalia. "Usse jaane do, Ram. Doston ke saath. Jhoomne." She paused, organized her voice to the weight of an ultimatum written on the back of a ration card. "Is ghar mein ek din bhi aisa hona chahiye jisse usse yaad rahe ki is andar kuch hone ke siva bhi zindagi hai. Tumhe nahi pata. Tumhara kaam hi alag hai. Lekin *main jaanti hoon bharosa kaisa dikhta hai — aur abhi usko humse bharosa uth raha hai, beta hote hote isse ghar ho raha hai. Aaj hi.*"

He looked away first. That was his concession; that was his surrender; in twenty years she had earned, perhaps, four of those.

"Peon," Ram said to the doorpost, sourest ancient joke, name of the boy's first coach, the name by which he had called his son from four. His whole back vibrated once.

---

It wasn't the water that answered Rudra upstairs. It was the door.

She came in with a dry kurta folded over her wrist and the household towel over her shoulder and did not knock — this was the house's one room where knocking had always been considered a foreign luxury, a boy's bathroom being, in the taxonomy of Indian mothers, roughly equivalent to a verandah. She set the kurta on his bed. She did not flinch from the steam curling off his chest, or ask whether the steam was his. She stood inside his door with her folded hands and looked at him — whole, tall as her now — for a moment that belonged to twenty years ago and to the moment after this one with equal injustice.

"Tape hata do," she said. "Geela ho jayega. Aaun?" — an old joke from teething months; the geezer-rope humour of babyhood.

"Ma."

"Kab tak chhupayega?" She made a small business of folding the bed's coverlet edge straight. "Meri taraf se lad. Baap ki taraf se mat lad. Ek hi kaafi tha is ghar ke liye." The words came out of her it seemed from very far away: from the old country of wives' kitchen-collisions, where women learn that the way to make two angry items not break the kitchen is to place oneself exactly, physically, between the stove and the shelf. "Woh jo usne kaha — dhabba — bhool dena. Uske muh se nikal gaya. Uske *andar* nahin nikla." A pause, long and straight. "Tu uska dhabba nahin hai, Rudra. Tu uska nirnay hai. Log —" and she did a thing with her chin, a small brutal shake — "log waade nibhaane ke liye ladakpan mein bahut chhoti si hadiyan todkar bade karte hain dusron ki. Tumhe samajh aayega. Kabhi."

"Kab?" Rudra said. His voice had found the tube from the age-five courtyard with no address change. "Kab samajh aayega, Ma? Kal? College ke baad? Shaadi ke baad? Jab woh log mujhe *le jaane* aayenge?"

The seam under his sternum, deep and golden and watching, answered her before she could: it stood out in the steaming skin like one slender line of script, and she looked at it, and he looked down at it, and in their shared look was the entire walled history of the house: the morning she had placed him like a raisin into the knotted architecture; the door she'd made inside a door she'd made; the small mason-mother agreements which had kept the roof on —

"Jab main aur tera baap na bach paayenge," she said. "Tab nahi bataungi. *Usse pehle.*" A stone moved into place somewhere under the sentence; she said it the way towers are talked about — *when we can no longer protect you; before that.* "Abhi nahi. Beta. *Abhi* aaja. Pooja-ghar ke diye mein tel khatam ho raha hai — bhar de."

And she went back down the stairs, taking her old shoulder-towel and her nineteen years with her, and Rudra stood in the steam of the opened bathroom door with the dry kurta over one hand and discovered that he did not, in fact, know what any of the four inhabitants of the house allowed themselves for lunch.

---

After that, behind the closed bathroom door, under the high cold water, Rudra stood with two palms plastered against the tiles, head bent — letting it run down his whole history, the nineteen years of him, the one day of him that had just unfolded in the dirt below. Cold water found the taped seam of his chest and stood aside from it like a crowd from heat; the steam rose off the red-gold seams of him in a fine, private plume, in the way a lamp is said to bow without extinguishing when it prays.

*Dhabba,* he thought. *I'm a mark that belongs somewhere against someone's debt. The house knew. It knew before I did; the house was built around it; my whole name is an accommodation the world made to this thing and then fooled me with.*

He stayed there till the water went cold all the way around. Then he made one strong unshaking motion with a wrapped hand and stood straight, chest to the merciless tile, and let, at last, one short English sentence — the most expensive kind — climb out of him through the water-noise, spoken southward, spoken like an unofficial pact with himself:

"Enough hiding."

---

In the small back storeroom off the dining passage, where the house kept its entire unproud history — sky-blue paint flaking off the inside ribs, an almirah older than the marriage, two steel trunks with their painted initials whiteworked by decades of ghee-smoke — his father stood alone with a table between his hands like a parapet.

On the table, the oiled cloth bundle lay where he had set it a long forearm's worth of him ago.

He did not open it. He crossed the room and came back, twice. Outside, the lane's municipal radio-bicycle went past playing someone's recorded Aarti tone-deaf and off-schedule, and in the yard behind his head Rukmini mixed the morning fresh flour for the boy's eleven o'clock, and the house protected none of them any longer from what it had always known; and he stood there above the oilcloth — the old herringbone wrapping, the sailor's hitch his fingers had tied and un-tied and tied for years now like the instruments of an honest penance — and he let his palm rest on it, once, flat, the way a dying man's hand finally blesses a forehead.

"Baad mein," Ram muttered to the bundle. Voice of a general forcing the front to hold by pure failure to accept its collapse. "Abhi nahi. Abhi — dekhna hai mujhe, bache hue dinon mein, kya kya tootta hai — aur kya kya — *ruk jaata hai*."

He retied the hitch with shaking, deliberate fingers: reef knot; fisherman; then the knot he had learned from a dead teacher thirty years ago in a city that did not have this one's name — the knot with the wrong little loop, the one only three arrested histories in the world could have taught him.

Then he turned out the small lamp-oil of the storeroom's dusk and went back out into the house, to stand.

The neem above the courtyard leaned against the house listening with its hundred bittersweet leaves, disapproving, furious, afraid — for even a tree knew better than to give a father permission to cry, and knew with the same certainty that under the house, forty feet down, in every direction at once, a great patient cup was being filled.

The day was required to continue. It did.
