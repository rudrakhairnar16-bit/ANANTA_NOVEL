# CHAPTER 14 — THE BOAT RACE

The poster went up on a Thursday, pasted with rice-glue to the notice wall of Assi's chai shop between a lost-goat announcement and a tuition-class schedule, lettered by hand in the definitive red of events that have happened every year for longer than anyone has been checking:

**GANGA NAUKA DAUD — RAJGHAT SE PANCHGANGA TAK — SHANIVAR DOPAHAR 4 BAJE — ENTRY ₹200 — INAAM: ₹5001 + TROPHY + PURANE CHAMPION KA NAMAN**

Under it someone had ballpointed, without malice: *iss saal bharosa mat karna, bukhaar se zyada tez hai.*

Veer circled the date in the group chat with a photograph and a single message — **main aur tu** — and the argument lasted well into the night, because Rudra wanted it on record that rowing was a sport of synchronization and not heroism, and Veer wanted it on record that fighting a pahalwan in a fairground had been technically Rudra's fault for allowing him to get bored, and both of them, at bottom, needed very badly to do the simplest possible thing with their bodies in public for once, without anything glowing anywhere.

A boat race was exactly, blessedly, a thing with rules.

The girls were appointed by mutual tyranny to Management: strategy, registration, betting-tier surveillance (Ira), and morale (Aadhya, who claimed to despise all competitive sport and could name the batting order of four international sides historically).

"They're doing it for *themselves*," Ira explained to the chai-shop wall, as if charging admission to her own resignation. "Hamara boat hum jitayenge. Dekh lena. Lekin ek minute ka bhi umas nahi rahega beech mein."

The registration was conducted beneath the chai-shop's awning at a folding table run by Munna Majhi the elder — seventy-something, a torso like an oar, a face cross-hatched by sixty ghat-winters, famous as the man who had once piloted a whole wedding party through the flood of '78 without losing so much as one packet of chura. He looked the two of them up and down — the mountain boy and the wrapped knuckles — with the serenity of a man assessing secondhand furniture.

"Do sau," he said. "Naam?"

"Sawant. Pathak. Saath mein likho," said Veer.

"Do ladke hote toh paise oopar se lagte. Teen sau."

"Uncle, do sau toh poster pe—"

"Poster professional ke liye hai. Tum dono college waale ho. College waalon se zyada liya jaata hai kyunki tum boat ko jhooti umeed dete ho." But the old man was smiling through the haggle in the way of a man who had decided fifty years ago that the youth was part of the scenery he was sworn to like anyway; he took the notes from Veer's roll one by one, wet his thumb, counted to exactly two hundred, and struck the entry into the tin. "Register likhna padega. Bhaari boat mat lena. Jalpari bhaari hai — sahab-log ko nahi dete." He dipped the iron-nibbed pen into campaign-blue ink and wrote their names in with square strokes, and then, dry, still not looking up: "Suno. Subah do aadmi aaye the. Motorbike waale. Pooch rahe the ghat pe us raat kaun tha — jab khambha gira. Naam-muhalla-prize detail. Maine kaha dekha nahi." He looked up at last, one eye wide and belonging to caution, the other narrow and belonging to memory. "College wale kaam padhai karte ho yeh bhi?"

"Kabhi-kabhi," Rudra said, pleasantly, in the courtyard voice. "Unhone kisaan-kapat pahna tha kya?"

"Windcheater. Grey. Aur pair mein aisa boot jo bhaiya log nahi pahante." The old man swiped the register shut on their names. "Jeeto ya haaro — shaam ke pehle ghar nikal jaana. Chaar ghat pe paanch cheez daud rahi hai aajkal sivaye paani ke. Tum dono ko chhaap ke rakhna, shaniwar ke pehle bhi chalta hai."

They took the boat key from his hand without looking at each other. Down on the water, the afternoon's early practice runs were crashing the peace in ecstasy — hulls cracking over each other's wakes, oars up, boys standing to whoop.

"Thank you, Munna-ji," said Veer.

"Hmm," said the old man, and looked out at his river as a man looks at a beloved and increasingly unreliable cousin. "Jalpari ka khayal rakhna. Bhaari hai par wafadaar hai. Jaise boodha chowkidaar."

---

The day before the race they had an hour alone with Jalpari, for induction, because Veer held the view — entirely correct — that you do not begin a relationship with a machine on the morning it decides your reputation.

She lay at her chain off the Assi steps in the Friday late light, twenty-two feet of old river-timber, broad amidships, painted river-duck brown with a brass patch near the bow bearing her name in Devanagari and beneath the name, in painted numerals half-erased by seasons, a phone number for someone who had died. Veer climbed down first, barefoot, performed his own and everybody's checks — once around the waterline with his palm; once around the oar-locks listening for crack-song; bailing out the afternoon's ingress with the lota kept under the stern thwart for exactly that purpose — and only then did Rudra hand down the cardboard box of supplies: the electric tape, the sit-upon cushions produced mysteriously from someone's house, the sugar-sack of spare rope, four paracetamol and a pair of sturdy words of encouragement Ira had snipped off a previous year's science-college poster because she had judged them needed here.

In one hour they were at work — Veer caulking the bow's leak with the tar-soap donated by the chai-shop owner downstairs, Aadhya re-drilling the loose rowlock with her bicycle multi-tool and two bolts, Ira rechecking everything twice with a protractor as if she disputed geometry's permanence on principle. There was sweet disagreement. There were cut fingers and apology-band-aids.

At one point Veer stood on the thwart in the half-darkness with his arms out, narrating the principles of keel balance to an audience of three tired friends and seven moored hulks, and Aadhya, paint on her chin, said fondly: "Mummy kehti hain — jo ladka boat ko jitna pyaar se dekh raha hai, wohi naukari se dekhna chahiye tha—"

"Naukri boring hai," Veer replied, serene, from his pedestal. "Boat mein naav hai."

When they finished — *approximately* when they finished: nineteen minutes after Veer announced completion — Jalpari lay on her own shadow in no pain, the bilge dry, the locks true, and she'd taken aboard, invisible to any eye, her full complement: four hours of their work, their banter sitting like dust-pearls in her seams, their shared clumsy hopes already taking passenger-space on every thwart. She did not leak a cupful that evening. The next morning she leaked exactly enough to make it literary.

Saturday, four o'clock: the river had turned out.

Race days on the Ganga at Varanasi are as old as oars; the municipality came in later and licensed them. The start line lay north off Rajghat, where the current was widest and most honest — a stretch of brown-green water that gave any rower exactly as much river as his education deserved — with the finish buoy a white-painted oil drum bobbing opposite Panchganga, a route of perhaps two kilometres, downstream, diagonally hugging the ghatside so the crowd could follow along the steps like a standing ovation in motion.

Seventeen boats had entered. By hydrological legacy they divided, as they divided every year, into three unchangeable nations: the **boatmen's sons**, lean, gorgeously economical boys of the river who had been pulling oars since they were eight and whose strokes were as standardized as scripture (entry of Munna Majhi's people, the reigning champions for four years in their red trim boat); the **gym's rearguard**, broad and desperate, alumni of every akhara and cable-gym in Sigra and Lanka, strong as oxcarts and precisely as hydrodynamic; and the **others** — college boys, a pair of widowed enthusiasts, one team of boys from the polytechnic who had shown up in matching jerseys with *GANGA WARRIORS* printed on the back, who would capsize within sight of the starting rope, ritually, as they did every year.

The boat Veer had rented was old, twenty-two feet of heavy river-made timber named *Jalpari* on a patch of brass, and she leaked with the serene persistence of long experience.

"Ye doobi hui boat hai," Ira pronounced, verifications completed. "Rent wapas le lo."

"Doobi hui boat se hi jeette hain," Veer said serenely, settling his whole converted throne into the bow thwart and adjusting decibels by way of testing the oar-locks. "Fast boat waala drama pata hai kya hota hai? Jeetne pe log shak karte hain. Hum thoda poorane, mazboot, sarkari, seedha-nikamme falsely aahat wale hain."

"Five hundred aur ek rupee ka trophy ke liye ye sab speech de raha hai," muttered Aadhya.

"Society ke liye cup hai yeh!" announced Veer. Utter conviction. Somewhere underneath the conviction: that thing — the gold, the horizon-spanning bigness — which had, three weeks in, stopped answering his door and begun, instead, to *listen from the kitchen.* He could feel it in his wrist today, in the low sunshine like permanent feverishness. It had the decency — he and it had come to arrangements, he'd discovered — to keep entirely silent if he did not *ask* it things. Today he was going to do some rowing there, with it, within it, intending not to ask; that was the plan. He was, indisputably, the amateur champion of plans.

Rudra, in the stern seat, looked astern — composed, contented. "Ready?"

"Ready."

Ira stood on the topmost step with both windcheater pockets heavier than the windcheater, and shouted down the standard of her blessing: "Dono, laziness mat karna, pani heavy hai, tairne ki koshish mat karna, bas stroke—"

"— aur tu kya kar rahi hogi udhar chaar logon ki report file kar ke," Rudra yelled back pleasantly, and pushed off, and the river took them.

---

The start's drum had echoed twice off the facades before the two of them exhaled.

"Utpat," Aadhya pronounced, standing at the crowd's edge on tiptoe — and then, with something small and satisfied written along her mouth: "Chai piyogi? Mere taraf se."

Ira nodded once and followed — down the terraces toward the chai stall, without conversation, in the new quality of walk the last ten days had fabricated for them out of opposing materials: two women in parallel, neither asking the other's pace to change.

The chai came. They sat on the overlook stones above the river-lane and watched small shapes in the thread of light inscribe some old contention onto the afternoon.

"Tum log aise bahar bahut aate ho?" Ira said presently, data-gathering despite herself.

"Aate toh the," Aadhya said. "Ghoomne ke bahane se nahi — ek doosre ko dekhne ke bahane se." She scratched her nail along the step's old grain, half-listening to the crowd thunder as somebody went topsy-turvy into the shallows. "Mere ghar mein gaya-trip maana hai. Toh main jhooth bolti hoon — 'zoo ja rahi hoon,' 'library ja rahi hoon' — aur phir raat ko apni ungliyan ginti hoon." A long mouthful of chai, suspiciously examined. "Bachpan se mujhe kissi bhi din mein ek hi lamha mila hai jismein gate nahi tha. Wo ped ke andar wala — jahan adda jamake khadi ho sakti thi. Zameen wala." Her glance touched Ira's, checking for mockery, found none. "Ab zameen mujhe sun rahi hai waapis. Tum madad kar rahi ho — pata hai mujhe. Main aur tum dono apna poora nahi batate, kyunki kuch baatein bolte hi khatarnak ho jaati hain. Par hum dono ko shak nahi hota ek doosre pe. Bas itna hi chahti thi."

"Sun rahi hoon," said Ira. And then, with the exact kindness of a surveyor taking a witness-stone within reach: "Theek hai. Main bhi tumse poora nahi bolti. Ek din bolongi, jab proof haath mein hoga. Aaj nahi — kyunki aaj usne paani pe haath rakh diya hai, aur neeche wala dekhta hai ki maine dekha ya nahi." She drained the plastic cup. "Ghabrana mat. Race dekh le pehle. Yeh dono jeetne waale hain, aur mujhe statistics mein interest nahi."

"Hmm," said Aadhya, and for one minute they were — elbows on knees, completely full — two people who had never had friends before, watching their boys win twenty metres at a time.

---

The race went out — the way all boat races go out — drastically wide of anybody's plan.

The start rope snapped, the drum struck from the starter's rowboat, and the first hundred metres of the Ganga became, in seconds, a boiling traffic court. The polytechnic team pulled portside with the perfect synchronized incompetence they had trained all year, bore down onto the reigning champions' red trim boat like a runaway metro, missed by an oar's width, over-corrected — *splash* — and found water. The crowd, who came for nothing else, went up in a single huge libelous rejoicing.

Veer howled with laughter and lost the stroke count.

"Count pe ab tera future hai!" Rudra shouted from the stern, over the noise, dragging them back onto four-four breath with the throttle of his own two arms — and by the manner of all such recoveries, in powered boats or lives, the two of them found it at the same stroke: metronome-breath, eyes up, discipline bootstrapped from eleven years of courtyard drills, Veer feeling the load through his forearm and converting it the way a schoolboy converts stolen mangoes into afternoon; and they went along the lane of water harder and faster, harder and faster, passing the gym's ox-crews one by one (rancid and jealous cries in their wake, bets wavering, small fortunes knees-first down), the ghat-steps' crowd pacing them along the shore like a moving wedding.

At the halfway mark — the Narad Ghat alignment, where the shore wears its one slip of old temple-flag — they were third. Ahead, the champions' red trim, and one lean boat of the old khidki crews whose boys were known for racing anything, over anything, in any season. The red trim's two oarsmen, Munna Majhi's twin sons, pulled in deep fine malice, like rowing was pension they'd rather die than give up, and Veer, six seats' worth of mountain, looked over his shoulder, once, at Rudra, and said, with an enormous grin he could not suppress into the shape of any approved family speech:

"Toh le jaaun na inhe!"

"Le jaa." Rudra was laughing despite his ribs, despite the tape at his collarbone, despite even the coal of him banking slow and interested at the circus of human daring being done around it. "Count pe rakh. Chaar-chaar. Aane de inko — pata chalna chahiye unko apna level —"

But the river, un-licensed and possessed of its own entries, got there first.

---

It began as a wrongness in Rudra's own wake.

The wake behind a river-boat follows physics with the loyalty of a wounded soldier; it should fan out V-shaped, two shoulders of brown water peeling off in magnified homage. Their wake today — as Rudra glimpsed it over his shoulder between strokes, face tightening within himself — *sank.* It went from them aft and flattened discreetly into the river's surface as if taking instructions; and a long slack line of calm followed their boat, over the pulled water, as if the river had been ironed where they passed. When he took his next stroke, on the blade's exit — the water under the blade resisted *upward* — against the blade's own recovery — and pushed him gently but unmistakably *astern,* and under his taped sternum that seam stood up at forty million volts of attention, and it was all he could do in that half-second not to yell.

The bottom of the river was looking *at* their boat.

He remembered this wake. He had given this wake to it once before, on a dark night, burning. It had put its mouth, however softly, on Manikarnika and bidden him count. It recognized him now — or, no: not *him*; it recognized **the thing** — the furnace he carried. It was bumping its nose along his hull plates, doggish, meek, enormous, *recalling.*

"Rudra," Veer said, over his shoulder, voice low — eyes forward, hauling. "Ye kaisa gadbad lag raha hai paani. Jaise — *keechad*."

"Kheench raha hai woh cheez," Rudra heard himself answer — level, thrilled-terrified steady — "rok raha hai hume. Competition mein bhi nahi chhodta. **Stroke count dhayaan rakh. Veer — bas chalta rahe.**"

---

And Ira, on the ghat, gasped.

She had followed them by eye from the bank with the race's artillery column of spectators, but instruments kept finding in her hand their own fascinations — the compass had wound itself throughout the start so softly that she'd thought of casing it to its felt pouch; but now, in dead dry air, it was revolving. It was doing the slow willful inspection tour of the compass rose, like an astronaut re-learning which home was. She raised it to the water then. Bright as hook.

Ganga was lying beneath the race: the great darbari water, grandmother-water, mother-water of history — and under it, at forty metres down, a parallel river ran, recent, purpose-made, darkened; it had rope-lines converging under the exact mid-point of the race course; it doubled, sometimes trebled where the pride of seventeen boats' best strokes lashed it; and it gave nothing back. The surface of the official river glittered, sunned itself, accepted adoration. The second river tasted the boat where its shadow darkened the top of it, climbed it thirstily, *sipped,* dropped back into inventory.

The river was being skimmed, and the roadmap of the skimming, in broad daylight, among the boats of celebrating boys, was the Festival's own: six soft tapping-places — like cups taken up by waiters at a wedding — with one drinking-knot off Manikarnika that she *knew,* that she had drawn from a dead man's dark memory with lamp-lit guilt; it had flattened at last by the Dream-side of the walls she had made around it; it had grown *greedy.*

She took the camera of her phone and got, in eight seconds, four photographs of what no camera could photograph — the angle of the rowers' shadows over the race course's surface, clearly showing sheen; the compass needle swinging free across bearing-worn cardinal; the river. Her hands, even for the festival's sake, did not tremble very much. Upstream, where the banyan's ruin still stood wrapped in municipal red cloth like a wounded sentinel, the crows, off for long, all came back at once.

---

On the river, Veer jerked his oar out a half-beat early, sun in his teeth, thought of his mother's flour-smell and any god, and brought the whole craft's speed down off a count with a new motion nobody taught him; somewhere aft he heard Rudra grunt — heard the throttled volume of a rest-day exercise pushing its way back up the boy's spine — and with the whole blaze of fury-feel in him he shouted in a voice of disproportioned happiness —

"Tight! Left! *Stroke!*—"

The boat lifted — a that-sound that is not the water, that is not air; a wet plain *snarl* ran along their keel underside from bow to stern as if a long dog brushed beneath the boards — and they went half-a-boat faster, passing the champions' red trim at the final bend with three-quarters of a length of open river — and crossed the finish drum-buoy at Panchganga into the groan and roar of thousands, and Munna Majhi's twins, who flunked exams with equal patience every year and did not intend to learn, pulled their dying strokes into the crowd-afield like men told good news.

Veer, still inside the noise, dropped the oar's worn handles, caught a whole rack of breath in one drag, and lay entirely backwards across Jalpari's thwart like a wet piece of sacking. Tufts of broken sky lay above him. Somewhere the crowd-kingdom was absolutely that — blankets and clapping — and Rudra, still upright and shaking in every piled-up joint, looked down at the river that ran along its old channels as though nothing had happened.

The wake behind them flattened politely. Then, one hundred feet aft, it stopped moving.

"Chal," Rudra said, a very long way from the register of heroes, and began to steer them, slowly, with people arguing in dozens of directions on the shore, towards the landing-steps; and each row of water coming up against Jalpari's flank touched her as if in sermons — subsided — and retired.

On the steps, the sport that had been played on that water for a hundred years concluded in the hundred-years-old way: without podiums. Munna Majhi's twins climbed out of the red trim boat, spat expertly over opposite shoulders, shook the winners' hands with the complete warmth of the defeated — "Agle saal yahin. Yahi boat. Yaad rakhna." — and were gone into the crowd before the trophy arrived, because losing, if you keep its hours correct, is simply an appointment for next year.

The prize came down through a relay of khaki-shirted organizers: five thousand one rupees in an envelope sealed with cellotape, and the trophy, plastic-and-gold, handed over with both hands by the councillor's nephew, everybody stiff as a wedding photograph. "Haath mein lo, haath!" hissed somebody's uncle from the second step, and Veer took it with both of his — the photo flashed — and for the record of the district's paper-of-note there they stood, two champions grinning with their foreheads shining, a pink bear balloon tied accidentally permanent to Veer's wrist giving the shot its immortal official weirdness, at the edge of the winning-boat two girls looking levelly at the camera in completely different register of not-smiling.

"Champions se nivedan," the announcer's loudspeaker clucked bewildered bilingual, "ki ve log apna ghar-pata stage pe darj karwayen" — which produced general volleys of unrepeatable shore-commentary and the first honest laughter of the day.

"Chalo ghar," Ira called from the high step.

"Ghar chalte hain," Munna-ji agreed from his table, and looked out of his one wide eye at the two boys and past them at the water, at the two rivers lying braided under the dusk, and then up into the sky above the city as if consulting a senior. "Jaldi. Aaj paani bahut *jaag* raha hai."

And nobody who stood there that evening could afterwards have explained why they would remember it as the last warm ordinary dusk — the crowd thinning into the lanes with folded poster-boards, Jalpari riding at her chain among the moored hulks, the lamp-lighters coming out with their long poles like the setting-out of a smaller, insubordinate heaven upon the town.

---

The trophy was plastic and gold-plated in the tradition of everywhere; it had a plastic boat on it. It went home on Veer's lap in the auto, upon his enormous knees, and he held it with the ginger meticulousness of a christening uncle, only saying, once every minute or so, "Behti hui cheez ke upar se jeeta hoon — *jeeta hoon bhai*," unable to do anything else at all with the inside of himself without losing a structured plan of being a person.

It was Aadhya, crammed along with the rest of them into the auto's back seat, who — after watching Ira silently add and subtract line-angles with her eyes the whole way, crossing and uncrossing two rivers only she could see — said into the engine-noise:

"Kal sab milte hain, chai pe. Maine sun liya tum dono ka bachon-wala bhaasha khelna race ke peechhe. Detail mein. *Bataenge.* Sab."

Her voice didn't crack; it carried the complete anti-moral of the hardened local, the girl who had attended prayers with demons in their heart and then gone to buy potatoes. The auto jounced; a small ache ran from the driver's brake through everyone's bones.

And Veer held up the trophy against the streetlight, so the cheap gold flooded them all for one passing second in waves of imitation splendour they did not mind at all — they were too tired, too rowed-out, too *alive* to mind. At the end of the day it was, properly, the first object their four lives had won together, and even Ira leaned around it, once, briefly, a satisfied pride-drunk parenthesis of a person:

"Paanch hazaar ek rupaye," Veer reminded the window, happily, for the eighth time. "Aur *naman*."

"Trophy apne baap ka?" said Rudra.

"Trophy APNA hai, bhai. Kal chhapne waali hai photo — dekh lena akhbaar mein—"

"Mandir arti nahi," Ira corrected without looking up, "marathon coverage hota hai usko."

"Jo bhi. Kal tak poora Nagar Nigam poochhega. *Kaun hai yeh ladke — jo boat wo chala rahe the paani bhaichara—*"

---

That night, after a dinner she ate standing and unremembered, Ira opened the notebook onto one whole page and drew the two rivers.

The first — surface, shown to the spectator — she drew level, in clean ruled strips, the way the official charts had it. Unwavering. Sacred. Zero.

Under it she drew, with two alternating rows of grey storm-hatching, the second river — the one that ran no barges, the one that lay directly beneath the real one's light: its side-gutters opening onto six little inductor-points, and at the seventh — out of scale, lower than truth could be drawn — she hatched the mouth.

She dated the drawing, top right corner, in her most impersonal hand. She marked it:

*SUBJECT: river rendered by halves. OBSERVATION: aaj pehli baar dhoop mein kheencha. It is pacing up its strength.*

Below that she wrote, pencil wearing through the soft paper:

**7 din.**

And then — because her hand had finally been told what kind of arithmetic was imminent, and the arithmetic did not deserve the dignity of more ink than this — just, alone in the middle of the page:

**7.**
