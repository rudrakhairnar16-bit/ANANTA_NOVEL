# CHAPTER 10 — THE PROTECTOR'S FIST

The fight, when it properly began, lasted eleven seconds before it started breaking things that didn't belong to anybody.

Afterwards — afterwards each of them would privately demolish and rebuild the eleven seconds their whole lives, would disagree about order and direction and who had stood where, would agree on nothing except two facts: that something under the ghat had been drinking when it should not have been, and that the fight had ended at a stone pillar with a sound like a mountain coughing itself loose; and that in between there had been *a light* — and that this light had come out of Veer Sawant's right hand and gone back into it again, like a guest who realizes he has the wrong address.

The eleven seconds went, as best as witnesses who were also bleeding could reconstruct them, like this.

The left-hand man with the strut-braced arm — the building-department smile, the dented newel post, the voice of bored municipal kindness — took one interrupted look at his superior, who was still bent over the coil that had mistaken Rudra for a harvest-row. Confusion read upward from operator to operator the way weather moves between fields. The third man, until then silent at the base of the pillar of the ruined gate — a man built like a filing cabinet, with a lanyard-tag that said nothing in grey laminate — moved to close the bag off behind them: eight casual paces, positioning a body the size of a sofa between the boys and the crowd.

Ira moved first. She broke — not toward the fight, not away from it, but across its mean grain, cutting behind the ruined gate's tilting masonry on the diagonal she'd been occupying for forty seconds — the paces between her wrist-strip blackening by the centimetre. She wasn't armed. She didn't intend to be. She had a different idea entirely: kneel at the lean middle man's feet, at his case of brushed steel laid open like a doctor's bag — she had memorized which gear was being fed into what with maps of her own — and throw the brass-and-coil idiot *scavenger* into Ganga six steps away, downstream of ten thousand prayers.

She got both hands on the case.

The left-hand man — who had never once turned his head — moved without turning it.

Later she would try to describe the next two seconds to Rudra on a roof where words would go, and would have to settle for: *it was like watching a train decide to stop.* The man's arm came across through empty air toward her — an arm with a machine's sleeve of alloy mesh, braced about the forearm like a dark puppeteer caging the bone — coming on a line for her face with the detachment of a ticket-stamper —

— and then there came from around the gate's ruin, at a run that wasn't running, a shape.

Veer took the blow on the bandage.

It made a sound. Everyone in earshot — however few of them, inside the midge-cluster of roar the aarti had become — understood that sound as the end of something human. His whole forearm from wrist to elbow went white-hot agony; the skin of it moved visibly sideways and came back; and under his feet the flags of the ghat's old charity-flagstones were station-pegged with something that went out from him through the arches of his soles and came back to him *as load*; and he stood, exactly as he had promised his mother one midnight, small, aged eleven, feverish, waiting with a tumbler of nimbu-pani in service beside her matches — *stand, son, you are a post; your mother is the tent* —

and the strut-braced arm, strong enough to shear a stone newel, *stopped on him.*

Stopped.

And in the stopped one second — held in the palm of that halted blow like the hush after a mis-hit thunderclap — Veer had time for one whole memory, the kind a drowning man is rumoured to receive free of charge. He was nine again, in their lane in Sigra, and the fruit-seller's cart had gone over on its axle-pin at the corner by the temple tank, pinning the old pony; and the men — his uncles, the shopkeepers — had all been arranging to lift, talking it into committees, when nine-year-old Veer had quietly gone under the cart-edge himself and stood up. The cart had risen off his back like a basket off a hook. The pony had scrambled free. The lane had cheered. And he had run home without waiting for the sweets, run home crying, not from pain — there had been no pain — from the *goodness* of it, the sweet, smooth, terrible rightness in his spine when the weight was the most and he was the least broken by it; and his mother, flour-handed at the door, had taken his wet face in both hands and said what mothers say when they are translating God in real time: *Beta, isliye toh strong diya hai. Rakhna. Baten mat.*

He had never told anyone that he had gone back to the same corner, three evenings running, secretly hoping the cart would fall again.

The filing-cabinet man paused mid-stride, something wrong addressing his instincts. The bored kindness drained out of the left-hand man's face. For one breath the two of them — appliance and post — were eye to eye over the halted forearm. The bandage on Veer's forearm was smoking faintly at every seam. And Veer — who carried no groan aloud, who had given the night so far only a couple of closed hasty prayers and one rude joke about plates — felt the thing he had been reporting since the pyre-ghat, the drag of darkness he had been paying for in pharmacy bills, decide that the arrangement was concluded: that whatever had been *transferred into* him at Manikarnika that night — as he kept bed, trembled, and tried to read his palm under tube-light — had come into him not as injury but as deposit; was here now as calling; and stood up out of the small of his back like a tenant given notice, and opened the windows of his right arm.

His right fist caught the light.

Not reflected it. Caught it the way a lamp's flame lights from inside — the knuckles became four small suns behind his own skin; the forearm's old burn lines shone suddenly through the dressing in seams of gold like a paper lantern held to a bonfire; the whole weapon he had dreamt of himself as — the colonial power of him, the peacekeeper of six-feet-four and two hundred and twenty honest-born weights — kindled up in a single position, and every person present, down to the three scouts, understood the difference between a punch and *will* instantly, the way everyone understands the difference between a grocery list and a psalm.

Veer punched.

The lazy kindness had not yet finished leaving the left-hand man's face when the gold arrived at his jaw's angle and undoubtedly completed something in it. The man left the ground. He left the ground with the completeness of a man lifted by carpet — cleanly, wholly, his pivot never found — travelled three body lengths through the October-night ghat-smoke, struck the waist of the great gate-pillar of the fallen shrine — a column of chunar sandstone as old as the first maratha repairs, four feet across its flats, base sunk past Ganga's own memory — and the pillar, which had stood through the flaying of two empires and the appetites of four centuries of river, *cracked through and carried him onward*, crown shearing down.

The falling of it took two seconds exactly.

In those two seconds, the aarti's second mantra was climbing its wheels of flame at the platform; the river answered with its clapped choir of wooden hulls; a woman's conch-armed exhale completed itself — and within the walled noise of all that devotion, inside the little leased privacy behind the gate-ruin, gold light drained out of a fist and nothing in the world noticed, because nothing in the world had been looking — except, sixty feet up the ghat's flank, the third-floor window bars of a sleeping pilgrim-lodge, behind which a very old person sat up out of no reason and put one palm flat against the cold glass, and blinked, twice, at Dashashwamedh, as if a grandchild had called a name in another room.

It took the rest with no sound at all — as if the ghat had considered denying the fact and then thought better of it. The crown of the column went into the river forty feet down with a sound entirely disproportionate, a bloop of stone; the man went into the masonry works below the gate and lay there, his alloy scaffold sparking once in a little electric desperate dying twitch; the remaining two men, operator-surgeons with nerves of calibrated lunch-room steel, did their sums on the spot and found that the locals had acquired manager-grade assets.

The lean middle man looked at the coil — dead now, drained into the blowback; looked at Rudra, at what was written there that he could not see; looked at the fist of the pillar-breaker and did some addressing of reports in his head.

He said one word. Not *retreat* — it was not a soldier's word — a word, rather, of technical scope: *abort.*

The two of them were gone down the northern steps within eight seconds carrying their cases, and by the time they were gone the crowd behind the ruined gate was swarming down the first terrace like water finding its bowl, public opinion rapidly assembling forensics — whose building, whose pillar, whose boys — and the machine of Varanasi rumour, whose opened floodgates no ministry could close, got its head into its hands.

---

Veer stood looking at his own right hand as if someone else had been wearing it.

The gold was gone. It went back down the seams of the old burn like water abandoning the roots it had lifted up; the knuckles were bare skin and blunt ordinary blood, one split across the fourth knuckle, the fist of a boy who had punched something once and remorsefully. He turned the hand over — palm up — and the light there was sodium, and nobody's gold; and something in the way he turned it over, slowly, like a man reading his own suspension-order, made Ira stop from a dead run — eyes burning brine of two careful days turned to molasses in one second — and say,

"Veer."

He put the hand behind his back like it was stolen goods. The trembling had begun in both forearms at once, the kind of deep structural trembling that the body issues when a thing has been asked of it that it never budgeted for. He was whiter than anyone in that crowd seemed entitled to be at three ghats past midnight.

"Maine aise—" He tried his voice out. It failed by a syllable. Tried it again. "Maine *aise kya kiya*—" He looked, for purchase, at the pillar's sheared stump. Pieces of the man he had felled were climbing out from the rubble under their own power with the alert, affectless economy of insects. Out of the smile of the fight the left-hand man's jaw was wrong in a way that looked systemic. His eyes were open. He moved his fingers — one — two — they worked; the scaffold on the arm sparked in sympathy.

"Th-Th-THE—Rudra—"

"Sambhal ke!" Rudra was there — shouldering past the first glance-down faces of the pressing public, catching Veer by the upper arm below the ancients-of-Ranjit-colony tattoo: hauling him round towards the ghat wall where the dark angles of gate rubble would take them all. The crowd behind them broke over the ruined column like a fast tide over a wreck.

"Ladke—!" a panda shouted after them, pointing — "IDHAR AAYA THA NA PATTHAR—"

"Civil wall gira!" Rudra shouted back, turning his head enough for the voice to carry, his face absolutely at peace underneath: seventeen years of good-son training on parade. "Poorana hai! Yahan dekho — sab baras se gira hua hai! Aap idhar mat khade raho—" and he pointed helpfully at the leaning masonry of the ticket-office wall, a standing source of authentic menace, year round.

Public opinion, rudderless, swung its hundred heads to the harmless-seeming structural unsoundness and surrounded it, donating theories freely.

"BMC ka kaam hai—"
"BMC yahan hota hi nahin hai, Nagar Nigam hai—"
"Earthquake tha chhota sa? Maine feel kiya—"
"Beam gira hoga — aarti ki vibration se—"
"Vibration se *pathar* girta hai?" — the oldest man present, offended to his bones — "Beta, pathar KABHI nahi girta. Hum hain toh Banaras hai—"
"Mujhe lagta hai flash-mob tha—"

Somewhere beyond the terraces a police whistle began its long administrative argument with the evening, two sharp notes, then three, then a whole grammar of them, and the crowd performed the mandatory dispersal-shuffle of people who want simultaneously to help and to never be named in connection with anything. A boatman in the second row was already telling a stranger the definitive account: a landslide; no, a cracker; no, a politician's protest; his cousin had seen it personally — all three versions delivered in a single breath with the serene promiscuity of an eyewitness who has seen nothing and will therefore miss nothing.

Behind the corner of rubble Rudra stopped Veer with a hand he didn't know he was using yet. Pulled him back against the wall, braced him face-to-crowd like a product display, and said — casually, loudly, for the ten citizens on the other side of the stones to hear —

"Thoda behosh hai yaar, kuch zyada hi garmi thi na—" the voice of a boy comforting his taller classmate — and into the private breath-space between their sternums, dropping every register out from under his throat: "Chup. Chup reh. *Kuch nahi hua.* Ira — ghabrao mat bas— hato— usko seedha, seedha— Seedha khada karo—" — Veer's knees were another matter, were finding their own independent line of descent down the wall — "—Aadhya— pani— ungliyon pe—"

Veer put his face into the angle of the wall above Rudra's head and said into the stones, very low: "Mujhse gaadi palat jaati toh mujhe pata tha kya main kaun hoon. Pillar nahi palta tha. *Meri muthi palat gayi.*" He paused to reassemble himself. "Ladke se lekar police tak sab pooch rahe rahenge. Jhooth bola maine. Abuse bhi kiya. Thappad maar dala aadmi ko. Par pillar, Rudra — pillar maine giraya. Muthi se. Cheez jo mein hoon usme aisi cheez kahan se aa gayi?"

"Ruk," Rudra said.

"Rudra—"

"Ruk." Something in Rudra's chest — the thing that had lain under his mother's knit-boned morning work and his father's uneasy hammer-nights and now stood slightly more visible than it had yesterday, not even three hours ago — gave a seam's fine rebus of pain. Under his kurta collar, where the cloth until that night had been empty pavements, a hairline — a thread of warm geometry, silver-dark and straight — flexed awake for exactly the duration of one heartbeat, and banked down, and Rudra, whose body had been raised all its life to read the inner furniture of training, knew what he had just been informed of.

He kept his voice. Kept it the entire way down to even.

"Aaj raat tu zyada kehta hai toh sab pakda jayega," he said. "Kal baat karte hain. *Kal.* Aaj: bhaago. Bheed mein raho. Kuch nahi hua. Hum aarti dekhne aaye the. Sabko yahi bolna hai."

Through the gravel underfoot, beneath the week's collected dirt and the reverent spill of ghiya from a hundred boats' shoulders and the conch-thrum changing its speed toward second mantra — dark water filled the city's bucket, counted by ten gold things and two hands over an elbow. Four city lads and lasses with a blackening receipt stood in the crush of the oldest evening on the river, in the smoke, receiving the crowd's elbowing benign oblivion like taxis. None of them could have said what they'd seen.

Below them all, at forty-five feet under the platform's flagstone, a strand of the ring the last of the coil's salvaged leak ran rewoven — snapped — and the drain beneath it opened its little black ear, half an inch wider.

---

They stood for a while where the north lane took them — in the dark passage with the broken streetlight two ghats above the aarti — each of them facing out, a box-formation without anyone meaning it.

The last chai-stall of the lane was down to its embers; the chaiwala, a vertical skinny man dozing upright against his own kettle like a bird on a branch, sold them four glasses anyway — not because four glasses is profit, but because four young people standing silent in a broken formation at eleven at night, dust on three of them and one of them white as paper, is a sight that Varanasi chaiwalas process without comment, and with extra adrak.

Nobody said anything for the first two sips.

Then Aadhya took Veer's right hand, and Veer let her — and that was a new thing, that letting — and turned it, palm up, under the stall's weak bulb. The skin of the knuckles was split in two places, ordinary split skin, ordinary bone-colored bruises coming up under it, ordinary boy. She looked at it a long time, fingertip hovering just above the fourth knuckle, not touching, because out of superstition or science she was afraid she'd be able to feel the thing through the skin if she touched it; and behind her eyes, unsaid, the arithmetic of her own night three days ago at the banyan's roots — measured now against *this* — and the estimates she came to made her shut her eyes.

"Garam tha?" she asked finally, very low.

Veer thought about it for some time. "Garam nahi," he said. "…Accha laga." His voice went small in a way nobody had ever heard from Veer before, the voice of a man confessing to a diary. "Ek second ke liye. Sabse zyada accha jo maine kabhi feel kiya. Yehi dara raha hai mujhe. Pillar nahi."

No one said anything. The kettle sighed like the long-suffering matron of all of them.

"Kal college mein," Ira said, "sabko lagna chahiye ki hum aarti mein the aur kuch gadbad hui aas paas, hum ghabra gaye aur ghar aa gaye. That's it. That's the whole story. Jo bhi udhar hua —" her eyes went once to Veer's fist, once to Rudra's sternum, once, by accident, to her own blackening wrist-strip still folded in her sleeve, "— chaaron jaante hain. Kisi paanchve ko nahi. Police ko nahi. Ghar pe nahi."

"Parents ko nahi?" Veer looked up. I've never lied to my mother in my life, his face said, with the authority of the genuinely blessed. It was a real question.

"Tere ghar pe bhi nahi, gadhe," Rudra said, and then stopped, because the memory of his own father standing on a roof with a staff across his knees had come up in him like a wave and he was no longer sure what he was arguing; and started over, gentler: "Baad mein. Jab pata ho kya bolna hai. Abhi bolenge toh... wo log sabko door kar denge. Ghar samajhna."

And there it was — out in the air, in a broken lane, over four glasses of adrak chai — the pact. Nobody signed it. Everybody did.

"Lekin ek baat," Veer said, looking down the lane toward the river-glare. "Woh teen — wapis aayenge."

"Hmm," Ira said. "Log hain. Ladke nahi. Organization hai unke peeche. Loudspeaker wala din nahi tha aaj unka — trial tha. Instruments test ho rahe the." She finished her glass and set it down on the counter with the exactitude of a seal on a letter. "Aaj aaye the toh kal aayenge. Aage se milte rahenge."

"Toh hum taiyaar karenge," Veer said.

"Tum teen taiyaar karo," Ira said flatly. "Main ginti karti hoon."

They drank. Somewhere on the river a lone oar creaked against its pin.

"Ek cheez aur," Ira said at last. "Steel wala case. Maine dekha. Side pe stamped tha."

The top of Veer's head, against the wall, did not move.

"Aur—?" Aadhya said.

"Do shabd. Aage A. Pichhe M." Ira didn't take her eyes off the north steps. "Angrezi ke do words. *Aegis Meridian.*" She spelled both of them, in case the dark had misspelled them in air, and then said the two words aloud a second time, flat, without hatred or fear, in the manner of a notary sealing a document — and the alley, its drains and drains' disagreeable godhood and all, the commercial radio up the lane issuing *Jaanch jaari*, took the two words into its understory with the obedience of all Varanasi.

Rudra closed his eyes. In the dark of them a hairline — half an inch long, on the inside of his sternum, under his collar, where sweat cooled — could not quite be banished.

"Ira," he said.

"Hmm."

"Note kar lena. Kal subah chai pe batana mujhe. Kahan se shuru hota hai, kitne din, sab."

"Kitne din ka, main pehle se jaanti hoon." Iris-thin light from a far lamp-shrine moved across her expressionless face. "Gyarah. Bas gyarah bache hain."

---

At home — a little after one — Rudra stood before the bathroom mirror with his shirt off and the light off, and read his chest with his eyes shut.

With the eyes shut it was clear — clearest, in fact: the body as a country lit from beneath by the road-work of its own power lines, all the wire-geography he had been born with, the dark's accurate survey. Where the light ran it ran to the measure drilled into him since two; where it dampened it held the seventeen-year-old plaster of his parents' impossible marriage of intents: one road at the throat, a ring of heavy gates at the breastbone, and now — hair-fine, the length of half a breadstick, exactly honest as all his father's damage had ever been — a seam.

He put the heel of one wrapped hand to it, and breathed, and counted, and felt his mother's lullaby come up in him on its own — wordless — its eleventh note hanging like one arm raised at the door.

*Main kya kar sakta hoon, Ma?* he had asked her, three nights before — and she had looked down at him with nineteen years standing in her eyes, and said:

*Sab kuch, beta.*

---

One floor below, probably for the last hour, two people who had not slept were pretending they had.

Rukmini sat with her back against the kitchen's doorframe, on the floor, in the posture of a woman on a train platform, the folded towel still over her shoulder like a field scarf. She had been there since the gate's iron voice announced him at one bell — had stood to come up, and sat down again — because she had heard her son go past her door and there were ten things wrong with the walk and she had known them, all ten, knot by methodical knot, the way you count rosary: *left shoulder low; heel-strike soft — favouring the ribs; breath on a count of four he'd set himself because his own wouldn't hold; energy* — and that was where her counting stopped being anything a mother in Varanasi could say aloud.

Up the stairs, on the rooftop under the cold standing garnets of November, Ram sat with his staff across his lap and the night x-ray weather flowering slow over the northeast — where, if the mind's instruments were trusted, the river-town was trending midnight-blue and hungry — and he had been sitting exactly like that, changeless, since eight. He had heard the gate at one. He had heard his son's ten wrong things on the stairs. He had not gone down, because a sentry does not leave a wall and because the particular kind of wrong that was in those footsteps had, once, long ago, been in his own.

"Neeche aa ke so ja," Rukmini called quietly from the terrace door — she had come up after all, with the water lota the worst excuse in the world. "Barah baj gaye."

"Tune suna?" Ram asked the horizon.

She came and stood beside the parapet, near enough to be merciful, far enough to be obedient. "Suna."

"Fight," he said. It wasn't a question. "Ghat pe kuch hua. Ladke ka chalna badal gaya. Ek kadam aisa tha jaisa—" He stopped. For a moment the two of them stood looking at two different counties of the same dark.

"Khud se lad raha tha koi?" Rukmini asked, still quietly, eyes on her own folded fingers — and the question was a hundred-pound wager she placed, offhand, like a loafer's coin at the tea-stall, as if mothers didn't bet their thoraxes on just such turns of phrase.

"Khud se nahi," Ram said — very slow. "Khud ke andar waale se." And then he said nothing more at all, and he kept the staff's weary companionship, and somewhere over the city beyond numberless roofs a cloth-banner hauled over a two-lane intersection — *DASHASHWAMEDH AARTI — COMMEMORATIVE* — shivered in the faint night-offshore as if the words themselves had overheard something through the wall and wanted to be taken down.

She stayed there with him without going any nearer until the watch-feel of the hour hand moved her. She did not ask when it would happen. And he didn't tell her what was said to be coming, and the years of not-telling wore all the preliminary faces of his love.

Outside, the lane dog finished its night-check of the thresholds and climbed onto the newspaper stack outside the shuttered paan kiosk and arranged itself to sleep. Over the river two districts away, the ghat's prayer-smoke roved in the dark — losing its edge, thickening slowly, settling like a closed hand.
