# CHAPTER 15 — THE LIBRARY

The morning of it, at home, had insisted on being ordinary first.

Breakfast. Her father's chair — pulled out, sat in, asked over: an accountant of familial habits, measuring her silences by teaspoons. Her mother, who for three weeks had managed a tense up-curve towards entente — *zoo se phone aaya tha, college jaa rahi ho na* — brought the dal to table with her own unusual stint of looking directly, and said, standing over her daughter's chair with both House-lamps lit:

"Chaudah din ho gaye us raat ko."

"Haan, Ammu."

"Bina raat ke kuch bhi khaya kiye ho tum?" It was a sentence almost the same shape as her habitually careful ones, and at the same time a precisely and intricately formulated sentence that had, visibly, been rehearsed; its edges wavered wherever it had had to carry a weight. "Subah se bhuk lagi hogi. Pakode bana rahi hoon thodi der mein. Tum college se aa jao toh dono saath khayenge."

"Aa jaungi, Ammu. Paanch tak." She took this as well as its promise allowed her. Her mother nodded tersely, and the whole silent ever-understood treaty of mornings was, by all surface measures, at end.

Her father, from the courtyard's other doorway, in a morning-calm voice too consistent with his normal volume for anyone who did not know the family: "Nayi kitaab kharidoge toh bill le aana. Hisaab complete rahega."

It was its own translation: *tumhe pata hai, humein pata hai; hamein bill chahiye, tumhe cover chahiye.* But what stayed with her all day was the way, without ever checking, without ever even looking at her, both parents had each in their own idiom promised to keep the door open as long as the secret kept itself — the way houses can practice fine border-mercy without any treaty-paper at all. "Papa—" she had to stop. "Haan. Bill le aaungi." The gate clicked its usual dignity behind her.

"Tab se kabhi dikhti ho ruk ke," her mother added at the door, in the tone of somebody closing inventory. She put down the empty bowl and looked at Aadhya's hands. Only that: at the hands. Then, entirely lightly, in the direction of the windows: "Padhna mat zyada raat ko. Aankhein kharab hoti hain. Aaj jaldi aa jana, padhayi ke saath-saath khaane ka bhi time table banao."

Aadhya went, with something like a long-held breath finally released, and knew — as she had known since that night — that the mothers of this city can be fluent in their daughters without translation, and that her mother had offered a treaty of secret-keeping, singularly and unmistakably deliberate. She touched her mother's hand once, leaving; the hand only returned the pressure; neither of them had spoken of it at all.

The 8:40 shared-auto carried her down the avenue like nothing special — three girls behind handkerchiefs, a procession of bicycle bells, dust and sun.

---

The Central Library stood on the campus' far side like the serious elder of all its buildings — tall teak stacks, fans hanging off rods like sleeping fruit-bats, clerestory lights gone umber through nine monsoons of uncleaned panes, dust ruling every surface with the unchallenged tenure of a founding family. The present government of it was administered from the issue desk by Shastri-ji — officially retired, officially re-engaged, in actuality irreplaceable — a man with a white parted beard and a cataloguer's spinal curve, known on campus to have memorized the location of every book in the building in protest against the computer.

Aadhya came in on a Wednesday afternoon with her roll-bag and her reasons and signed the register, under discipline, in a column of other people's Wednesdays.

She had decided, on Sunday night, lying awake, that she was done being the only person on the crew who studied nothing. Veer had his arm. Rudra had his tapes. Ira had her whole mad attic-instrument-room. Aadhya meant this with no vanity: she wanted, ten nights after her leaf turned grey-headed at the banyan's roots, to have *data* — on record, in some authoritative neutral voice printed decades before she existed — about what a tree remembers when it dies standing; about the way the city's underground was plumbed; about the ring. About anything.

The banyan had made her a promise she could not name. The library, she reasoned, was where promises get filed.

---

Botany first — the respectable runway. She spent the first hour among hardbound monographs and journals in the Plant Sciences alcove, looking for anything at all on *Ficus benghalensis* and sudden necrosis; found, as she suspected, decades of work on roots and none on ghosts. The genus-entries said the same calm things — aerial prop roots, canopies of acres, tremendous longevity — and she turned pages feeling the dry opacity of scholarship answer her, and she could not have said exactly when she first felt it. Somewhere between hour one and hour two, in the alcove's deep quiet, she became aware that she was leaning — that her body had, unasked, begun pressing its weight into the shelf's flank as if into friendly bark.

She pulled herself upright, embarrassed. The shelf's wood was kind as always, inanimate. Nobody had been back here among the low cabinets since the term's last exam; the old stacks smelled of night-glue and ingested rain.

On impulse — because she had learned, over eleven strange nights, that the only tool she owned was attention — she put her palm flat against the inside upright of the racked shelf and *asked.* Not aloud. Not even in words: the way she'd asked the marigold once, a year ago, in the mela-line of memory when a crushed flower half stood up under her thumb — with presence, with the offer of her pulse as an ear.

The wood answered. Faintly. Ridiculously.

It informed her that it had once been a tree forty miles east, carted in as logs on bullock-wagon roads that had become tramlines that had become nothing; that it had spent its own youth shaking in wind it missed like a marriage; that it was, as shelving, exactly as happy as it could afford to be, in a world made of its body's purposes; and that against the East wall (her heart knocked) there was a shelf where a book had been opened so often at two particular gatherings that the spine still kept the memory of a reader's hands, eleven years of warmth pressed into the hinge like oil into a door-frame.

She stood with her palm resting flat on teak and did not let herself weep because there was a reader-girl two alcoves over and she refused to be *that* girl in the quiet section.

She tried Indology.

There, the card catalog — a wooden cabinet older than her grandmother, brass tab-handles worn pale — brought up what she had secretly been hunting: a whole nest of references on the **Pancha Koshi Yatra**. Books from another century when pilgrim-geography was still a science men confessed to publicly: an 1876 route-survey with unfolding linen plates; a Vishwanath-era miscellany in two bindings; an octavo bound in red with the ghost-of-the-colophon smell of the good stuff; a thin green folio with the District Board seal on its spine — the encyclopoedia of this region's charitability, temple administration, walking customs, well-maintained since by people who wore sweaters.

She took them to the alcove's long table. She had the feeling — foolish, narcotic — that the room had pulled in closer to listen.

For the next two hours she read with the ferocity of a person researching her own illness in a hospital corridor. The 1876 route-survey, opened to its linen plate, gave her the first clean picture: the circuit's five kos, the dharamshalas at the cardinal stands, the halting-stones where processions changed their shoulders — and, running faintly under the walker's tracks in the plate's faded blue, *the wells*. Not water wells: the old time-marks of the circuit, halting-niches of a different plumbing entirely, each drawn as a small lotus-notch at the plate's margin, each accompanied by the surveyor's bored pencil: *tank, dry; tank, dubious; tank, visited only by certain families.* Six of them stood, on the plate, at exact intervals around the circumference, and the seventh stood at its heart — she put her ruler-straight stare against it and verified, mark by mark, the same seven that Ira had ringed in red across three weeks of compass-drift: N-3 among them, sitting where the campus lawns remembered a great old tree.

The miscellany's second binding told her who had *tended* the stations. That was the section with the pages missing — and now she understood why the excisions were cleanest there: it was the registry of the *keepers* — which family watered which niche, which line of widows had fed which halting-point since before the Company, whose diwans had signed the temple accounts in the famine years, and (she discovered by measuring the size of the gap with one finger along the spine) roughly forty further pages on the *history* of one such line of keepers — a line that in this registered copy ceased, exactly, to exist at all.

Of the name of that line, every copy of every volume retained precisely nothing. Where it should have stood, the binding's heart opened on the brown canyon of tape.

She wrote the date-patterns down. She copied the marginals letter by letter, tiny ergonomic script, her father's hand over her shoulder. She wrote, at the bottom of her own page, slower: *Somebody spent sixty years removing a name from the shelves. Somebody else spent those same years learning where the holes were. And on the books' spines the blade-hands came back to keep the hole neat — three weeks ago — when a lamp cracked at a ghat and the city began, every night, to go gently more dim.*

And below that, inside a square bracket, in her own hand, before her nerve could repeal it:

*[Is this what the tree saw, when it looked down into the ground?]*

---

The Pancha Koshi entries were themselves. Perfectly ordinary, perfectly devout: the circuit, the five kos, the halting-points where pilgrims took the night's shelter (the colonial surveyor's spellings mangled like pilgrims' feet on stones); the famous old fact that half the circumference lay off-road even in 1876 because the route pre-dated roads; and column by column, the unchanging round phrases about *accumulated merit* describing what the yatra stored where it walked.

She wrote, dizzy with small gratitude, with both hands.

Three references in, the mischief began.

She had noticed the pattern as a coldness before she noticed it as a fact. Book four — the octavo — opened shyly, on pasted endpapers left over from reigns. The section on the five-kos circuit was there. The section on the sacred geography of the region's lineages was there — **in theory.** Thirty pages were missing. Not torn in anger, not budget-cut: *removed* — excised — with fine straight blades, exactly to the innermost 2mm of the spine, like teeth drilled out by someone who cared about mouths. Where those pages should have opened there was a two-page trench of quiet, and on the remaining stub-leaf, in penciled minuscule hand, half-erased by someone else's rubber years ago, a column of marginalia bent to accommodate itself:

*…par itihaas koi ghar nahi hota jis mein sabke naam likhe hon…*

*…yatra the nadi ke niche dhara chhodti hai…*

*…parampara se bada keval ek bar jo asli ho chuka ho…*

And below them, at the bottom of the stub — in a different hand: darker pencil, later, energetic enough to pressure through onto the second stub —

*…iska poora naam mat dhundo. Isme faayda nahi hai…*

She sat for a full minute with her finger resting halfway across those words, looking down through them with all her attention, like water looking through ice.

Book five had the same surgery. Book six, the green District Board folio, same. In each, the excision was exact to the same two gatherings: the lineage-section of the old geographies, and the compound gazette of the ring's holy tree-stations — who tended which, in whose family's keeping. In each, someone's hand had closed each wound with brown library tape, in years when tape was not issued, by hands which knew the alcove's job.

Someone had gone, with plain industry, through *every copy* of the region's old pieced-together sacred geography, and removed the same pages from all of them.

Her hands had gone cold somewhere in the middle. She registered this the way another woman registers rain — academically, from behind glass — and kept going back through the alcove, volume by volume. The excision was not despair, not vandalism. Vandalism jabs. This was a scalpel's trace: sixty pages removed from copy after copy after copy, and each wound finished with archive-grade attention, and each tape-seal a slightly different width — *not one year's work. Several years*. Whoever did it had come back, again and again, improving, rehearsing concealment when concealment was no longer concealable; they had, in places, replaced what they took with cut-down leaves from condemned duplicates, so that page-counts would pass a hurried audit, and only the truly patient would ever find the joints where the paper's age betrayed the swap. After the third book she understood the shape of the thief's attention: it was not stealing treasure. It was *censoring a lineage* — removing the location-record of a stream of nursed memory so thoroughly that even the librarians forgot there had ever been water there.

She worked through two hours of this as if walking a long road at night, eyes grounded on the stones immediately ahead, careful not to look up at the distance of it all. If she looked up — she knew — she would be overtaken by the biggest thing her hands had not yet found the courage to hold: the realization that *someone had known there was knowledge worth this much surgery*, generations before any coil-holding men in grey windcheaters came asking about a family's history with the nerve to bend over her roll-bag outside a college gate.

She was not the first person to search these shelves. That was explicable. What she was was the *only other,* and her predecessors — plural — had left, unmistakably, the signs of their passing: anachronistic rubber-bands gone to petrified rope; a child's homework-page marker wedged into a 1931 finance report at exactly the relevant table; shavings of blade-cut page-edge caked yellow-brown along two of the spines — holding, still, after all these waiting years, the faintest exhausted smokiness of incense. Whoever owned these earlier hands had not trusted librarians with where they had been. They had not even trusted each other.

And under the last stub of the last folio, deep into the crease, there was a librarian's pencil-line note, on the origins-page, right against the table of contents — in that darker, later hand — three words:

**Stop looking.**

---

Twenty minutes later, heart finally consenting to slow to a jog, she went back through the register copies at the issue desk — the visitor-books of decades — and found them in order:

Nineteen years ago: an officer's hand, long and elegant, blue ink, the name entered eleven times at three-month intervals — then, at some final spring date, simply stopping, the way a heart stops when its owner's work moves elsewhere. The hand was the near-brother of a handwriting she knew from her own father's letters; near enough to make her wrist move in sympathy before her mind understood why. But the register's name was not his. Another man's. A name she did not know, filed against the same stacks, the same alcove, the same requests.

Before him — a generation earlier — a second visitor: the pencil-neat hand of the stub-marginals themselves, the same cadence, cleared through the same desk. Water had damaged two of those entries. On the third, the blue-black letters floated loose but legible. She read what she could and closed the file gently, the way you close a door on a room where somebody else's grandfather is sleeping.

And the last name — the one that stayed with her — was dated twenty-one days ago. A Monday. Cramped modern handwriting, no indigo, no fountain pen, ballpoint's shallow trench. Two weeks after the burning night at the ghats. Logged twice again in the fortnight since. Always the alcove. Always Indology. Never issuing — only reading, only cutting.

*Who else,* she wondered, the cold climbing her in slow administrative layers, *in this city brings a blade to a library?*

She put her palm flat against a shelf upright — the way, lately, she was learning one did, to check. The old wood was drowsy and kind; there was gratitude banked in this building the way it was banked in prayer-halls — three generations of opened books and wandering readers stamping the air of the alcove with their appetites — and under the gratitude, ever so faint under the floor's two tons of sleeping records, she felt the other presence also: the drilled agency of the blade-hands, still fresh, like chalk-lines on a floor where a body had been.

She pulled her hand back before the shelves could begin to answer in detail, folded every paper she had copied into her roll-bag, and walked the length of the silent reading room with the exact, unhurried calm of a person who has rehearsed, all week, not running.

---

Back at the issue desk, she took a year's borrowing file squarely in both arms.

The library was emptying around her in the daily ritual — readers folding gone light into their bags, stools being hooked under tables with the settled choreography of long tenancy — while outside, the campus honked and went by with the luck of buses. Shastri-ji looked up because his catalog of gestures was large enough to include reader-departures.

"Almirah B," she said, as innocently as she could arrange. "Wo purane gazetteers — unke reference mein ek naam tha. *Shastri* likha tha notes mein. Aap jaante honge."

The old librarian looked at her for a while over his spectacles with the deliberation of a man whose job, vocationally, was to distinguish one edition from another. Then he smiled, a true one, sad at the edges.

"Gazetteer aaj pandrah saal mein teesra insaan khole hai," he said. "Pehle Pandey sahab — jo ab sheher mein nahi hain; unke kiiye khud hi utha ke diya tha table. Phir ek aur sahab — unka naam nahi bataya jaa sakta, unhone likh ke diya tha ki nahi batana. Dono ne wahi pages maange the jo aaj aapko milte nahi." He tapped the register's closed cover twice, forefinger only, like a man tapping the lid of a well to check if things echo. "Beta. Kabhi-kabhi books ke liye tape theek hota hai. Logon ke liye nahi. Jo nikaala gaya, uske aadhe gharon ke bache ab bhi is sheher mein hain. Aisa mat sochna ki kaagaz tote gaye toh baat tootti hai."

She stood with her roll-bag hugged, and her heart going deliberately at the doorway of every cold idea possible, and asked the only safe question available:

"Un dusre sahab ko main pehchaanungi kaise?"

"Pehchaan rahi ho tumhe," said Shastri-ji gently, snapping the register shut on her name like a boltsman going home. "Kal aa jaana. Kal bhi kholuunga." A pause; the old face did something complicated, and decided. "Meri apni diary hai. Chhattis saal yahan bitaye hain — jo mitaaya jaata tha, jo shabd jhanke kaate jaate the, main raat ko apni diary mein utaar leta tha. Tape ki bhi umar nahi hoti zyada. Kaagaz phool jaata hai, shabd mera reh jaata hai." His smile creased the ends of his eyes wide. "Kal do baje. Lekin ek sharat — kisi ko mat batana. *Kisi ko.* Is sheher mein jo log sach ki hifazat karte hain, ve haar baithe aadmi jaise chalte hain — aur mujhe aise hi jeena aaya."

Aadhya did not thank him. She bowed — a motion from nowhere, a muscle of the correct kind — and went out into the moth-coloured hallway with the day's heat still held in the stones, and the first feather-touch of hope at her ribs.

The hope kept for seven seconds.

At the gate's book-return bin she stopped dead: leaning against the pillar beside it, reading the newspaper back-to-front in an acrylic gesture, was a man in a grey windcheater — too bald, too weather-safe for the mosquito hour, who collected his weight with his shoulders as if he frequently carried something in his pockets which she very well knew. He did not look up. He did not, as far as her terrified eyes measured, *need* to.

She struck north instantly, up the principal avenue of students — alive, peopled, fully sunlit — and a shared auto bore her off along the great lanes home.

At the turning she permitted herself one glance back. The man had not moved at all; the newspaper was being read with total absorption, back page first, a posture sustained with theatrical discipline past all reason of a man's comfort, past even the property of patience — and only then, from the moving conveyance, did it dawn on her that the newspaper's sheet — the front side visible only beyond his shoulder — displayed a page of the paper whose morning headline she had read over the poha: *GHAT STAMBHA GIRAA: JAANCH JAARI*. He had opened the paper to the *last* page, to read it, and he was not reading the last page.

She got the auto's number from the numberplate box of her proper instincts, filed it in the deepest pocket of her routine, and did not think of it again in the auto, not even once, her pulse and the vehicle's rattle keeping one strictly separate account; the driver's mirror was full of the road behind and had nothing in it; but for the five kilometres home she kept herself from turning around in any case, a posture of an inch of the head that cost her an hour of peace later.

---

The Central Library's best lamps went out at six. Shastri-ji, his day's administration finally completed, walked from the issue desk to the alcove he remembered being prepared to like in 1991, put his hand in a specific place on the Catalogue cabinet, took out a rubber-banded notebook no wider than a newspaper column, and wrote in it one line in his customary Devanagari — very old, very short:

*Aaj teesri aayi. Sahas hai. Ankhon mein sawaal hai, haar mein nahi. Uparwala saath de.*

---

She rode back to Lanka in a shared auto with her roll-bag against her chest like a baby during an emergency landing, and with every lurch of its banking the evening's cargo rearranged itself inside her: the torn books; the same excised gatherings in copy after copy; the two earlier readers going three-monthly to the same well; the third one — three weeks ago, ballpoint — cutting as if the books were vegetables and dinner was at last due; *Stop looking*, one hand; and her own hand, steadying itself on the auto's frame-rail, going cold all through.

At her street-corner, paying the driver, she found the second note.

It was folded twice, taped to the underside of her roll-bag's flap — placed there sometime between the alcove and the bus, by hands she had sat twenty feet from for an hour and had never once noticed — and it said, in the darker, later pencil:

**Stop looking. Main dekh rahi hoon.**

She stood on her own pavement in the day's last violence of birdsong, with her own gate in view and her mother's balcony chair in it, and she made herself a promise cheaper and heavier than any other she had sworn that month: that she would not tell her mother about the library, and that she would — immediately, tonight, before courage had time to open account — tell the other three *everything*.

And below the pavement's four mineral inches of skin, under the auto's humming diesel trail and the tailings of the evening's last traffic, the old city went on with the day's final settling of its warmth — some of it blessedly borne by her neighbourhood, much of it older gratitude, all of it being carried down, slowly, through the counting stations, on somebody's measured orders.

---

In her room that night she did two slow, practical things.

First: she sat herself under the desk lamp with a square of cotton cloth and inspected her own hands. She had washed them ten thousand times in her ordinary life — after trains, after hospital visits, after mela food — but tonight it was inventory, not hygiene; her palms under the lamplight like a pair of confiscated instruments. She looked a long time at their fine lines. She looked for any seed of stranger-colour under the skin and found nothing. She looked at the thumb that had left a green shadow on bark one night, and said aloud to no one:

"Thoda sabr karo. Kuch seekh lunga— *seekh lungi* pehle."

Then she took out her practical file and a pencil, and spent the next hours making her Day's work look like homework: a clean ruled index of everything she had copied from the torn books — neat, cross-referenced, exactly as a student files notes against a viva. If anyone asked: Pancha Koshi project. Pilgrimage routes of the old city. Geography elective. The evidence smiled back innocently off the page; but inside its marginal numbers, inside the gaps of a photocopied plate, it silently carried the trench-map of what had been stolen, by whom, over how many years.

Around eleven her mother came in with warm milk — the eleven-o'clock milk, oldest treaty in the building — and did not ask anything. Aadhya covered the two names with a page-marker, took the katori with due seriousness, and sat drinking in long thinking sips while her mother, pausing once at the door, said:

"Kal college hai?"

"Shanivaar hai. Library jaa rahi hoon. Ek pustak waale Dada hain wahan — purane gazetteer ke boss — unke paas kuch hai. Kal poochungi."

Her mother looked at her once, sidelong, measure and mercy combined, and left the door arranged in the house's old shared silence.

Aadhya turned the lamp low and slept. For the first time in weeks of being marked, she chose not to listen for what was moving under the city — chose it deliberately, like closing a window — and slept the unreasonable sleep of someone who had, at last, work to do.
