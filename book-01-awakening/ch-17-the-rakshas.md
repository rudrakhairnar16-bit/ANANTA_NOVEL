# CHAPTER 17 — THE RAKSHAS

Friday came up out of the river-fog like a creditor's knock.

Ira was at the chai stall at five exactly — college-peeche-wali, where the adrak went in double and the biscuit tin had rusted in a decorative way — notebook already open on the tin table, held flat by a stone. The other three arrived in ones and twos, out of breath, out of excuses, entirely present. The stall's radio burbled the local evening news: a second water-main failure, this one on the west end. Nothing visible. Everything on record.

"Paanch," Ira said, in place of greeting.

Nobody asked paanch-kya. Not any more.

"Kal raat," Ira said, flipping a page, "jab hum terrace pe the — toast, glass, glass ka chhin — maine kahan tha ki woh bhi ek diya hai. Subah tak kheench liya gaya. Aaj subah naya count liya." She touched the number in the margin with two fingers, the way you touch a letter from someone who cannot write back. "Chhe se seedha — *paanch.*"

"Ek hi raat mein ek din gira?" Veer put his elbow down slow.

"Tum chaaron jab ek saath sach bolte ho na," said Ira, "toh ek jagah bahut roshni ek saath jal jaati hai." She drew the stub of pencil across the notebook — the city's circle, seven points, and the heavy dot at the heart. "Unko wohi chahiye. Mela ke din double gaya tha. Kal raat poora ek din zar gaya. *Khushi sabse amir anna hai unki kheti mein.*"

"Toh ab? Khush mat ho?" Aadhya asked, dry.

"Nahi." Ira shut the notebook. "Khushi rok nahi sakte — rokna bhi nahi chahiye, varna jeet ke baad zinda kaise raho ge. Lekin *ginti* toh chalni chahiye. Har din subah main measure lungi. Tumlog apne-apne kaam jaldi-se-jaldi pakad lo. Har chore who thing — jaldi, aur wapas." Her eyes went once to Rudra's kurta — to the taped chest under it — and away, clean as a scalpel. "Aur jo chipaya hua hai raaz, usse dekhte raho. Akele mat dekho."

The chai came. Steam went up. Below the table, out of the stall's little light, the evening opened its first door.

Aadhya had carried something since Wednesday and could not carry it alone any more. "Kal do baje main Shastri-ji se mili," she said. "Unhone apni diary dikhai. Chhattis saal ka apna register — jo kaata gaya sarkari kaagaz se, woh unhone raat raat bhar utara. Ek naam baar-baar aata hai. *Ek widow ka.* Uski line ne stations sambhale — sau saal tak. Aur phir poora naam hi kaat diya gaya, jagah-jagah." She laid her ruled page flat; on its corner, in an old blue hand, the diary-margin sentence she had copied: *last survivor's family — address destroyed.* "Register mein teesri entry — pehli baar likhne waala — nineteen saal pehle. Officer jaisa haath. Naam ka pehla akshar *R*."

She said it without looking at Rudra, and Rudra did not move, and the other two watched both of them with the care of people who have promised to ask the truth and are paying an instalment early.

"Aur mujhe follow kiya ja raha hai," Aadhya added, levelly. "College gate. Akhbaar peeche se padhne waala."

"Sirf tumhe nahi," Rudra said. He did not point. They followed his eyes: across the lane, the same grey windcheater, today employed as a rickshaw-repair man, a wrench leaning unused in one patient hand. "Kal se hum inse chhupte nahi. Chhupna unka kaam hai — humara nahi. Dekhenge seedha. Bhaagne ka nahi. Aur kisi ko chot nahi — apni jaan bachani hai, bas."

"Bas," Ira agreed. "Apni jaan. Ghar waqt pe. Sab ko sab bataana."

They drank. The sun slid lower. Somewhere under the table, under the street, under the whole tired beautiful city, the heart knock the crews had learned to hear gave its nine-second note, a fraction louder than yesterday, and none of them said so aloud.

---

The city's darkness arrived between two chutneys. Rudra went home; he had given his word. Rukmini sat by the window with the kanjeevaram pulled over her shoulders against the cooling air, reading the week's folded paper, her sore foot resting on a stack of folded boat-blankets that still smelled vaguely of the race.

"Mainaaj aath tak aa jaunga," Rudra told her.

She nodded without looking up, considered the paper's wedding announcements again, and said, "Jamun ka paudha aa raha hai." She had wanted, since before her operation, a small fruit tree for the courtyard's blind corner. "Shaam-shakkar waala nahi. Asli jamun. Bholenath wale laaye hain mandi se, kal lagana."

"Haan," he said. And there was no strangeness in the courtyard at all, just a woman and a boy and the leaves that were coming.

Ram was upstairs at the storeroom lamp. The lane's evening hush had settled onto the doors as though an invisible army of sweepers had passed. Rudra washed, ate standing against the wall, listening to the downstairs clock hum its way toward the hour of its own nightly prayer.

At seven-forty he stood in the storeroom doorway, hands loosely together, saying nothing.

"Jaa," Ram said, without lifting his head from the awl. "Le apna sab saath."

And so the chest-tape went on, in the two-minute laying of cool neem it always was, thong and diagonal wrap drawn with the practiced cruelty of mercy across the heat that had been building under his ribs all day. His father's hands were steady as instruments. When it was done, the eyes met once, and in them was neither question nor permission — only the private arithmetic of a man who had survived four decades of knowing, and who had promised himself he would never explain. They said their ordinary small goodnight; and the boy went out into the lane with his heart wrapped exactly as it was safe for his heart to be wrapped, and it occurred to him — as it had begun to occur to him regularly now — that someday soon, some night, the tape would not be enough, and what would happen then was a question his father's shoulders were carrying on both sides of the door.

"Jaana," Ram said after him — barely voice.

Rudra went. And the door shut behind him in its accustomed neutral way, which was as close to a blessing as that doorway manufactured.

---

Ira and Veer occupied the Panchganga chabootra by eight. The ghat's nameplate stood in moonlight, black script on bleached stone, and the river below rode its evening way in long frothy pools, each lung of the bank breathing in and out behind its ruined towers. At the corner of the dusk the ghat assembled its usual orchestra — the tin whistles of birds who never accepted the city's clocks, the slow knocking of footsteps on old stone, the snapped-then-relit sparks of a belated aarti drifting down on the air like a rumour. And beneath it all, beneath every sense the naked ear could teach them, the old bones of the laid-earth lines gave their small insistent tock — at intervals of nine seconds, one two, as though deep below something were trying to come awake off their side's schedule and on no one's permission.

"Itna bhaari hai aaj," Ira said, mostly to her wrist-strip. "Kaafaa nahi raha. Bhaari."

"Kya fir se wo jagah hai?" Veer asked. Yehi hai na — Panchganga."

"Yehi tha. Yehi se hi." She put the strip back on her wrist, knotting it tighter than before. "Jagaah waali ghat. Sadaa se pehla."

Others used the stair: a bent old figure slid triple-bowed into a sitting niche; a woman with a hen in a basket went the way she had come, her eyes on Veer's arm a half-second too long for comfort but exactly the length people saw things. They kept the peace. Twice more the earth gave its underground punctuation, the second time soft enough that Veer could not tell argument from instruction. It landed in the knot under his chest and became, for the briefest second, something he would have called kind, if nothing else could have been.

Then came the sound of mud, laid down heavily on the steps above the water in a way that meant *somebody was very wet.* Veer heard it the way his mind remembered hearing an outrider approach: outside, at a distance, in the reeds across the water's longer pace.

Except that what came out of the reeds could not have been someone.

He could not move a foot. These night-things do not live on walking; they live on stillness, on the pauses of everyone who sees them. It came on along the bank-shoulder like a refugee from the entire condemned neighbourhood of daylight. It was rolling its head feebly against the wind like a drunk ignoring stars. There was a face.

The face was old in a way he could not have named — not old at the elbows, not old in the gums; old like brick in the sun, old like ash from a decree. It was not staring at them so much as it was letting them have the first look, the way one allows the people of the neighbourhood the first sight of a stranger one owns.

It leaned over and smiled, or what was left for it to smile smiled, for the dented. The whole left cheek was gone through the skin down to something clean, and through the opening Veer could see that the skull behind was not quite close to a shape.

"Baanch raha," it said then, in something close to Bhojpuri-ribbed old speech — a voice whose middle register was all worm-eaten and whose vowel boxes seemed to have been reknotted language by the unwise — "maine tumhare raaz suna hai. Hum baar-aankhon ko is sheher mein. Charon. Charon ki aankhen bhari hui hain. Kya tum sochte ho ki main itni door se aaya hoon tumse sirf *prem* se baat karne?"

And then it came forward, lazily, with the slow exhausted economy of a very old animal-god who no longer has the convention of human pride in attendance, its ruined legs moving in burst-cloth with the rhythmic **swish of blown ash for feet**; its face black-hinge-arguable under the convenient dark; its eyes grey as moth-ashes.

Veer heard himself say, entirely unwisely, "Jhuke re—"

"Veer nahi," said Ira in the steadiest voice she had ever owned. And then down the wind, from Assi's direction, a far-call and a far-answer in a pause, and Ira's hand shot flat onto the notebook's page as she counted two seconds of the thing's attention and matched them against the route of her own panic. "Wo campus jaa raha hai," she said. "Bhaago mat. Yaad rahe — bhaago mat — toh chalo—"

and the thing laughed at the top of the Panchganga stair for one ancient second and turned — and went. Sheer vacancy followed like a struck bow.

---

By eight twenty-three the college's western gates had shut their thin cricket-less night, and the thing that had come up along the river arrived under the library arches as naturally as a wall walks.

It had gone around the city to reach them.

Aadhya, who was standing in the quadrangle with her arms full of borrowed translation-pages, never knew who screamed first. She knew that she heard her own name screamed by a girl in the corridor, turned, and came face-on to the thing hovering over the same patch of grass Shastri-ji had tended every Tuesday and Saturday since before she'd been born. She gave herself no explanation for how quickly she was moving; she was moving quickly for no reason her body refused to accept. She ran between the columns without a word and put herself directly in its path.

It paid her the compliment of attention. Its neck came up, full-face, teeth, and its mouth moved.

"Aaiye," it said, in its rotted polite cadence. "Mera neta." It took one slow step, and the grass under its assenting foot turned white — a ring of white that bled outward and outward with no botanical logic, and kept growing, feet to yards, until even Aadhya standing at the quadrangle's centre felt the earth *compel* feet to frontage. "Chhattis saal se tere paas kuch hai — le aao."

The pale ring hit the edge of the quad. Her hands were already raised.

She did not think. There was no thought and no thought-spared. Her palms closed on nothing and the whole vegetable account of the quadrangle's kingdom whisked into her tightening fists with a speed no gardener on this planet had ever possessed: scattered green beads burnt and flew from her fingers like sparks beaten from cold iron, whirling, sticking, returning from invisible wires dense in spatial weight and dark in colour and only partially intelligent — and then they collided with the ground's green and did not ask anything.

The grass of the quadrangle flew forward at her command — not fast grass, not wind-tossed grass: a quick-flung sheet of thousand-year green, torn up from its laziness into purpose.

It hit the thing's half-human chest and did not push it. It *grabbed.* Newly sprouted — impossibly sprouted — shoots of pea-vine and ashoka and twenty feet of jasmine tendon sank split rootlets around the black-cowled tangle of the thing, whose startled many-mouths drew in a deep red oblique of reflex; and then the ropes flowered over it shoulder to chin: a woven green fabric the size of a gate, winding and tightening, hauling life out of lawn after lawn in three seconds of one sustained gasp —

Aadhya stood at its source, arms straightened stiff as a pyramid's flag, and neither knew nor wanted to know: her nails bled; her face was pale.

The thing swung. Ropes coiled, peeled, split ivy; it fed on one strand of jasmine and recoiled mid-scream (a scream of grass screaming back), and it came again, and she wrapped it again, twice as hard. Veer vaulted over a low hedge and hit the ground carrying her weight before she could fall; somewhere behind her Ira had both hands to her own mouth counting in tandem seconds.

Then out of nowhere — down the three-hundred-year-old stair toward the college's shattered gate — Rudra came.

He came through them. The tape was heavier than usual across his ribs that evening, riding with body's promised laughter; afterward he could not have told if a wave of fury had passed under it, or whether he had brought it himself in his hands. He ran barefoot across the lawn-footing, striding once mad under lamplight, climbed the vine's own ascending green ramp without bearing any particular course, cut the air to the thing's left flank at a hanging angle, and hit it with all of himself, from the same side and in the same direction:

*his whole body, knotted in land-and-ring, and a scream no word ever owned.*

The impact carried it through the veil of green she massed between her people, tore it out of the staff-and-rope wrap, and threw it across the quad to the foot of a lamp-post. It landed spun, and Rudra hit it again — in the head at the edge of its temples, twice as hard as it knew, and his hands broke nothing at all each time, trembled from the nerves that his skin refused to accept as bruised — and again, and again.

Between the second and third strikes the air around him went colourless. The tape on his chest peeled backwards under the mutiny of how much he was trying not to give; then split, not wildly this time but *flat,* wide, and brilliant — flowing into his wide-open palm — and the restless bright that neither sky nor earth could contain now spat outward, striking no thing and no shadow, covering everything at once that this huge erupting unprotected brightness could choose: the bottom of his fist ploughed the dirt and carried the entity splashing, gasping, six feet down into the quad's mud.

Not cast. Not pinned. Driven, by root and land together.

The black outline of it was gone.

Its eyes rallied dimly — a couple of new mouths for how many servants above it had fed at this trough over its long life, no one could tell — and in the last slow ratio of joint to breath it found the grass again: one complete looking-out of a non-lidded intelligence, paused. Its chest and throat arched.

And the entire dying shadow said low, sagaciously, in a Bhojpuri two generations older than Rudra, with an amen-smile leaking dark from its lips — each word carrying, exactly, the weight of every question the boy had been forbidden for nineteen years to ask:

*"You don't even know what you are, Asuran child."*

The breath shed. The shadow of the body unraveled, not with a battle-cry. Only the dead *use* of the words remained in the air, ground out to a grain, ground in under the brown quad.

Then the air came back.

---

Aadhya's vines were already withering where the grass had turned white around the thing's first footfall; the rest subsided tenderly, letting tendrils fall back into soil as if ashamed of having trusted. The quad rang empty.

Rudra stood over the sunken earth with his hands shaking in the hot air and turned slowly in place. Three faces looked back at him. Nobody had words; the twenty-second pause passed, as it passes when a thing is finished and nobody yet knows what it was called.

He could assemble only one sentence out of the scene:

*Asuran child.*

His ears rang with the ancient vowel-colour of it. Under the college's broken night, with half the quadrangle's grass folded back where the thing had passed, he saw how quickly the evening had collapsed from private to public theatre. In twelve heartbeats the black outline in the mud began a patient downward migration — sunken, dripped away, folded into the ground through some wormhole of its own knowing — and was gone before anyone could have stopped it or named it.

"Kya tha," Rudra said at last. "Kya tha woh *aakhri line* —"

"Theek mat socho," said Aadhya. She was sitting on her own splayed hands on the lowest stair, hair plastered to one cheek, refusing every hand Veer offered her. The skin-pads of her fingers were bleeding where the vine-threads had drawn their toll, and she was not crying; she was watching the place where the earth refused to give the thing a decent grave. "Kal sochenge. Aaj — saans."

"Ira kahan hai."

"Yahan hoon," said Ira, eyes still shut, on the second stair. She stood at last. Her face was not a witness's face. It was the face of a clerk who has watched which bricks of a bridge blew. "Rudra. *Tape.*"

Not a question. A count. He looked down: somewhere between the second and third strike the chest-tape had come undone — not torn by the enemy, unwound from inside, a string loose of its own fatigue — and the chest beneath had been bare to everything for three full seconds.

"Maine koshish ki thi —" he began.

"Tune tape ke bina *saara* kavach zameen pe gira diya," she said, steady and exact. "Aur neeche kuch sunta rehta hai hamesha. Meri wrist-strip — dekh."

She held it out. The strip was still warm. The dot of darkness she marked nightly had swelled by a ring she had never marked before: not an eighth-station dot. A radiance-ring, the record of something large answering from underneath, the way a well answers a dropped stone.

"Kal se," Ira said, to all of them, to the dark, to herself — "hum estimate nahi karte apni sides. Kal se hum apne aap ko *measure* karte hain. Rules same hain. Bhaago mat. Sach bolna. Apni jaan." She looked around slowly, once, at each of them. "Aur ek naya — *koi bhi dheere se nahi dekh sakta ki uski body kya bol rahi hai. Koi bhi akela nahi bolega apne mooh mein uski aakhri baat.*"

Veer made a rumbling assent without opening his folded arms.

She opened the notebook's back page and wrote, with unusual slowness, in a hand she would deny later:

*Rudra ne tape ke bina chaaron ko bachaya. Zameen ne dekh liya. Meri strip ne sun liya. Darwaza neeche bhi sun raha hai. Main darwaza nahi kholungi khud.*

She shut it before the earth could file a comment.

Veer did a slow circuit of the quad alone while the others steadied themselves. At the white ring's edge he crouched, touched the dead grass — no warmth, no cold, no pull in his wrist: it was *finished*. It was the first thing of the enemy's making he had seen in weeks that finished instead of collecting, and the difference between the two verbs shook him worse than the fight.

He came back and reported it in his own unconsulted vocabulary: "Jo kha liya — uska hisaab khatam kar diya. Wapas kuch nahi aayega udhar se. Main poora ghoom aaya. Sab diye hue the."

"Tabhi toh," Ira said quietly, "shuru mein kam, baad mein zyada." Nobody translated it. All four understood in the body: *the taps yield most when the accounts are freshest*.

Then Aadhya held up her hands in the lamplight. The knuckle-pads had stopped bleeding. Around her smallest finger, pressed beneath the skin like a welt seen through glass, ran a thread of colour the exact warm gold of a marigold petal. She turned the finger slowly. Nobody had words for it. Ira wrote it down anyway, with the time.


---

They separated late and in pairs, leaving behind an empty quadrangle where the brown earth was slowly furling itself over the hollow, and where a hundred pale new grass-threads were already replacing the ruined lawn in obedience to tomorrow's timetables, as though tonight had taught nobody anything except, perhaps, grass.

Aadhya sat on Veer's cycle-carrier and held on with both hands and said not a word for nine minutes. Ira rode ahead on her rusted machine, veering badly at crossings, stopping herself, counting under her breath. Between them they offered two jokes in exchange, broken-off, kindly; each was declined politely by the evening.

Rudra walked. He walked through the unlit alley and across the bridge the boat-race had contested with torches and neon sails, and did not run anywhere — there was no surface left in the city tonight on which to run that would not have witnessed whatever his ribcage was doing under the new skin.

He said it once, aloud, on the bridge, at the empty water:

"You don't even know what you are" — and then, in the tone a man uses to weigh mustard seeds — "kya hoon main…"

Nothing answered. He was not sure whether he had wanted it to.

Below him the river negotiated its channels. Somewhere far and damp, in the grid of the city's underground, the old seven marks burned faintly at their posts like seven coals that had forgotten what fire was, and the newest warmth — the pale ring his friend's hands had left in a college lawn — subsided last and slowest into the soil, admitting to itself it might outlive the pause.

Then he went home, alone, at walking pace, as if at a funeral nobody had announced.

Two turns from home, Ram Yadav's door opened on a lantern held by a hand more steady than any face at eleven at night should have to be greeted by.

He said, to nobody in particular, into the vestibule's air: "Rukmini. Paani nikalo."

The father stood a long way inside the doorway holding the lantern where it belonged, and watched his son come up the lane: shirt torn at the diagonal where the wrap had burst, wrists bruised dark along their flex-lines, eyes carrying too much that had already been spent successfully. The veteran's gaze made one full transit over the body — the way it always did — and asked nothing with it. *How* was not a currency that traded at that door.

"Aye," said Ram at last, to the lane and the night beyond it: "Dubara mat karna — mere bina."

He let his son pass.

"Main theek hoon," Rudra said, into the cold stair air.

"Andar," said Ram, finishing it.

Rukmini rose without sound. She came up with a tray — cold well-water, rock-salt on the rim of a steel glass — and looked at her son's chest the way field surgeons look: unloaded, unstartled, every bruise assigned its shelf in some account only mothers keep permanently updated. Rudra drank in two slow drafts, and the evening's unmatched labour finally went out of him in one long exhale. His mother touched his forehead once with the back of two fingers and let it go.

"Kal jamun lagaenge," she said, looking at the tray, not at his face. "Subah. Aap dono. Main rukne waali nahi hoon."

Behind them, Ram watched the lane until the moon leaned out from the neighbour's awning and corrected its station. He put both hands behind his back. Nineteen years of practiced peace gathered in his shoulders and reached, as from a great height, one sentence's worth of descent; the rope of it tugged once, twice; nothing arrived. The father gave no word except the one the doorway had been built around, the boy's whole life:

"Jaana."

Upstairs, the house's lamps went down one by one in their usual order — kitchen, gallery, storeroom — and the last lamp, the one in his father's hand, held out longest, as lamps do when the thing they are guarding is walking away from them up a flight of stairs.

---

Below the whole city the old line took its measure of the night. Seven faint coals along the ancient ring burned at their posts — and an eighth warmth, newest by exactly one evening, subsided slowly into a college lawn and joined their count without being invited, the way a new star files itself quietly in an old constellation. What had been a ring of seven was, for one night, a ring with a hollow at its heart no longer empty: refilled without permission, fed without ceremony, noticed nowhere.

*You don't even know what you are.*

Half a month ago he could have thought those words and stayed himself. He no longer could.
