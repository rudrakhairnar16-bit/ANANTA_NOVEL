# CHAPTER 2 — THE UNSEEN CURRENT

Aadhya noticed the tree before anyone else.

This was not unusual. She noticed things — the way a crack in a wall widened by a millimeter overnight, the way a stray dog favored its left leg on Tuesdays but not Wednesdays, the way the light in the BHU library's third-floor reading room shifted from gold to grey at exactly 4:17 PM in October but 3:58 PM in November. She noticed things the way other people breathed — involuntarily, constantly, and with a growing sense of exhaustion that she had learned, over nineteen years, to hide behind a smile and a carefully maintained GPA.

The banyan tree stood at the eastern edge of the BHU campus, near the old Sanskrit faculty building that nobody used anymore because the roof had partially collapsed during the 2019 monsoon and the administration had decided that repairing a building dedicated to a dead language was not, in their estimation, a priority. The tree was ancient — older than the university, older than the British cantonment that had preceded it, possibly older than the Mughal structures whose foundations still lurked beneath the campus lawns like buried teeth. Its aerial roots hung in thick curtains from branches that spread across a canopy wider than a cricket pitch, and in the three years Aadhya had been walking past it on her way to morning lectures, she had never once seen it look anything other than magnificently, aggressively alive.

Until this morning.

She stopped on the path, her backpack slipping off one shoulder, and stared.

The tree was dying.

Not gradually, not in the slow, seasonal way that trees in North India shed leaves in winter and regrew them in spring. This was something else — something violent and accelerated, as if the life was being sucked out of the wood in real time. The leaves on the eastern-facing branches had turned from deep green to a sickly, translucent yellow overnight. The bark on the trunk's southern face was peeling away in long, curling strips, revealing wood underneath that was grey and dry and cracked, the texture of bone left too long in the sun. And the aerial roots — those magnificent, cascading curtains of living wood — hung limp and desiccated, swaying in the morning breeze with the hollow, papery sound of dead things.

Aadhya's chest tightened. She didn't understand the reaction — it was a tree, after all, one of thousands on the campus, and trees died all the time from disease and drought and the particular brand of urban neglect that Varanasi practiced with such casual expertise. But the tightness in her chest was real, physical, a constriction around her sternum that felt less like sadness and more like recognition. As if some part of her body knew what was happening to this tree, even if her conscious mind did not.

She stepped off the path and walked toward the trunk.

The air changed as she approached. She felt it the way you feel the temperature drop when you open a refrigerator door — a subtle shift, a thinning, a wrongness that registered in the hairs on her arms before her brain could articulate why. The air around the tree was cold. Not cool, not pleasant, but cold in the way that a hospital corridor is cold, sterile and hostile and entirely indifferent to your comfort. And underneath the cold, there was something else — a vibration, a low-frequency hum that she felt in her teeth and her fingertips and the soles of her feet, a sound that was not quite a sound but a pressure, a weight, a presence.

She reached out and touched the bark.

The reaction was instantaneous.

A pulse of energy — green, warm, impossibly alive — surged from her palm into the dying wood. She felt it leave her body the way blood leaves a wound, a rush of something vital and irreplaceable flowing out of her and into the tree's desiccated roots. The bark under her hand warmed. The grey wood flushed with color. A single leaf on the branch above her head turned from yellow back to green, the transformation so rapid and so visible that Aadhya gasped and snatched her hand away.

The pulse stopped. The leaf stayed green. The rest of the tree remained dying.

Aadhya stared at her hand. Her palm was tingling, the skin flushed pink, and for a moment — just a moment — she could see something beneath the surface, a faint, luminous network of lines that pulsed in time with her heartbeat, like veins made of light. Then the vision faded, her hand returned to normal, and she was standing alone under a dying tree on a university campus at 7:42 AM with a lecture on postcolonial literature in eighteen minutes and absolutely no framework for understanding what had just happened.

She backed away from the tree. Her heart was hammering. Her hands were shaking.

*Not again,* she thought. *Please, not again.*

---

The first time it happened, she was seven.

She had found a sparrow on the balcony of her family's apartment in Lanka, Varanasi — a tiny, brown, unremarkable bird lying on its side with its beak open and its eyes half-closed, its chest barely moving. She had picked it up, cradling it in her cupped hands, and felt a surge of desperate, overwhelming sadness — the specific, world-ending grief that only a seven-year-old can summon for a dying creature she had known for approximately four seconds. She had whispered, "Please don't die," into the bird's feathers, and felt something warm flow out of her fingers and into the bird's tiny body.

The sparrow opened its eyes. It stood up. It chirped once, sharply, and flew away.

Aadhya had fainted. Her mother had found her on the balcony floor, pale and cold, and spent the next three hours at the emergency room running tests that revealed absolutely nothing wrong.

The second time, she was twelve. The monsoon had flooded the garden behind her school, and the marigold beds — her favorite, the ones she watered every morning before classes — were submerged under two feet of brown, stagnant water. She had waded in, ankle-deep, and pressed her hands into the mud, and felt that same warm surge, that same outpouring of something she couldn't name. By the next morning, the floodwaters had receded and the marigolds were blooming — impossibly, defiantly, in colors so vivid that the gardening staff accused someone of replacing the plants overnight.

The third time, she was sixteen. A dog — a stray, mangy, one-eared creature she had been feeding behind the chemistry building — was hit by a scooter on the main road. She found it lying in the gutter, its hind legs crushed, its breathing shallow and wet. She knelt in the filth and placed her hands on the dog's broken spine and felt the energy pour out of her in a torrent, so much that her vision went white and her nose started bleeding. The dog's legs healed. The dog stood up and licked her face and trotted away as if nothing had happened.

Aadhya spent two days in bed afterward, her body aching as if she had run a marathon, her blood count mysteriously low. Her parents took her to a specialist in Delhi. The specialist found nothing. The specialist suggested stress. The specialist prescribed vitamins.

After the third incident, Aadhya made a decision: she would not touch dying things anymore. She would not let the energy out. She would clamp it down, box it up, bury it under layers of academic achievement and social normalcy and the carefully constructed persona of a bright, slightly intense, fundamentally ordinary college student. She would not be the girl who healed things. She would not be the girl who fainted. She would not be the girl whose nose bled when she touched a broken dog.

She had almost succeeded. For three years, the energy had stayed dormant, a low hum in the background of her consciousness that she could ignore the way you ignore the sound of a refrigerator running.

And then the banyan tree started dying, and the hum became a roar, and the energy surged out of her hand before she could stop it, and now she was standing on a campus path with her heart in her throat and the taste of copper in her mouth and the growing, terrifying suspicion that whatever was killing the tree was the same thing that was waking up inside her.

She turned and walked toward the cafeteria. She needed chai. She needed noise. She needed to be surrounded by people who were worried about exams and internships and the WiFi password, not by the silent, screaming frequency of a dying tree and the green fire in her veins.

---

The BHU cafeteria at 8:15 AM was a war zone of clattering steel plates, shouting chai-wallahs, and the particular brand of chaotic energy that emerges when two thousand sleep-deprived students attempt to consume breakfast simultaneously in a space designed for eight hundred. Aadhya navigated the crowd with practiced efficiency, dodging elbows and backpacks, and secured a corner table near the window — her usual spot, the one with the view of the Ganges and the slightly less aggressive ants.

She ordered chai and a samosa and opened her notebook and tried to focus on the postcolonial literature essay she was supposed to be drafting. The words swam on the page. Her hand was still tingling.

"You look terrible."

She looked up. Rudra was standing on the other side of the table, holding a plate of poha and looking exactly as he always looked at this hour — disheveled, irritated, and radiating the specific brand of hostile energy that suggested he had already been awake for several hours and had not enjoyed a single minute of it. His knuckles were wrapped in what appeared to be a torn strip of his own kurta, and there was a bruise forming along his left jawline that he was making no effort to hide.

"Good morning to you too," Aadhya said.

Rudra dropped into the chair opposite her and attacked his poha with the enthusiasm of someone eating out of obligation rather than pleasure. He chewed in silence for approximately thirty seconds, then said, without looking up: "You were staring at that tree again."

Aadhya's pen froze. "What tree?"

"The big one. Near the Sanskrit building. The one that looks like it's been murdered." He shoveled another spoonful of poha into his mouth. "You've been staring at it every morning for a week. It's weird."

"It's dying," Aadhya said, the words coming out sharper than she intended. "Something is killing it. The energy around it is — wrong. Parasitic. Like something is feeding on it from underneath."

Rudra looked up. His expression was the one she had come to recognize as his default setting when confronted with anything that couldn't be punched, thrown, or solved through physical exertion: polite, distant, and profoundly uninterested.

"It's a tree, Aadhya."

"I know it's a tree."

"Trees die. It's Varanasi. Everything here is either dying or being cremated. That's the whole brand." He gestured vaguely toward the window, where the smoke from Manikarnika Ghat's funeral pyres was already rising in pale columns against the morning sky. "You're overthinking it."

Aadhya stared at him. There was something about Rudra that infuriated her — not his sarcasm, which she could handle, and not his dismissiveness, which she had learned to interpret as a defense mechanism rather than genuine indifference, but the gap between what he said and what his body revealed. He was telling her she was overthinking a dying tree, but his eyes — those dark, restless eyes that always seemed to be tracking something the rest of them couldn't see — were fixed on the window, on the exact spot where the banyan tree stood, and his wrapped knuckles were clenched around his spoon with a tension that had nothing to do with poha.

He knew something. She was sure of it.

"Fine," she said, closing her notebook. "It's just a tree. I'm overthinking. You're right."

Rudra's eyes flicked back to her face. For a moment, the mask slipped — the same way it always did, briefly and involuntarily, revealing a flash of something that might have been guilt, or might have been fear, or might have been the exhaustion of a person carrying a weight he couldn't share.

"I didn't say you were wrong," he said quietly. "I said you were overthinking."

He stood, picked up his plate, and walked away before she could respond, merging into the cafeteria crowd with the fluid, unconscious grace of someone whose body had been trained to move through hostile spaces since before he could read.

Aadhya watched him go. She noticed the bruise on his jaw. She noticed the blood seeping through the makeshift bandage on his knuckles. She noticed the way he favored his left shoulder, the subtle wince when he raised his arm to push through the cafeteria door.

*What are you hiding, Rudra?* she thought.

The chai-wallah dropped a fresh glass of chai on her table with a clatter. She wrapped her hands around the hot steel and let the warmth seep into her tingling palms, and tried very hard not to think about green light and dying trees and the feeling of something ancient and enormous stirring beneath the soil of a city that had been built on top of the bones of older, stranger things.

She failed.
