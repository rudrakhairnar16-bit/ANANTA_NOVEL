# CHAPTER 6 — ROOTS

Aadhya did not sleep on the night after the burning ghat.

This was not unusual only in degree. She had slept badly for nine days — since the morning the banyan had announced its dying, since the cold had learned her name, since some sub-basement of her nineteen years had begun ticking like a wire. But tonight the insomnia had *content*. Tonight the dark behind her lids kept reopening on the same sequence: the flames lying down like grain under pressure; the grey filaments brightening, flowerless; the son's face as his father sat up out of the fire wearing something else's agenda; the crowd's avalanche; and worse than all of it, worm-quiet under the screams, that deep subterranean rustle — *like a garden at midnight heard through a wall* — the sound of roots moving in soil they had no business touching.

She had got off her mattress twice already. Once for water, once for the window. Her wrist remembered cold.

On the bed's far side, illuminated by the street-sodium suffering quietly through the curtain, her physics notebook lay with her half-finished elasticity derivation, and she looked at the neat equations — stress, strain, the young modulus — and they looked like a language from a country that had declared war on hers and lost. *Strain under load. Where was the elastic limit of a day? Of a tree? Of a nineteen-year-old girl who had watched the dead rehearse life?*

At 11:40 PM she accepted, formally, what her feet had decided at ten.

She dressed — jeans, kurti, the dupatta ravelled up to a scarf; thick socks; trembling hands making a report of her shoelaces — and took the family's old grey scooter from the shadow between parked autos where it lived like aloof furniture, key already cut smooth as a fossil from a decade of her mother's kurtas' pockets. Her mother was not in bed. Her mother was on the balcony in the wicker chair — the chair whose creak Aadhya had known since the apartment in Lanka, since the balcony of the sparrow, since all her life's past lives — asleep with wool over her knees and three rows of knitting frozen at the needle. Mouth slightly open. Even in sleep, even there, some watchman-part of the woman's face was *on duty*.

Aadhya eased the door to, the way one takes leave of a shrine.

In the stairwell she felt her own heartbeat in her teeth, and tasted — when she swallowed — the first copper, anticipated, like chewed foil.

The city at midnight was a text she'd read every year of her life and never once at this hour.

Lanka's fruit carts slept under their tarpaulins, stacked like the animals of a wooden ark. The paan kiosk's shutter was a cliff-face of steel, one magazine page of a film star's smile still stapled to it, staring. Somewhere a pressure-hose cleaned tomorrow; somewhere two dogs disputed an inheritance of fish-heads; the old roadside neems pulled the last of their thin leaves tighter into their branches as she passed, as if her headlight were wind. And the ghats — down the long incline of Saket Nagar, four careful lefts beyond the post office — the ghats were *changed*: no floodlights any longer, no ropes, but light-columns of the tv crews were all extinguished and the pyre columns had resumed, grey and unhurried and blameless, the city's appetite washing its face after the incident. She could not see Manikarnika from the overpass and was glad.

She parked outside BHU's lane-barrier on the Sanskrit wing side, which had been tied shut with rubber and apology since her first year, where the wall behind the derelict faculty had lost a panel of fencing to a bohar's root and no engineering section in eleven years had felt the need to remember it.

A watchman slept inside the glass gatehouse, earphones in, head arranged as if prayerfully upon the counter; she had rehearsed the lie all the way here — *my register, sir, exam tomorrow, please sir, two minutes* — but it turned out the campus at half-past midnight stood watch for saboteurs and never once for a girl with a nosebleed starting, slipping through a fence-gap like a thought through a comb. The rubber-bound barrier, the dew, the unmown council lawn stippled with the night's cold drip, the whole elephant-dark of the botanical dead-end behind the Sanskrit building: all of it held still as she walked into it, with the terrible tranquillity of a lung between breaths.

There was no moon. She was glad. She did not fully understand why she was glad until afterward: no moon meant no witnesses that reflected.

The banyan was waiting.

---

Nine days ago the tree had been dying.

Now the tree was *gone* — and what stood under the stars in its place was a thing that looked, from twenty paces, like a banyan drawn by someone who had heard of banyans once, from a witness, briefly, in the dark. The canopy's geometry still spoke its name in silhouette — the flying root-curtains, the trunks-within-trunks, the great wing-spread of the secondary canopy — but all of it was uniformly, exhaustively grey, grey as soap, grey as the slag-ash from the iron-smelters beyond Kamaccha, and even at this distance she could hear it. Not with ears.

*It* — the sound had no right word — was a dryness, a rustle with no motion at its source, like the song of a record needle in an empty groove. Silence had stopped being an absence, she thought, and become a texture under this tree.

She should go home. Her body explained this thoroughly — heartbeat in her teeth, cold-wrist memory climbing the elbows, little hairs standing on her forearms like field-grass pointing all one way under a thunderstorm —

— except there was no thunderstorm —

She circled the trunk once, careful, as she imagined deer must circle water-holes in tiger country. And found the leaf.

*Her leaf* — she knew which it was, would have known it blind. The one her hand had put green back into on the first morning, nine days ago. She had counted it that morning in secret, had irrationally disbelieved her own evidence, a dew-fresh apple-green handkerchief in a congregation of shrivel —

It hung where it had hung.

But in nine days it had gone the colour of pond-water at dusk — not dead, *deeper*, streaked now with grey-black filaments branching from the stem end — anchor-end, she thought wildly — outward. The black was in its veins. It was the same veining that had run rivers under a dead man's skin on a pyre platform. It had been there nine hours or nine minutes. And the leaf, being drunk on her gift, had opened a door for the drinking; the little green she had poured in through the bark was now a cup handle on the tree's ruined door — she understood with animal certainty and without recourse to language: *the thing below had found the tree by the same warmth she'd fed it, and she'd been feeding the tree and the tap below it as one dish.*

This is what I could not explain in the cafeteria, she thought, very tremblingly. With Rudra watching me. With his graveyard bruises. *The tree is not dying, it is being eaten, and it has been eaten for longer than I have known it, and tonight it no longer has any strength to fight, and I am the only person in the world who knows because—*

Because I can hear the difference between a drying-up and a drinking-down.

Because whatever is wrong with the tree — is *what is wrong with me.*

Her eyes were wet. The bare branches scratched a mild anarchy on the high clouds.

She put out her hand — the left one, the hand not yet on bark — and laid it flat against one of the aerial-root curtains, the thick corded strand that had hung in that exact gradient her whole university life.

And got — not memory, nothing so helpful as memory — but *weight*. The strand's dead fibre was a rope hung with uncountable small bells of experience, all rung silent decades ago: the forty years of children who had swung on this very cord and been sworn at from lecture windows; the night in 2019 when the Sanskrit roof had come down and the tree had taken half the falling masonry into these strands like combers into a net, and nobody had ever said thank you; the hidden daily congress of barbers, brief-men, napping mid-day electricians, amavasya-worshippers with their pinches of vermilion, the jamun-sellers' knives nicked into its ankle every July. The banyan had been the Sanskrit wing's second building; it had never charged fees; it was sixty years deep in the gratitude of the uneducated and the devotional habits of the department sweepers, and all of that was still here, banked under bark gone grey as soap —

And all of it was flowing out, grain by grain, down through the root-tips, into a vein she could now feel the shape of the way one feels a pulse through a quilt.

*It's going somewhere,* she thought. *None of this is dying. It's being carried.*

Her knees made the next decision; they folded her down onto the leaf-mould at the trunk's root-flare, where the ground was coldest and therefore — her whole body now knew the arithmetic — nearest the bore of the vein's mouth.

Down below, in the dark inside the dark under them, she *heard* the lines.

---

Once, when she was seven — the balcony, the brown bundle of warm feather weighting its beak open in her palms; the school-flood when she was twelve — the marigolds rucked and drowned and pressed back out of the mud by something that did not know it was a girl; the dog at sixteen — both times she had done it kneeling, with her *hands in the earth*.

So she knelt. Thank you, she thought, to nobody. Palm flat on the mustarded humus of the trunk's root-flare, sixty years of fallen leaf under her fingers, earth-cold and pleasantly ordinary for one dear second —

The second ended.

It did not come as images — it never had before, and she understood only then how little the other times had shown her. It came as *placement*, as if the night had been asked to make room for her. Her head lifted, her whole body listening, and behind her eyes the city unfolded a second city: *the ring*.

She saw — felt — knew — the pilgrimage circuit that Ira had sketched in red ink across her graph paper in the cafeteria. It was below her, fifty, sixty feet beneath the mud of the lawns, drawn around the old city like a ribcage around a heart. Fifteen-odd kilometres of it, the five kos of the old walking-route, and each kos a slow bright artery of *warmth* — she had no word that fit what flowed there; 'energy' was too small and 'power' too vulgar, so her mind did what it had always done and found it in inventory: the camphor-ash of a million evening lamps ground into river-silt; names whispered into water at first light, every day, for longer than the university's ledgers could count; flowers laid at thresholds by hands now dust; fasts kept by mothers whose granddaughters' granddaughters still kept them; the rope-smoke of ten thousand boatmen's mornings; ghee, jasmine oil, eleven o'clock bhajans leaking from somebody's window radio — all of it cached, gathered, sunk into the soil and old stone where so many generations had said thank-you that the saying-of-it had become a thing, a *substance*, circulating slowly like light through amber.

The city's savings. The account that faith kept.

*The reservoir.*

*The reservoir.*

And at one point on the south-eastern curve — where the map's circle had darkened in Ira's redrawing — the arteries bent out of the earth's argument and hooked *down*.

The black came up the line to meet her thoughts like the answer to a whistle.

It was not a line. It was the absence made technical — a bore, a throat, a wound with walls, running from down at the ghat-wards bedrock *through* the ring's rich artery, braided into it, the way the tapeworm braids into an animal's guts: tissue-native to its host. It was sucking on the ring. It had cut wells into the reservoir and stood in them, lapping: slow, scheduled, cumulative — a decade's trickle, nine days' gulp. It was on a ninth night now of geometrically widening the tap, and she could feel — as she knelt barefoot and gasping on sixty years of leaf-mould with her hand stirring faintly green —

She could feel that it was nearly *full*.

Far down the bore — so far her senses refused to give it shape, as a mouth refused certain words — was a *capacity*, a vessel whose outline she could just touch with terror's antennae: some great cup or tank or stomach, being carefully, patiently poured toward brimming.

*The cup is being filled,* said Ira's voice in her head from the rooftop, thirty-six hours ago. *The question is — at what point do you stop filling a cup?*

Aadhya sobbed, once, aloud.

The banyan's branches clicked above her. The dead leaf swung.

"Bas," she said, to herself, to the bore in the earth, the way a woman talks herself over the ledge. "Bas. Bas. Enough."

She was not, it is essential to understand, a soft girl. She was the daughter of a clerk who had never missed a day's honesty; she had fought the state school for a lab-key, fought the college for her scholarship, three boys in her second year for the microscope, fate for a fever — but she had never in her life fought anything that filled up like this: level rising nightly, shoreless, without a throat to break. There was no argument with it, no answer required. There was just ordinary here: a girl kneeling on the earth with the ground *eating* under her knees.

Her eyes made the decision her mind was otherwise engaged to make. They filled. Her hand shut harder on the cold bark.

"Stop it," she said. Uselessly. Out loud. Her voice wet.

And — like a key trying the lock the soul keeps on every body's furnace-door — from the centre of her, that old heat, the one that had been a flicker on the balcony, a candle in the flood, a torch for a broken spine —

— came up like someone had stepped on its accelerator.

---

The green came out of her palms the way sunrise comes out of a horizon: not projection but arrival.

In the material dark, it was the brightest thing in Lanka road-side — both her hands from wrist to fingertip now fully incandescent, bottled lime lightning, veins going up under the skin of her forearms like floodlit channels in an aerial photograph, and under them, through her skin, the tide climbing her arms, shoulder-wards, into the sealed ring above the sternum where her heart must be. Somewhere far above the ground her mouth made a sound that was not a word.

Light flooded the bole of the tree. She could see the tree-grain through the bark as if the dead banyan had become translucent — veins gone to black lace, grey flesh, hollows; she saw, and the seeing hurt, the pattern the emptiness had been knit into, a crochet of hunger decades in the making, spliced up the trunk, fisted in its root-tips — and likewise the bole of the dying tree *opened* and drank from her, accepted, steeping, like a grandmother's face into warm hands.

The trunk warmed. The grey admitted colour reluctantly, at the bark-level, as coals admit breath.

At her hands' outline, a ring of radiance ran outward through the ground — moss igniting green, the humus steaming gently into the cold — and the ring, wide as two tall men lying head-to-toe, blinked the lawn alive in its passing: blades of winter-browned grass stood up shuddering, went soft and complete as if the frost had been un-rung from their cells. One aerial root — the dry curtain-strand her knee touched — lay under her light like a rope being mended strand by strand, grey pith slipping to live fibre, cork to heartwood, out from her.

Above them both the canopy came on softly all over, ten thousand dead leaves remembering concurrently the texture of the undersides of their youth, and for the duration of her breathing — four counts? forever? — they lay *green-fleshed against the starless cold*, trembling, and every crow asleep in the municipal limbs beyond the fence woke up screaming.

She looked up at her own hands and the aureoles of them had simplified to radiance.

The taste in the mouth then — that was the cost waking up and presenting itself before the miracle had even settled its bill: copper, no longer anticipated, *delivered*; something rattled loose behind her nose; a drop of noseblood fell onto her wrist and sang sizzling in the green light, and the blood sang brighter, like matter agreeing to become energy, and—

The bore down in the earth below the ghat *flinched*.

She felt it as plainly as a horse-suddenly-halted is felt between the knees: the careful, patient, decade-long drinking down below — *threw a hitch*. The bore had registered an unprecedented *source* standing over the ground of its flow. A mindless something — ancient, full, near-brimming — oriented itself toward her, blindly, the way a bull orientates toward cloth. The sucking at the arteries hesitated. The walls of the bore flexed with attention. And where the siphon's current paused along the arteries, it *rushed* harder up the one tap it had already cut — her tree's tap —

— *took her taste* —

Aadhya tore her palm off the bark like tearing it off a hot iron.

She was already standing, or she never was. The dark came slamming back; the leaf-ring trembled hopeless around her like a lamp's halo after the lamp has gone; and she reeled through the canopy-curtain, blood tipping twin ribbons out of her nose and mouth, heartbeat crashing at the cage of her teeth, vision already swerving-side-black-and-concave.

Out of the canopy's dying light, off the Sanskrit building's lawn, through the panel-gap in the bohar-root fence she ran — clipping someone's generator pipe with her shoulder, sprawling once on the dew — stung palms, grass-wet knees, up again, *run, think later* —

— the Campus sentry's glass house empty now, empty of him —

She started the scooter with one foot like a curse and went out of Sanskrit-building lane at a velocity the tyres had not been purchased for.

The shuttered little streets of Lanka slid by, hazy yellow, deaf. One old dog that knew her from her morning feeding rounds — one-eared, ash-freckled, held together by loyalty and mange — rose from the municipal debris-point at the corner of the last lane, saw her face, and whined, and came lurching alongside her at its ancient amble; she slowed the scooter to a roll and put her hand out, half a second before remembering, and halted it a hand-width above its scarred shoulders —

"Aaj nahi," she said, and her voice came apart. "Aaj — nahi. Ja. Apne raste—"

The dog — nineteen generations of Varanasi gutter-keepers bodied into one battered pensioner — sat back on its haunches and let her go.

She drove on alone between its eyes in mirrors and the sodium lights stretched her shadow longer than the street at every lamp, until the scooter's shadow resembled less a vehicle than a straight *line*, one street-width thin, dragged south over ground that, under fifty feet of compressed rock, had never forgotten the feel of a ring.

And the city was dimmer.

She did not trust the observation and could not unhave it. Every small night-shrine at the crossings — the tile-faced Hanuman with his steady brass lamp outside the ration shop; the Shiva niche with its single oil tongue on the wall by the Lanka turn — every bulb and flame she had ever known by heart went on doing everything correctly and yet came up short, seven watts short, one tessera of gold short, like photocopies of themselves. The ghee-dark window-shine of the old houses above the shuttered shops held on stubbornly and still gave the impression of embers being hastily read. Nothing was wrong anywhere. Everything was *less*.

The reservoir felt skimmed — even through the panic, with blood detonating quietly inside one nostril, the part of her that could not stop measuring things saw it and logged it: tonight's skimming was deeper than last night's skimming, and deeper by *more* than last night's had been deeper than the one before.

Whatever's cup was filling had widened the tap by two.

The bridge-sweeper was gone. The blind beggar at the temple steps was gone; but his dog — not *her* dog; a different dog altogether, spotted — was asleep in his rags with its head over the tin bowl as if holding the city together in his place, and it had not occurred to the dog to be watchful, and something in the sight — a small faithful thing sleeping on the lid of an empty collection-tin, blessed and empty — anyway, that was when Aadhya first began to cry in earnest, shoulder-shaking at sixty kilometres an hour through the last empty lane before home, no longer able to tell the sobs apart from the wind.

---

At home, the balcony chair was empty, the wool folded. The stairwell held her like an ear. Her mother had left the living room's small bulb on, and it lay over the cane furniture like an unasked question.

In the hall she passed her parents' bedroom and its open door, and there was her mother — still in the chair-side shawl, awake after all, sitting on the bed's edge in the near-dark with her hands folded, facing the door as if the door had been the thing she was waiting up for. Their eyes met for the length of a held breath.

"Zoo se phone aaya tha," her mother said at last — mild, unreadable, the voice of a woman who had decided years ago that the truth, when it wanted to arrive, would take its own route. "Priya ki mummy ka. Pooch rahi thi tum theek ho. Maine keh diya haan."

A pause, precisely long enough to hold everything neither of them said.

"Kal college hai na," her mother said. It was not a question and not an instruction. It was a rope thrown across nineteen years of gap between them, thin as cotton and load-bearing as love. "So jao. Bahut der ho gayi."

"Ammu—" Aadhya began.

"So jao," said her mother, and lay down with her back to the door, which in their house had always meant: *I have done what I can with tonight.*

She locked the bathroom and stood in the hum of the ceiling fan she had known since her first board exams, running the tap, working the copper discolouration off her skin with both hands until the water streamed clear.

Her face in the mirror: blood-diluted, whiter than rice-water; her breathing staggered, stretched.

Beneath the skin of her raised arms, the veins gleamed the faintest drowned-and-silver leaf-green and as she watched with horror — far away, quietly, hands spread flat on the basin's cold rim — the lamp-light in them guttered once, embered, and went out.

"Kya ho raha hai mujhe," Aadhya addressed the bathroom in the whisper of the filament still cooling below her sternum.

The bathroom, being Varanasi's, knew too much to answer.

On the knees of her jeans, pressed indelibly into the porous fabric, were threads of drying humus and, catching the lamp, two grains of living moss — still faintly green, small enough to hide, bright enough to accuse her.

She cupped water, put out the remaining shine as a mother smothers coal, and wept with her head against the mirror until her forehead pressed cold enough that the glass began faintly to fog.

---

Far out under the drainage-wreckage west of the old library and the bohar-roots and the fence, beneath eleven years of absent engineering and fifty feet of brown earth and black rock, at the end of the bore-narrowed tap-line the parasite-field's three eastern-node filaments lay closest to the skin of the trunk-base where she'd knelt.

For an interval of minutes after she had left, they did not pull; did not drink.

They stayed curled mid-syphon, filaments vibrating faintly, like spiders tasting the silent air left by departed prey.

Then, wrapping themselves slowly around the soil-print of one leaf-heat left before it, they *hauled*: the whole night-dark woven mass leaned one degree toward the banyan's lawn and the campus above it; and the ring of glow-bleached soil around the trunk where her hands had stood began — not visibly — to be read grain by grain.

Stone, root, leafmould; the bore had picked up her flavour like a cup by its handle.

It returned slow seconds after to its arteries. But it had now *two* scents within the city limits: a furnace under a seal, and a garden under a girl.

And down the full dark length of the bore — all the way down past the bedrock, past the seam of old iron-stained strata, past nine days' deepening harvest of the city's slow-warmed prayers — a vessel that could not properly grow edges stirred its walls by one millimetre, listening, and upon the tenth night geometrically began again, two taps wider than before.
