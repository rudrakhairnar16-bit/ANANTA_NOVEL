# CHAPTER 16 — THE ROOFTOP

Veer had inherited, by birth and by luck and by the mysterious diplomacy of his mother, the use of the highest roof on his lane — the terrace above the old neighbours' wing, where the water tank squatted like a bald owlet, where a forty-year-old TV antenna stood bent double against the sky, and where, at sunset, the whole city seemed to postpone itself, the way rooms pause when a door is opening somewhere else.

His mother had spent the day constructing pakoras with the entrenched resolve of a woman funding an expedition. "Chaar baje aayenge?" she narrated without looking up — "Main bahar nahi ja rahi. Tum log upar baithte jao, aur sambhal ke." She'd sent up a steel tray — the pakora pyramid, four katoris of green chutney squeezed from nothing, a jug of nimbu-shikanji, and on the tray's exact compass-centre, one saucer of salt that she rotated, arbitrarily suspiciously superstitiously, to true north. "Nazar laga mat dikhana un charon ko," she warned him, slapping away his sneaking hand. "Bohot achhe lagte hain wo log."

"Ammi—"

"Pakode garam rehne chahiye."

They came up at half past five, with two packets of roasted corn bought on the corner-steps as a teenagers' tithe, and Aadhya hugging her roll-bag against the roof's squabbling evening wind, and Ira locking the trapdoor behind them — a habit she had imported, directly and undisguisedly, from the month she had spent believing the Durgakund door was not fully able to keep anything in or out. Nobody minded. Nobody locked the other one with any different care.

The view, when they dismounted the final awkward terrace-door step and dispersed along the coping into their now-definite places — Veer at the tank, Aadhya at the north parapet, Ira at the corner where the sun went down — was the usual private miracle of a city in which you could stand still. To the north, temple-tops and rose-dust air and one triple-cornered old kiosk sheening; northeast, in rinsed lines, the river sliding stored and lazy gold westwards, carrying drained prayer-smoke on its back; and along the vast flat galaxy of roofs around them, other families coming out to beat their rugs or to fly a last evening kite where it caught the last usable wind, children mouse-brown with play. Everywhere the sounds: women calling in children, the city's night-detail preparing for route.

Aadhya told them about the library.

She told it flat, the way you recount a stranger's misfortune — the excisions, the two earlier names at intervals of a generation, the third name with its ballpoint three weeks old, the notes — and she handed around the two paper slips like a teacher distributing bad results. They passed the pencil-fragments from hand to hand. *Stop looking*, read each of them in turn, with scrupulous fairness; and the second one: *Main dekh rahi hoon.*

"Itni si cheez mein itna data hai," said Ira, pinching the second note's edge up to the last of the light. "Pehla note — chhote pen se, dabey hue, purana mood, *warning*. Doosra note — naya pressure, seedha, saaf — *protest*. 'Main dekh rahi hoon' — matlab jo likh rahi hai wo humein rok nahi rahi, wo khud dekh rahi hai humari jagah." Her eyes stayed on the paper as if daring it to confess further. "Lekin pages kaatne waala koi aur hai. Blade tez hai, professional haath." She held the slip steady against the dying light. "Katne waala alag. Rokne waali alag. Rokne waali likhti hai 'main dekh rahi hoon' — matlab wo ab bhi *wahan hai*. Roz ya kabhi-kabhi. Par dekhti zaroor hai."

"Jo dekh rahi hai, wo humari taraf hai?" Veer asked.

"Pata nahi," Ira said. "Par humein bola nahi gaya police ko. Roka gaya sirf. Kabhi-kabhi rokna bhi madad hoti hai."

Rudra had been quiet through all of it — knuckles wrapped, palms cupped together — and now, without moving, he said:

"Teesra naam. Register wala. Gyarah dafa likhne waala. Uska naam kya tha?"

His voice did not rise, and nobody answered him straight, because the answer was sitting already in each of their ribs. Ira set the slip down on the parapet with the cautious flat care of a pathologist laying out one exhibit.

"Unnees saal pehle," she said. "Pehli entry. Blue ink. Officer jaisa haath. Naam —" she shook her head once — "naam ka pehla akshar *R* hai. Bas itna bacha hai. Aage ka portion paani ne khaa liya." She let the words sit. "Do saal woh aata raha. Phir likhna band kar diya."

Nobody had a verdict on that. Down the parapet Aadhya watched Rudra's face perform, without a single word, the whole thing it had so many times promised her in courtyard code that it would never perform: the silent item-by-item topple of the masonry called *nineteen years*; the eyes shuttering from inside; the breath — in, four — the breath — hold, four — and the re-settling. Nobody said anything for ten whole breaths.

The sun detached itself from the last ledge west of them and dimmed to gratefuller color.

In that minute of dimming, Ira did what Ira did. The wrist-strip came out of her sleeve's pinned margin without looking; the compass surfaced from her pocket on the blind confidence of habit; she took one reading of each — once at the sky, once toward the street below them, once *down* — and stowed them away, and said nothing for a minute.

"Kitna?" Veer asked finally, with the blunt courage of a child asking the dentist where it will hurt.

"Chhe din," Ira said. "Mela ke waqt maine kaha tha aath. Ab usi rate se bhi tez — *chhe*." She opened the notebook to the gridded page and tilted it so all of them could see: the seven circled points around the cup-shape, the days ruled down the margin like a school timetable, each date given its figure. "Aarti waali raat: gyarah. Mela ke din: aath. Aaj: chhe. Rate tez ho raha hai. Chhote taps barah baje se ab dupahar ko bhi chalte hain. Jo pehle sirf raaton mein pee rhe the — ab wo din mein bhi mod sakte hain."

The four of them looked at the page the way people at a play look at the rain beginning over the stage.

"Toh cheezein delay nahi karti," Aadhya said.

"Delay nahi karti," said Ira. "Khushi ke din sabse bada collection hota hai. Ye formula yaad rakh lo. Mela ka din sabse tez gaya. Race ka din doosre number pe. Aarti teesre." She closed the notebook with its small leather slap. "Isliye aaj ka din count karta hai. Humne aaj ek dusre se apna darr baanta hai — ye bhi ek diya hai isi din ka — dekh lena, yeh tapakega kal raat. Kal se kaam."

Everyone heard the tank give its hushed steel gurgle, like something clearing its throat to agree.

Then Veer clapped his knees and stood, huge, relieved to be able to give anything to anyone. "Toh ab — koi meeting-log madad ki? Chai? Kuch bhi? Kyunki agar main aur ek second yahan beykaar baitha raha toh main tank ke upar chadh jaaunga —"

"Baith jaa," said Aadhya, not ungently, "dramebaaz."

— and after a while it was decided, formally, the way the four of them decided everything: by silence going unpunished just a little too long. Veer dragged the two cane chairs and the khaat-stool from the corner; Aadhya put the library-bag against the antenna's knee and sat on the parapet-coping with her legs dangling over two storeys of air as if air were handrails; Ira took the sundown corner with her back to the light like a lookout; Rudra leaned against the cool curve of the tank and let the steel hold some of his evening for him.

A normal sunset kept them company. Off to the northeast the city articulated its evening devotions in layers — bells from the Sankat Mochan side, a scooter-wallah's argument with his own brakes, the smells of six dinners beginning. Something neighbours would later describe as "bahut shaanti lag rahi thi bheed mein" — and under it all, the by-now familiar second transit of the dusk: the far dominion of harvested light doing its daily accounts.

It was Ira who began the real business. She did it with a quality of academic formaldehyde, as if conducting an examination no one was required to pass:

"Aaj se hum chaaron ke rules mein ek add hota hai," she told the corn packet. "Ek-ek baat. *Darr.* Sabse bada waala. Chaaron bolenge. Main shuru karti hoon."

"Ye kya group therapy game hai," Rudra said, alarmed.

"Ruk ja. Bolne de," said Aadhya. She was looking out at the thinning evening; the mela's memory still stood folded over the ghats — last night's diya-lines gone to trampled mud at their stations. "Bolo. Sun rahi hoon."

So Ira — archivist of tremors, self-appointed keeper of everything measurable — spoke facing the broken antenna, into its five bent arms, as into a conference table:

"Mujhe apne sapno ke *sach* hone se darr lagta hai." She steadied the words with the care of someone pouring into a narrow mouth. "Literal. Raaton waale. Bachpan se ek hi hai — ek andhera kamra, ek kursi, ek aadmi baitha hua ghutne pe talwaar liye, aur kisi ki aankhein jo dekh rahi hoti hain ki main dekh rahi hoon. Main hamesha sochti thi — ye dimaag ka pressure hai, orphanage ka residue, ghar ka intezaar. Par hafte bhar pehle mera dream aur mera compass dono ek hi cheez dikhane lage." A breath. "Sapne ka dar isliye nahi ki ve sach honge. Isliye ki ve *plan* hain. Kisi ka. Aur plan complete hote hi mera main-hona khatam ho jayega — main sirf uska naam rah jaoungi." She nodded once, recorder gone flat. "Likh lena. Yehi hai mera sabse bada. Agla bole."

Silence — the good kind — held the roof.

Then Veer rolled his corn-packet's sleeve in both fists and, like a man volunteering for roll-call, said:

"Mera sabse simple hai. *Useless reh jaana.*" A flat half-laugh, unloading. "Sab kuch ho raha hai — tum teeno ke andar kuch jagah banti jaa rahi hai — aur main... main wahan khada hoon photo mein. Bada aadmi. Lambe haath. Pillar pe haath rakh diya us raat — luck thi. Lekin agli baar? Agli baar agar wo luck na aayi — agar meri muthi phir *wahi* nahi hui jab zaroorat padi — toh main kya hoon? Khaana laane waala. Auto pakadne waala. Maa ke ghar ka lamp." He stopped; when he began again, his voice had gone somewhere knees-first into work. "Log kehte hain darr maut ka hota hai. Mujhe maut se darr nahi hai. Mujhe *faltu* se darr hai. Ki ek din tum log kisi aisi jagah khade hoge jahan bandook nahi flag kaam aayega — aur main wahan bas... *haath jod ke khada ho jaunga.* Vo din mere liye maut hai. Bas itna hi."

He finished red and looked immediately sorry for having taken a whole minute of the sunset. Nobody permitted him to regret it.

Under his rolled sleeve, along the old burn, the tan-gold lines flexed once as he unclenched his fist, and slept.

Aadhya took a while. Down in the last lane a cow looked over a wall at nothing; nobody waited on anyone.

"Mera darr aur bhi gadbad hai," she said. "Mujhe nahi lagta ki cheezein bigad jayengi. Mujhe lagta hai ki *main* bigad dungi — apne haathon se. Us raat ped ke paas jo mere haath jale — logon ne kaha kitna khoobsurat, kitna hara — par main sirf ye sochti hoon: jo cheez ped ke andar ghus sakti hai, wo insaan ke andar bhi ghus sakti hai. Ek din haath mila rahi thi apni classmate ko — usne touch kiya aur mujhse zyada usko achha laga — wo chill gayi itni achhi feeling mein, aur main raat bhar jaagi ki maine use *inject* toh nahi kar diya kuch?" She held her two hands out into the sunset, palms up, empty as caught birds being released. "Isliye ab main kisi se haath nahi milati. Isliye main pooja mein piche khadi hoti hoon. Mera sabse bada darr ye hai ki kabhi — galti se, mohabbat se, maa ko gale lagate waqt — meri ye cheez kisi apne ko khaa jaye aur mujhe pata bhi na chale ki kis waqt gate khula."

Her voice stayed level to the end of it, and the end of it undid her anyway: she wept — soundlessly, untheatrically, the weeping of a person whose books keep to their own accounting — and Ira's hand found her shoulder blade before either of the boys saw reason to move, flat-palmed, applying the kind of arithmetic a body can answer.

For a long time none of them attempted any language at all.

Then Rudra stood forward off the tank, and faced the whole small sunset, and delivered his, flat, clinically:

"Mujhe apne papa banne ka darr hai."

He let the sentence occupy the air. The building two lanes over sounded its brass plate against a steel vessel, like a clock.

"Roz subah dekhta hoon unhe aangan mein, chaar baje, roz — aur har saal thoda kam aadmi dikhayi deta hai, thoda zyada farz." He kept his eyes on the far roofs, where the TV antennas stood in their flocks like dead vegetation. "Pandrah saal pit-te hue, aur ek din bhi unhone yeh nahin bataya ki *kyun*. Main toota raha, woh jodta raha training se. Ab andar waali cheez phat rahi hai toh pehli khabar manzil se pehle meri zubaan pe likhi mili — *fever tha, theek hoon, kuch nahi hai.* Kis se seekha? Unhi se." He swallowed. "Toh mera darr ye nahi ki main bura banunga. Darr ye hai ki main unke jaisa ho jaunga — sahi rehte hue bhi akela, pilate bhi raho aur door bhi rakho. Ki ek din tum teeno ko meri jhooti OK-gaadi aise dekhoge jaise deewar dekhte ho. Aur poochna band kar doge."

He breathed out through the nose, once, and found the end of it: "Sabse zyada darr mujhe is baat ka hai ki ek din tum mera hisaab lena chhod doge. Ki tum heel samjhoge ke *ye toh ab aise hi hai.* Poochna mat chhodna. Mere andar kuch hai jo bola nahi jaata — tum bolna. Hamesha bolna. Ussi mein zinda rahoonga."

The sun came down like a struck cymbal into the city's west of roofs.

Nobody said *nahi yaar*, which was, right then, a mercy rare enough to be holy. The four-held thing by the tank simply stood in silence. Aadhya pressed once at the corners of her eyes with the heel of her wrist; Veer sat with his temple on his fist, the trophy of the race week standing somewhere below in his mother's puja-corner like a rumor of him; Ira, crouched by the tank's curve, scratched so small a pencil-line across her knee-level notebook that no one heard it go down.

— and Veer said, when the pause had been kind long enough:

"Toh isliye aaj hum ek niyaam banate hain. Sirf ek."

He sat up. There was in his whole frame then an uncharacteristic stillness, the gravity of the big sincere; all three of them straightened out of their slants to receive it.

"Peer promise. Ek dusre se. Kabhi bhi, kisi bhi din, andar ka darr agar daba ke rakha — toh bolna padega. Aur dusra —" he turned the flat of his palm at each of them in turn, careful, and included, "—dusra jhooth ka jawaab nahi dega. Koi bolega 'theek hoon, sab normal hai' — toh teen puchenge: *sach?* aur jawaab mein jhooth nahi chalega. Aadmi apne andar ki deewar ko girte hue pakadna chahta ho toh baaki teen rok sakte hain. Bas. Kaho."

The dusk slowly gathered its brightness up off the gulleys of roofs.

"Nahi normal hai," said Aadhya, testing the new phrase, finding it fit her. And one by one, in the laid-back tin hum of the lanes below, each voice came up:

"Sach puchenge. — Nahi normal." — Veer.

"Poochenge roz. — Nahi normal." — Ira.

"Nahi normal hai," Rudra said last, quietly, as something settled in his sternum — visibly gentled, listening; as if, under the tape and the stitches and the whole conspiracy of other people's protection, somebody had finally knocked from the outside and been told *come in.*

It was Ira who laughed first — a small round sound, thoughtless, out-loud, getting away from her like a parrot off its rope; then Aadhya, one arm in the air defending the flood in her face; then Rudra, losing the battle of his mouth-corners against his will in the half-dark; then Veer, who was to blame for all of it, whistled once in salute of the finished sun. The whole roof faced west for it, old antenna bent like something hailing home. The neighbourhood heard the clink of four cold drinks set down on a water tank, the ungoverned brightness of four nineteen-year-olds laughing down a day, and in rooms below, half a dozen people turned their heads toward it, and passed on, and smiled, and ached invisibly, with the old utterly particular grief of overheard youth.
After a while, over the pakora-tray's wreckage, they made the one operational decision of the evening.

"Kal se," Ira said, "raat ki ghumne ki list banegi. Do-do ka schedule. Ghat chabootra, kabhi Assi, kabhi Panchganga." She let the list settle in the air like a chalk-drawn circle. "Koi ladwai nahi. Koi naa-gaali nahi. Sirf dekhna. Sirf likhna. Cheez jab skill karti hai na — ka, kab, kis taraf se — mujhe dekhna hai. Aage jo bhi hona chahta hai, mere hisaab se vomit hi nahi hua chahta — poora prepared controller hua chahta hai."

"Toh ladki kehti hai planning, tum shaam ghumai bologi," said Veer, with a large grin entirely of relief. "Main ready hoon."

"Mummy ko bol dungi extra classes," said Aadhya. "Woh ab mujhpe —" she stopped, looked at the empty stairhead, translated a thought: "—bharosa kar rahi hain bina proof ke. Kal pooch bhi leti hain school mein agar uthaana ho toh uthaana padega. Figure out kar lungi."

"Ground rules," said Rudra.

"Ground rules abhi bane hain," Ira said. "Bas ek jo chahiye mujhe kal pencil mein—"

"Jo?"

"Bhaago mat," she said, quietly. Address to none of them, and all of them. "Jab bhi jo bhi hota hai — jal raha ho, bhaago, ya jhooth bolo mat bas — bhaago mat. Log jo bhaagte hain wo girte hain theli ki taraf. Chale hue jaate hain."

She nodded once, at the tank's edge.

There was a silence with a note of murmurs — then Rudra asked,

"Doosra?"

"Doosra tu pahle se jaanta hai," Ira said. "'Nahi normal hai.' Tum bolnoge na hamesha?"

"Bolega," Rudra said.

And because ceremonies, half-finished, smart like half-set bones, Veer stood up in the middle of their crew and raised his steel tumbler of nimbu-shikanji to the roof's sky and to nothing visible in the west.

"Toh formal kar dete hain," he announced. "Panchon ke saamne."

Ira tilted her head. "Panchon? Paanchwa kaun?"

"Tere padosi mein hai." He nodded, one small chin-dip, at her pocket where the compass lay against the heart-line. "Toh." The tumbler swung to the antenna: "Chaar log. Ek nazariya. Ek sheher. Aur ek cheez jiska naam abhi tak kisi ne nahi diya." No four steel tumblers on a rooftop have ever needed a linguist to add up to a toast. "Kal se — jab tak dar hai, jab tak naadaan hai, jab tak bhaagne ka mann hai — chaaron apni asli aankh se dekhna. Us hisaab se bolna. Bagair faltu ke."

"Veer bhai," Rudra said, dry as a ledger, "faltu nahi chahiye."

"Wahi toh keh raha hoon," said Veer, wounded. "Bagair *flatus* ke."

"Yeh shabd kahan se late ho tum?" Ira demanded.

"Bangalore. Meri mausi—"

"Accept," said Ira.

"Accept," said Aadhya, catching the last thread of the joke.

"Arre bhai —" Rudra raised his glass, having already lost the veto to the three voices on his roof — "Accept."

The four steel tumblers clicked on their rims over the city, all round, each edge touching each, in the dusk. The shikanji was evening-warm by then, faintly grainy, ice long surrendered; and they drank it standing, four of them on a rented roof, like people sworn in under a tent of evening; and if any other roof on the street mistook the small quiet orchestra of it for boys humming farewells in a neem-shadowed quarter, let it: some oaths read better small.

Below and west, through the cloud's last veil, a light came on in a roof-house near the college — went off — came on — off — *twice*, measured, like somebody passing a window twice to count the lane. Ira, watching without being watched, glanced at her wrist and said, too low to belong to any toast:

"Woh register waala saaf nahi hai. Kal se tum do apana yahee chakkar—"

She stopped. The light stayed off. Nobody else had seen it. The tank gurgled again, consenting to the whole arrangement of the evening, and the four glasses went empty with a last small hydrological clink, four lives raising the smallest possible toast entirely to itself.

---

Dismount was nine, across the hatch, down the tilted staircase where every resident for thirty years had put a hand on the same rail. At the bottom the lane began: dinner's smells, the music-system family adjusting one hour less loud, the carrom board going past all accounting below a porch tube-light.

They stood in the very middle of it for a while as a single unit, because the evening had got so freighted with plans and ceremonies that nobody could find who should signal the dispersal. Veer solved it, in his fashion: got the bicycle out of its lock and turned it into the send-off for all of them:

"Chal — kal paanch baje milte hain. College ke peeche wali chai pe. Aur bhaagne ka nahi, haan?" — thigh over the bar, one foot's flirting pedal — "Jitna zor se hum apas mein baant rahe hain na, utna hi bayan ho raha hai ki cheez hamari taraf tez aa rahi hai."

Then he was off, chain-song going cheerful down the lane.

Ira went opposite-wise: slight quick figure, one hand raised behind her in goodbye without turning, half her brain already counting something in units only she collected.

Aadhya and Rudra walked the last two turns to the rickshaw stand in the quiet of a hundred little homes' supper-endings. On the bench, two boys too young for the hour flicked a cigarette's ember back and forth like a counter; the sign-painter's shutter stood half-down; the milk-seller counted change for the record. She climbed into the auto sideways before it started and gave him back the whole evening in one look:

"Jo tune aaj bola, Rudra — ki poochna mat chhodna — woh sun ke mujhe bahut bada thehrav mila. Poochte rahenge. Chaahe tu jawab de, chaahe chup kar de."

"Kal paanch baje," he said. "College ke peeche wali chai. Adrak double."

"Baat yahin theek hai, double se," she said, and the auto took her away with its patient sputter, which sounded at that hour like the whole lane saying its own goodnight.

---

Home. Gate, lamp, door. He did not pass the storeroom this time: he stopped, raised three wrapped fingers, and knocked twice on the centre panel, despite the hour.

Inside: a settling sound, leather and tools remembering their arrangement. Then: "Aaja."

On the threshold, framed in it, Rudra said to the man who sat working an awl through old strap-leather in the room's collected dusk:

"Kal se main raat ko bahar ghumunga. Veer ke saath. Ghat ki taraf. Bas — socha bata doon."

The awl came out of the leather and paused. Ram did not lift his eyes; he did not need to. His stillness was all in the perfect lowered hang of the hand.

"Jaa," he said at last.

"Aur kuch?" Rudra asked — because the whole day's interrogation had taught him the inventory of his father's permissions, and he expected a list.

The awl went back in. "Prashant ke ladke se mat ladna," his father said — the neighbourhood's dusty smuggler-son, rumour of cricket-bets and quarrel. Then, lower, after the kind of silence in which a man rehearses a larger sentence and puts it back in the drawer: "…aur mere bina mat karna woh."

"Woh kya?" Rudra said.

"Seene par tape laga ke bahar jaane wala kaam," said Ram, precisely, without needing to be told.

The air between them did the thing that air in that house had been doing since before Rudra knew its name: it filled with nineteen years of rescue and went out the door on its own.

"Theek hai," said Rudra. "Ghar pe jaldi aa jaunga."

The awl paused only once more — a man's full allowance of emotion — and went on with the strap.

"Rukmini ke saath dinner pe ruk jaana," Ram said at the moment of his son's exit's first step, almost gently, in the dialect they'd worn all their lives: *eat with your mother.*

"Haan ji," said Rudra.

---

Upstairs the house closed itself like a practicing installation. And Rudra fell asleep that night with one palm flat against the taped chest, practising not being lonely, while two rooms down a lullaby without words climbed thread by thread up the dark well of the staircase and lay, at the top of it, like a folded blanket nobody was any longer certain had not been there since the house began.

They gave the thing no name; the thing was forming nonetheless; and below them the lanes sang up their habitual colours, and beyond the river — measured in her room far away with the dampness at her wrist cooling westwards — the days left said six, and held.
