# CHAPTER 20 — THE ARRIVAL

Tuesday's first job was a kitchen job: milk, moti elaichi, and a mother who had decided overnight against remaining collapsed.

Rukmini was sitting up by nine AM — against the advice of four adults in one household and one boy at the head of the stairwell doing sentry-duty — with her hair re-braided and her arms busy folding a sun-dried stack of the family's squares and with the expression, worn as mother-armour through the whole bag of the morning, of a woman who intends to be prepared when the world requires her to be the religious millinery of herself. The doctor, dethroned by tea, sat politely on one knee of the household stool, was made to drink two small glasses of the same medicine he had prescribed standing, and was escorted, exonerated from attendance, out of the house within the half hour in ceremonious fashion by Rudra's sleeve on the elbow, wearing a look of offended notarization.

"Maa," Rudra said quietly.

"Hmm."

"Aaj bhi araam —"

"Kaam hai mujhe, beta." She did not look up, did not smile: a woman setting the squares like the stones of a small fort. "Mere liye kaam hi araam hai. Chal. Uth. Budh-din neeche aa raha hai — parso aaj hogaya — *Dadi* yaad hai? Unke chhat ka din hai aaj."

"Tuesday," said Rudra.

"Jaa. Bula unko shaam ko — pehle tum charon jao." She raised her forefinger without lifting her eyes from the fold. "Jab itna bada kaam ghar ke andar hota hai, toh gharon ke darwazon pe kadam aane-aane dene chahiye. Dosti ki patti — baandh di jaati hai. Unki diary mein jo likha jaata hai —" she paused, and for a second her eyes carried the far look of the women of that coast, "— woh likhne waala kabhi lauta nahi hai."

Rudra went out to the lane and stood with his back to the door one second longer than any errand required, listening to his own house settle behind him like a great animal lying back down, and then he walked. It was Tuesday. Count, per Ira: three days. And already all day the city had been moving its furniture around in the dark somewhere, in some inventory nobody had been shown — organ after organ of its old faithful count ticking on in the wells of doorways, in the eyes of lanes. He walked.

---

All four of them climbed the neem-shadowed stairs to Veer's grandmother's roof with the gravity of a deputation. Veer led, unhurried; Ira walked second, remote in the manner of testing instruments; Aadhya carried the cloth-bundled shard against her ribs like an earthen lamp nobody had yet lit; Rudra came last, counting windows. Somewhere across the four-lane radius every afternoon school had emptied on the same whistle, and its geese ran with nylon kites over the washlines — the whole air smelled of neem and heat.

Dadi was waiting at the chhat's head in the wicker chair, hands resting open on her knees, as she had once sat, explained her own mother to her, on a summer evening in a year Veer had not witnessed and would not now be told.

"Wait kiya hai maine," she said, "bahut lambe samay se."

Her voice, when she spoke again, had gone the way voices go among the women of these lineages when the naming of names is required of them: low, and royal without antiquity — the way of somebody reading finally the first page of the ledger of a house.

"Is ghar ki chhat pe charon baith jao," she said. "Sab ke sab. Line se."

They sat, and the neem-shadow covered all four of them, as though the street god's excellent indicators were all there in service of only grandmother's table.

---
"Kul," she said, and again the word was the bell's note, "jo main tumhare par-dada ke mooh se suna tha. Ab himmat karoge toh suno. *Kul dharaa hai.* Dharaa ka matlab sirf nadi nahi hota. Dharaa ka matlab hota hai — *jo mitti zameen haath mein liye baithti rahe* aur upar — jahaan tak zaroorat hai — chadh jaaye. Samajh rahe ho chaaro? In skin ke teen-nau naam hain pad ke aage: ek dharaa jo jalta hai, ek dharaa jo bagal baandhta hai, ek dharaa jo —"— her eyes went slowly over the four, and it was not apparent whether the pause was consideration or reluctance — "— ek dharaa jo beej giraati hai, beej soonghti hai, dhool ko bolna sikhaati hai. Aur chauthi dharaa — jo ginti karti hai."

Ira's fingers pressed once against her knees.

"Main itna hi jaanti hoon, bachcho — zyada tanaashahi nahi karti. Bas itna yaad rakho: dhara mein likha rehta hai kis ka hisaab kis par chalta hai. Jo dhara bhool gayi uska bachcha mandir tak akela jaata hai." She stopped, re-set: the old dialect had wandered a step off its road; the road called it back. "Baaki charcha vishesh logon se hoti hai. Main tumse vaada karti hoon — jis din avashyakta hogi, mere paas nahi koi bade potra se bulaayega tum logon ko."

Veer said, quite soberly, "Humara kul bhi tha kisi ka, Dadi?"

"Nahi pata, beta," she said — honestly, which in that house was an event recorded. "Log apna kul sirf tab bahar laate hain jab munh ki roti ounch-hot jaa rahi ho."

She took out of a low cotton pouch at her waist the old ledger-leaf, its spine worn water-red, and wrote four names across its margin in a hand so even it seemed the hand had simply been waiting decades for the list: *Veer. Ira. Aadhya.* — and on the fourth line, following Rudra's name, the pencil came back over the words — a second stroke, an indecision — and then a slow, deep line drawn beneath the name, as though the ledger had decided to seal something rather than inscribe it.

She closed the book. (It would be the next night before Aadhya noticed in the afterward-telling that Dadi had written nothing after Rudra's name at all — no final formality — only the line, drawn and sealed.)

"Ab niklo," said the old woman. "Suraj neeche hai. Do-teen ghante aaram karo sab ke sab.

At the turn of the stair they heard her add, without looking up from the pouch's string she was retying:

"Ira beti."

"Haan Dadi?"

"Jo dharaa ginti karti hai — uska kaam khatam kabhi nahi hota. Tumhara time hi shuru hua hai. Bas itna — *kabhi apni gin na bhoolna.*" She smiled, for one second, all the way from wherever she kept it. "Jaao."

---
The instruments began their verdicts before the sun had fully decided to lower.

Ira, alone at her attic window after the dadi session, had four alarms inside one hour. First: the wrist-strip flared its pale black for one full beat and refused to cool. Second: the compass went due west — and then, impossible to the calibration that had held a whole map-sheet week the previous fortnight, tilted past bearing into a relationship with magnetism none of her texts assigned: it was not pointing north. It was *weighing,* slowly, the way scales weigh. Third: the pigeons of the Crowe Road gable — the hundred-king flock that ate first, forever, the library corn — stopped feeding at four-thirty and circled and came back down and circled again, and at four-forty-five they all landed, silently, on the same parapet toe to toe facing west. Sweepermen on four different roofs were standing rakes-frozen, facing the same direction. Below the flats the first boats of evening were turning for the ghat before they had been meant to.

She counted the signature answers before they finished arriving. The strip on her wrist took and took. *Eight. Nine. Eleven. Seventeen.* — and at *twenty* she tore out a fresh notebook page and made a professional correction across the whole register of her science. This was no tap. This was not a siphon or a spigot. What was coming in was not here to harvest the city.

What was coming in was here in the normal way things reach Varanasi: *on the bridge road, in convoy.*

She wasted two whole minutes against the window because her mother had once told her she had a teacher's pulse — and in those two minutes the whole city of her windows offered her proofs in the only grammar she could trust absolutely. The evening glass-boats were wheeling. The antenna farmfolk stood against the sky. The river's flat gold corridors, laid out westward in breadth after breadth of low cloud burning like a wall around the road to Jaunpur, gave her the estimate by shiver: *before midnight.*

Then she was down the stairs, then on the street, cycling like a rigged middle-sized avalanche.

---

Aadhya's marigold thread had been warm for an hour when she felt the answering — not count — *list,* climbing westward under her skin like text laid out of a fire. The roads with it being laid open — the west bridge, the old GT road, the bypass the trucks preferred at night — the whole western gate of the city was *breathing in.*

"Wahan aa rahe hain," she told the aloe plant on the terrace, under her breath, and the aloe, euphoniously terrified, folded and opened a leaf like a book consulted. "Log aa rahe hain. Ab beej nahi — beejon ka *shahenshah.*"

The newspaper's third page told her the rest in its own municipal hesitancy: bridge-road traffic arrivals halted and replenished; several districts' outbound traffic outstation-forwarded; halted weekdays. To a newspaperman it was minutiae between two advertisements. To her, holding it against the freshly upending purview of her smallest finger, it was the manifest of a landing.

---
She found Ira pedalling toward her at the Assi crossing at the exact moment Ira found her — which was somehow both clarifying and correct. They wheeled, merged front-and-back, and before they had fully straightened Ira was saying aloud into the wind:

"Number bolti hoon — *bees.* Bees signatures. Ek nahi, bees. Bridge-road se. Aaj ki raat — gyarah bajane tak."

Aadhya rode it like a stone landing. "Shukra ka episode yaad hai?" she said. "Apne aap ko pakad ke rakho. Bhaagne ka nahi — *ghar pe raho*. Dadi ke baad jo likha gaya na humein — is raat ke liye likha gaya."

They split the lanes between them by unspoken rights: Ira went for Veer, Aadhya for Rudra's lane. Word moved as it always moved in this city — by mouths at crossings. Within forty minutes the four of them had their standing instructions, written by nobody, agreed by all: *be indoors. Be visible. Be exactly where your mothers expect. And after ten — nobody be alone.*

And at five-thirty the whole dossier of the day completed itself into a single plain total, repeated by Ira to Veer at his gate, by Aadhya to Rudra at his, like a child delivering medicine twice to the same fever:

*bees.* Twenty points of arrival. Twenty doors, all knocking at once.


By six the library's lantern portico stood chain-locked an hour early. Shastri-ji had not chained the gate in that style in forty years; the regulars who found it so stood at the bottom of the steps in the evening dust arguing with the closure as with a land deed.

To the girl waiting with her back to the landing's window-frame, he came up the inside stairs in person, with a small brown-paper parcel in both hands, tied with the red-and-white string of bookkeeping offices.

"Aadhya beti." She came forward. "Ye diary tumhare paas rahe. Ab se. Kal subah tak — *nahi* — tab tak jab tak main khud na maangu." He put it into her hands with the insistence of a man paying off a debt the bank would never call in. "Kisi ko mat dikhaana. Aur —" the white beard settled, the way it did when the next sentence had been queued for thirty-six years — "agar kisi raat tumhen woh shabd samajh na aaye, toh diary ke aakhri panne par likha ek naam hai. Us ko bhi yahin mat lana. Use *yaad* rakhna aur apne bachchon ko mat dikhana. Main apni naseeb zubaan se likh gaya hoon; tum abhi apni qalam se likh rahi ho. Farq bas itna hai."

"Kya naam hai, Dada?"

"Tumne poochha hi nahi kabhi — main bhi na bolunga. Kal library bandh rahegi. Aadat se. Aur agar kabhi tumhari kitaab poori khatam ho jaaye padhne se pehle —" the smile opened briefly, an umbrella in low light, "— toh aadmi chhat pe chadh ke dekh leta hai, baarish kitni door pahunchi hai. Baarish abhi Valmiki-nagar tak pahunchi hai. Baaki upar hai."

He patted her head once — one flat palm, squarely laid, the acknowledgment of service — took the keys from their pouch,and shut the hall against the whole settled evening of its own captivity.

"Jao beti," he said. "Badhti raho." Then, into the doorway's becoming dark: "Aur — jo tum soch rahi ho apni maa ke baare mein — bilkul sahi soch rahi ho."

His eyes above the beard were bright with something nobody could have named and been believed. She went down the chained steps slowly, and at the bottom step she let herself do the thing no daughter of a serious house was permitted on a public street at evening — she let herself run.

---

---

Evening did its unremarkable office.

At Rudra's house the dinning table was set to the family's four corners exactly as on every Tuesday any of them could remember: father at the head, mother at the stove's hand, boy at the foot — and the fourth corner set on its little cushion for nobody presently assigned. No one asked why. Rukmini set that place herself every week as unanswerably as clocks. Tonight, because the family had exhausted its silence well before the dal arrived, she moved through the kitchen on sheer rank and paid them all home — mother-wages in warm bowls. Ram ate as he had eaten for the last seven thousand evenings: in silence, posture intact, one wrist under the plate's own authority. Rudra ate exactly as he had eaten all of this last entrancement — while upstairs under the floorboards four crossed plates allowed themselves to wait in the family's fixed constellations.

She fell asleep in the chair at ten, both ends of her leg bound industrially around, and at ten-sixteen Ram carried her to her room as he had done every night of four decades: half a house in the arms of one quiet watchman, totally unnecessary, completely mandatory — the one minute each day on which the family chart kissed its own harbour.

Across the city the same tally was writing itself into every other family's weather.

Veer dined with his grandmother at the low chowki-table. She had retired his wrist herself earlier — new cotton thread, thick, wound and knotted with the unhesitating speed of a station-master clearing a train: two passes of the thumb, a check to the knothood, a nod. "Bolta hai woh haath?" she asked. "Bolta hai, Dadi," he said. The old lady made the coppery sound in her throat that was that kitchen's highest order of approval, and served him the first roti.

Ira spent the evening at her window accounting: Tuesday's count — _3 din_ — ruled under Monday's ink, one line over, steady, like a date written an hour early on a lease. Then, at half past ten, the ledger cancelled itself. The number that arrived in its place was delivered by no instrument at all: she saw it lit on the pigeons' facing wall, lit on the white parapet wall, lit on even her own second shadow on the floor under the lamp: *bees.* Go backteed numbers were her own work; the new one was the colony's. She checked nothing. She underlined it twice.

Then the ward heard itself: at 10:23 the first street dog yelped once and turned toward the river — one yip, the way benchmen pay the first coin of a hearing. At 10:27 every dog of the quarter repeated it in relay. At 10:31 all the dogs stopped, together, enthralled, like a string-section finishing a page. By 10:31 they were all sitting stock-still facing the west, the way pigeons had faced it already at four-forty-five. And nobody outside a thin vertical column of houses — the young ones and the grandmothers of the young ones, and the ones who'd been lieutenants in the mystery from the start — knew at all that anything was asking.

And Aadhya wrote her diary — the pencil columns, the closed fold, its best clause entered as always with the small ceremony of one who writes leaving a prayer's grip:

*"Tuesday. Dadi ne chaaron ke naam likh liye. Maine dekha — Rudra ke naam ke baad kuch nahi likha. Sirf ek rekha kheenchi. Ira ne kaha count 3 tha. Shaam ko wo *bees* banke aa gayi. Rudra ke dad, godi mein maa hum — yeh dekhne waale saare ab so jaate hain. Bas do din the waadhaane kiye the humko. Aur ab shabd beech mein ruk gaya hai... Shankha jo ek crot beed hai."*

She put down the pencil and placed her head against the back of the chair. From her window, low and easy to dangle over, the direct lamps of the westward road were visible away off in the distance, lined up like a long permanent queue.

At five minutes past ten she stood up, moved to the pane, transfixed by the lamp-queue, and for the first time in weeks locked her hands together exactly as temple-duty taught her mother the daughter to do for messages no one else can take delivery of; and her small ring finger went gold in the dark under the bandage, wholly accounted, entirely unpaid.

**"Jo aa rahe ho,"** she said aloud into the dark of the quarter, in the breath of somebody coming up for air over a well.

**"Sun lo — yeh tumhaari zamaana-nagri nahi hai. Tum isse road kehte ho. Hamus se sunoge tum — *yeh zameen hai.*"**

And it answered. The courtyard reprieved one last bow. Two blades of grass in the flower-tin straightened, in a way that never required you to measure them, under the window's final black — and if the city had a reply of its own it was curious and unhurried, like people in rooms above adapting a ration for them — but it did eventually rise with the wind that came off the west carrying dust and diesel and the thinnest salt of the river-road — and with it, passing far over everything on its way to the bridge, the city's answer to every earnest address ever made from a rooftop:

*haan.*

---
---

The city put itself to bed by tens. Dinner ran quiet; kitchens ran double the sounds to cover the day. Nobody in Rudra's lane asked aloud why every dog of the ward had come home before ten and faced the west side of its owner's roof.

At eleven-fifty Rudra carried his tray — the brass bottle, the dabbas Rukmini had filled herself from the chair, her one day's argument for being needed — up the back stairs to the roof's parapet, the terminus of his whole nineteen years of house, and sat down beside his father on the parapet's crown.

Ram was already standing. He had been standing for some time.

The moon had come off the far bank, low and white; the air's blown cotton had thinned; the Sankat Mochan bell — rung by nobody's counted hand that night — had just completed four slow deliberate notes over the west rooftops, and off north-east the whole cabled length of the bridge lay strung from stem-lamp to stem-lamp in its own sacrificial evening gold.

"Baith," Ram said.

Rudra sat on the high parapet-stone beside him, adding nothing the night had not already said.

Twice the river sounded below — the groan of a pier-rope, the small whistle the bridge's lamps made when the wind threaded them — and the third sound was not a river sound at all: a long flat thrum coming off the west road, long enough to travel through every roof-tile under them and shake the fine dust off the parapet close to where father and son were watching.

Then Ram, with both hands flat on the low wall — the posture of twenty-nine private midnights rolled into one — spoke without turning around:

"Unnees saal pehle ek aadmi mujhe andar baitha ke — mere saamne baithe hue — mujhe sikhaaya ki zameen ke neeche kya hai. Uska naam main kisi ko nahi doonga. Do haath the uske. Do aankhein thi. Aur ek baat usme thi jo main aaj tak bhool nahi paaya — woh mujhse zyada taqatwar tha, aur uspar mujh ko maarne mein koi hichkichahat nahi thi." He checked nothing, consulted nothing: the whole delivery was memorized and not memorized, like a private rate he had observed every night at some hour for nineteen years. "Usne mujhe tabhi kaha tha — jo rakhte hain usse zyada hai, jo kho rakhte hain. Aur jis din wo mujhe sab chhod ke chale gaye, tab maine ek hi cheez kan mein band kar li thi: *zamaana humesha us roop mein waapas aata hai jis roop mein hum use pehli baar khote hain.*"

"Aapse," Rudra said softly, toward the bridge rather than the face — "yahi chhupa rahi thi aapse unnees saal?"

His jaw moved once. Whatever was aright arrived. "Aur woh raat, tera chhota haath — sadak pe maine pehli baar khoya. 4 baje. Tu bhi nahi samjha."

There was no answer possible to this; Rudra did not give one. Out of the west, away over the district's flat-line of shed roofs, rose the first far shriek of the convoy with all the wetness and transition of things coming together from far across town — the soft unused hinge of metal heated by its own momentum through night's cool humidity — too many, too proud. Both hands of the father waited flat on the parapet. The dust knew what was grazing by on the other side of the city. The house stared with a long-lasting parish of sounds shut over lane after lane.

And the visible convoy began arriving.

They did not announce themselves as cars. They behaved on the bridge like a statement behaves on a wall: eight of them, long and black and similar-built, sheen whose surfaces carried no visible window-glows whatever, pressing their dark noses out of the westward gloom into the stem-lamp lights one after another in a straight continuous file – filing like the closing debits of a public ledger — crossing the *Ganges by it* without a single honk the whole breadth.

And at their lead, conspicuously, knowingly, eroding the rest into one heliocentric rank, went one slimmer and darker than them all — a child's length ahead of the next one.

Rudra, beside his father, made no sound. But he felt — as one feels on the face of a cold mountain the parted breath of the person with you suddenly stop — Ram's whole stillness tick upward, fall, and hold; the father's two hands grasped the low wall and did not tighten, and under his left thumb the dust of the wall came away anyway in one smooth curl, without being asked.

"Dad," Rudra said, with the bird's-breath voice of the managed seventeen tons greeting the unreadable world ahead of it. "Aapne already se pehchaand diya na use. Pehchaan liya na."

Below them the river moved toward the bridge's arches, black and available. Over its own surface the lead car reached the middle arch. The driver-side window was unrolled — three inches, then four — slow as hidden cogs — and a face presented itself to the city's procession of lamps: a face the colour of unfinished stone, with the kind of beard that had once been a name, attentive to the west side with all its immense history of casualness arranged on its lean skull like an honour.

Ram had already guessed before the window came down. Guessed for nineteen years. As long as his son had been breathing and mistaken and nourished and carefully adored like his own, this piece of knowledge had had to be somewhere close to guilt long before it was permitted to be anything else, long before it could be tried on the evening's balance-sheet:

*Ram had recognized the man in the lead car the moment the first signature touched his roof-tiles.*

And his son stood beside him, not seeing the bridge's smoke or the river's rope — watching the face it had cost nineteen years to learn to watch — and saw in it the door open on the room he had been petitioned all his youth to leave shut.

"It's starting." Ram Vashisht said it in English, evenly and without breathiness, the way weathered men conclude a memorandum they have carried a lifetime; and then — speaking it to the bridge-tower, and then over the river, and then so as to give it entirely to his son beside him and not at all across the city — *"It's starting. Everything I trained you for. Everything I was afraid of."*

And in that exact half-minute, in four separate houses across the city, four people stopped what they were doing, heart-first.

Aadhya, at her diary's pane with the lamp turned off and the gold thread lit under her bandage, lifted her head and did not pretend she didn't know the shape of what had made her lift it.

Ira, who had already been at her window for thirty-three minutes with everything being answered off her instruments, wrote down the time like a registrar: *11:54.* And beside it, without hesitation, the name she had been spending several months deciding ought not to be her hand: **it.**

Veer, on the chhat while his grandmother retied his wrist thread in the dark, felt all his arm weep through the sleeve — looked up — and knew. His grandmother did not turn her head either. She said, without looking up from her knot: "Shuru ho gaya, beta."

And Rukmini, alone at the curtained door of her room where her husband and son had been from the roof for the last quarter hour — who was holding herself to a straightness past the orders of any doctor — folded her bedspread once for nobody, once for the floor, and said, inside herself, in the tone mothers keep for an architecture of children who may need to sleep at the back of it: *idhar so jao. Tum teeno hain abhi.*

And the eight black vehicles crossed the Ganges bridge by the city's own lamps, entered the city's own gates without announcers, and crossed their own continuing nightward — and over Varanasi — seen or unseen — the first bell of the register rang its flat of the count and opened nothing at all.
