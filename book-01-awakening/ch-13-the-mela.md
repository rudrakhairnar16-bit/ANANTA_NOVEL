# CHAPTER 13 — THE MELA

His mother issued the orders at breakfast, in the form of a statement about other people's children.

"Sharma ji ke ladke log mela dekhne ja rahe hain raat ko," she said, sliding the third paratha onto his plate with the inexorable authority of a state granting citizenship. "Dev Deepawali pe. Itna bada din hai aur mera beta ghar mein baithega toh padosi log kahenge kuch baat hai."

"Kaunsi baat?" Rudra said, mouth already half-betrayed by paratha.

"Yehi — ki kuch baat hai." She turned back to the stove. "Veer ko bhi bol dena. Woh pilla panch-din se apni maa ke saamne bhi tape laga ke ghoom raha hai."

From the dining end of the table, behind the newspaper (today folded normally — a diplomatic signal whose significance was not lost on either of them), his father made no comment at all. Rudra, who had learned at six years of age to read this household like a field officer reads weather, interpreted: *permission, granted by one government, unopposed by the other.* Dev Deepawali night. Three nights hence. The ghats would be a single unbroken necklace of fire from Rajghat to Assi, which was a thing that happened every year and felt, this year, less like a festival and more like the city arguing its own case to the sky.

"Mela hum log saath mein dekh ke aayenge," said Rukmini, to the stove, the room, and the sky. "Usi din."

Dev Deepawali on the ghats of Varanasi is not a festival; it is an argument that the city makes, once a year, with the dark.

The argument goes like this: that fire, when it is small enough, multiplies. That a single brass diya holding one tablespoon of ghee and one cotton wick is nothing; that a hundred of them are a temple; that five hundred thousand of them, laid step by step along eighty-eight ghats in the keeping of volunteers and priests and boatmen and chai-wallas and grandmothers and children and thieves, become a shore of gold fifteen kilometres long — become, from the river's side, from mid-current where the tourist boats rock at their anchors, a second city that burns alongside the first, a Varanasi of smokeless flame laid down for one night only, for the gods' own stairway down to the river on the night of the full moon of Kartik.

They had arrived, by custom, by the lanes' oldest choreography: e-rickshaw to the point where wheels give up, then the walk — past the pooja-thali sellers triple-shifting, past the cane-furniture godown woken out of a decade to rent its frontage to a toy importer, past the municipal loudspeaker broadcasting *savdhaani* to no one available — and out at Assi's slope where the temporary fairground had assembled itself over the week like a travelling republic: the giant wheel turning against the charcoal sky in a spine of naked bulbs; the wooden drum of *maut-ka-kuan* exhaling dust and engine-scream every forty seconds as two young men orbited the horizontal world on second-owner motorcycles; the toy street with its year's trove of inflatable spacemen and paper horns; mehendi women along the mats, patient as monks, drawing onto new girls' palms the same paisley drawn at this spot for decades. A thousand smells conducted themselves without any sense of municipal authority: roasting corn, diesel, cheap attars, gunpowder praline, hot sugar breaking.

Veer acquired the pink bear balloon approximately here. Documented later as costing forty rupees, defended by him as *free complementary* with the purchase of roasted peanuts by means no one could reconstruct, it rode above them the rest of the evening like the expedition's own small blimp.

"Log dekh rahe hain," Aadhya informed him, once, accurately.

"Toh dekhen," said Veer, with the total inner peace of the innocent. "Mela mein sab apna level dikhate hain."

The four of them stood at Assi's top step at six-fifteen and looked north along that shore of gold, and nobody said anything for eleven seconds, which — for these four — was a historical epoch.

"Aath lakh diye," Ira said at last, very softly, giving it the dignity of data. "Kam se kam. Poore sheher ne aaj raat ke liye apni poori sena bitha di hai."

"Grammar book mein likh lena," said Aadhya, laughing, and hooked her arm through Ira's elbow — a motion so unearned and so specific to women's friendships that it briefly startled the years' worth of arm's-length vigilance out of both of them. "Chal. Jalebi pehle. Phir jaake dekhenge kya disaster tum log plan karke aaye ho."

The jalebi stand was old, and holy, and its holiness resided in a vat of bubbling ghee wide enough to bathe a small deity, tended by a man whose family had fried batter on that same corner since before the corporation had painted the corner yellow. He fried in the dark between two electric bulbs, with the fat light coming up off the ghee and illuminating his face from beneath like a portrait of contented damnation, and the queue bent for thirty meters.

"Moti jalebi," Rudra said, at the counter finally. "Ek plate. Sirf moti wali."

"Patli," said Aadhya, immediately, from beside him, without consultation.

"Tu tasteless hai," Rudra said. "Patli jalebi pehli nazar mein jalebi lagti hai aur khaane pe plastic. Moti jalebi ke andar syrup ki gaanth banti hai — *banti hai*, na ki baith jaati hai—"

"Logo, logo," Aadhya announced to thirty metres of queue, one arm out like a traffic constable, "isse dekho. Ye jalebi ke andar gaanth ki baat kar raha hai jaise iska ghar isi mein ho. Patli wali crisp hoti hai, ek dum kadak — moti wali sirf soft aur budhiya hoti hai —"

"—aur yahi donut-wali soch ne is desh ko aaj tak pahunchaya hai!" Rudra informed the queue, which had begun, in the ancient democratic manner of all Indian queues, to weigh in from both flanks — an old man condemning patli as "instant", a bride in new sneakers explaining to her husband that the difference was the *ghee-to-chashni ratio and that he would never understand anything in his life*, and a small boy on his father's shoulders, inexplicably and passionately, chanting *moti! moti! moti!*.

The jalebi-wala, beaming, listening to his life's philosophy refought for free in front of his own vat, fixed it: "Aadha-aadha," he decreed, and made two cones from newspaper and tore the difference clean. He would not accept money for the theory; this was his offering to the evening.

They ate the compromise at the ghat's edge, in the forecourt of the fire, while the loudspeakers somewhere on the main Dashashwamedh platform played old film bhajans between announcements and the river received its diyas from a hundred hands every second — boat-men launching the little offerings from mid-current, children lowering them from the steps with dazed liturgical care, the whole mirrored ghatscape swimming with small lights sailing south like a migration of benevolent insects.

"Dono achhi hai, tum dono bakwaas log ho," Veer announced through his mouth and both elbows, four cones' worth of judgment already gone in his cheeks.

The giant wheel took them next, because from it, Ira announced to nobody's surprise, one could see the drift-patterns of the whole crowd. They loaded in pairs — the natural mathematics of it executing itself without any of the four acknowledging there had been mathematics: Veer and Ira ahead, gondola swinging under his registered mass like a coin-purse on a nail; Rudra and Aadhya behind, entering to the patient's attendant lurch of ferrous furniture, sitting, the bar coming down.

Up they went. Varanasi arrange itself again below them — first ghats banked in gold, then the gilded roofs, then the overhead ornamentation of wires adorning the colony lanes, and last, as they hung at the top, the whole view: the town like an instrument case of jewels stirred by a black river. Aadhya forgot about height-fear because she'd never had a chance to cultivate one. She put both hands on the ridiculous safety bar and said, in a voice from before there were evenings like this:

"Yaar."

Rudra said nothing, in a manner that meant *haan, dekha maine bhi* — and that was the entire conversation until they went over the top and came down the far side, at which point she said, laughing into the wind's mouth, "Agar gir gaye toh jalebi ka case family court jaayega," and it was probably the best minute of the week for both of them, which neither then nor afterwards was ever going to mention.

At the toy-shooting gallery midway — a stall of tin ducks rowing left on a wire track under posters disowned by copyright — the stall-man offered five shots for twenty; Rudra took the spring rifle, checked the bent front sight against the tin with a quarter-second's adjustment learned with air-dried rags and pebbles back, way back, in the courtyard. He dropped four ducks in four shots and handed the rifle back.

The stall-man stared at him, then at the ducks, then in the bin where the prizes waited, and said, quietly and with conviction, to the world: "Koi toh aata hai achhe ghar se."

"Usse de dena," Rudra said, taking the sad little stuffed dog off the hook and handing it to the girl without looking at her, meeting the eyes of the midway.

Aadhya took the dog and looked at it for a long second. "Isko main kya karun?"

"Yaad rakhna jitna banduk-paper training mere liye teri taraf free mein rahi," Rudra said, gravely, folding his arms.

"Ha. *Ha.* Fine. Aata hun," and she walked in front to carry it, with the light from the wick-lines catching her shoulders, letting five unseen bystanders conclude, inaccurately on fifty counts, that the two of them were celebrating an engagement.

"Jhoola dekhega aakhiri mein. Pehle woh phir se maut ka kuan dikhane le jaa raha hai —" Veer tilted his head at the fairground bowls of noise above the Assi slope, where the barrel-cyclists' riders were even now defying municipal physics inside their wooden drums — "whoever's scared of the balloon, you know where my lap is."

---

Veer's arm-wrestling defeat of the mela's reigning tables began, as such things always begin, as lunch-bet idiocy and became, within half an hour, the whole evening's greased downhill slide.

The arm-wrestling table of this particular fairground — a module of the mela circuit that concerns itself with honour, shirts up the bicep, and a electricity of excited small-wagers — was reigned over by a soot-sweat goliath with the build of a municipal water-tank, who was popularly known, all along the Dev Deepawali circuit, by the nom de guerre of **Bhainsa** — standing, unstated, for buffalo. He had, by tallies chalked on a nearby wall by a promoter employed purely for the purpose, won one hundred and nine consecutive competitive wrestles at mela after mela, and he received challengers the way pianos receive moths.

Veer sat down opposite him for a ten-rupee stake and a rising ring of spectator noise.

"Chhote sahab," said Bhainsa, kindly, meaningfully squeezing a coconut of a fist, "deewar pe dekh raha hai mera record? Ek sau nau."

"Haan ji," said Veer bashfully, sitting, planting his elbow, settling his grip — and Rudra noticed, with the dry ice of dread, the way his friend had, entirely by instinct, *set* himself: elbow vertical, shoulder neutral, spine carrying load the way the courtyard drills from a different world had taught his spine when they were fifteen and being trained, neither one by the other but by the same merciless arithmetic, remembering a boyhood of staff corrections none of them knew the origin of —

Audience howling — *khao khao* — lock-grip — *ready—* —

"Chal—" said Bhainsa.

The arm went down backwards through all of Bhainsa's expectations with the irresistible momentum of a suspension bridge deciding to lie down. The tent's benches — twenty-two spectators — went up with the collective *oooooooo* of one single body. The promoter, chalking at his century-scored wall-board, froze mid-wicket, then realized, blinking, that One Hundred And Nine was about to revise. In the stands, people were shouting the whole Mahabharata with their bodies.

"Rematch," said Bhainsa, in the civilized and slightly horrified tones of the giant men that all tall tales are required by law to feed. "Ek aur."

Veer, who had not meant to — who *knew*, Rudra could see him knowing it in the exact instant after, with horror, colour rising — had fired one careless push of whatever the ghat-fight had mixed into his forearm, and had mistaken it for nothing at all. He stood up out of the chair backwards, smiling — people-pleasing, horrified —

"Bas bas — ek hi game, bhaiya —" and he dispersed the storm by clapping his own big hands together and thrusting the prize — the hundred rupees and a paper cone of roasted chana — back among the riot of his brand-new fairground legend, declining rematch with both palms raised in surrender, and Rudra got a shoulder around him and walked him out of there, laughing furiously, saying everything but the thing:

"Mazaak mein bhi itna daraya kyun karta hai yaar tu — ek wahi table hai poore mele mein jahan sambhalna zaroori tha."

"Maine kuch nahi kiya," Veer whispered, ducking through the tent-flap into the open fairground lights, almost pleading, almost laughing. "Rudra — sun raha hai? Maine *kuch bhi nahi* kiya."

I know, Rudra didn't say. Yehi toh problem hai, bhai. Kuch kiye bina yeh haal hai.

The fortune-teller's stall was two tarpaulin roofs away. Its owner-operator — a Bhojpuri-weight patriarch of a forecaster in a saffron turban gone coral pink with sun — had deployed the traditional constellation of props: a deck of worn "question-cards," a wheezing collection of hand-drawn zodiac charts, and a blue-plumaged parrot in a cage the size of a shoebox whose divine occupation it was, twelve hours a day, to pull cards out of a deck for ten rupees a prophecy.

Ira stopped in front of this establishment with the expression of a statistician beheld by a shrine.

"Dekho," she said. "Data ka mandir."

"Ira—" Aadhya began, who knew.

"Tum log uncertainty ki maa-behen karke maze lete ho, par yahan—" Ira gestured one open hand at the parrot's cage. "—ye hai. Reference standard. Mujhe dekhna hai ki prakriya kaise chalti hai. Paanch minute. Bas dekh rahi hoon—"

"Ira, _don't,_" said Rudra, hoping against twenty-two years of the city's physics.

She was already doing it. The patriarch, with the princely adaptability of men who have been asked by the decade for directions, conversions, job consultations and once — he reminisced weekly — a certificate for a wisdom tooth, registered her standing there in her kurta and her dry annihilating poise, read her ratio of devotion-to-spectacle in half a second, and without a word vacated the stall's one stool and presented it to her with the little consular bow men reserve for calamities or other proprietors.

"Beta, ek customer de do pehla," the patriarch requested patiently to both heavens, as Ira sat down. The parrot, sensing career change, practised breathing on the cage bars.

The first customer they drummed in was a fiftyish government-accountant grade passer-by whose question, shouted down from the porch of a nearby matar-chat stall, concerned his daughter's UPSC attempt. Ira took the man's hand the way she'd take a compass bearing — professionally, by the wrong end, left hand because Table A said left hand — told him to ask the parrot for a card — declined the card — closed her eyes exactly once, for the length of a wrist's cooling —

"Teen attempt ho gaye uski," she said, eyes still shut. "Problem Math nahi hai. Pakka nahi. *Paper* se pehle bhi wo blank ho jaati hai. Behan ko bolo poora sirf revise karaye ek baar, taking *notes out loud.*" She opened her eyes. "Is bar ho jayega. Shakht mehnat waali ladki hai. Daily sote waqt fan bahut zor ka chalti hai — wo band karwa dena. Neend se aadha paper banega."

The accountant stood blinking with ten rupees in his fingers. Then he put them down on the deck — folded twice, reverent — and said "Dhanyawaad beti" in the tone used for buses caught in time, and went off at speed. The patriarch watched him leave.

"Chauthi mein laga jitenge toh aana phir," the patriarch called after him, one professional to another, and then held the stool for Ira in silence, like a man signing over the bar to a better bartender. The parrot, watching the changed tenancy of its little stage, produced one ribald syllable of cage-opinion and went about its nuts. It had decided she was one of its own. The patriarch had decided she was one of his. Neither was wrong.

"Ye kya —" Rudra started, horrified-delighted.

"Seekh rahi thi bas," Ira explained, dead-eyed, as if detected in theft. "Main toh free mein li rahi thi information. Uska naam, bache, sab visible the. Main bhi dekh sakti hoon logon ko." But when the second customer sat down already — an anxious young mother with two toddlers and green mehendi fading off her ring finger — Ira went very still for two seconds, exactly the stillness of someone who has found a fish-hook under the water where a lily should be, and then gave the woman two entirely conventional sentences about patience and hidden blessings, and the woman went away exalted, and Ira would not meet Aadhya's eyes for ten minutes.

Aadhya, who knew precisely the dimensions of the look, took her elbow at the ninth minute and steered her out to the river.

"Kya tha wo?" she said quietly, when the fairground was half a diya behind them.

Ira looked out at the kneeling dark water. "Ghar ka address dikha mujhe," she said at last. "Kaunsi khidki, kaunsa lock. Bata deti toh kal raat koi wahan phir se ghus jaata — ya kuch. Main nahi bolti aisi cheezein." She folded her arms. "Problem ye hai, Aadhya — mujhe dikhta hai. Sabka. Bina pooche. Jaise mela mein wo photo lenewale hain na — log aate jaate rehte hain, photo ban jaati hai. Aaj tak maine kabhi kisi ka 'nakli' nahi bola, kabhi kisi ka asli matlab nahi bataya. Par aaj raat —" she stopped, and there was, for a heartbeat, a girl under the archivist — "—aaj raat mujhe wo aadmi bhi dikha jo parrot wale ke peeche baith ke diagram copy kar raha tha. Hamara nahi. *Apna.* Doosra jo kheench raha hai."

Aadhya put her arm around her — one arm, the temple-touch of shared ordinance — and let the river answer instead: the waves, the lights, the boats of gold, the little diyas sailing out of their makers' lives like errata.

They were all four still together at eleven, and nothing had exploded, which set a Book-of-Acts precedent.

The mela had softened into its final form by then: the vendors tearing down, lights coming off poles, the crowd's noise settling from human weather into the machinery of closing — bench-planks stacking, ropes unhooking, the giant wheel giving one last ceremonial rotation for nobody. The four of them stood at the water's edge, in a lane of matted grass where a thousand feet had passed, each of them about to say their goodnights; none of them saying them.

On the river before Dashashwamedh, out of some custom old beyond photograph, a last boat moved along mid-current throwing lamps in slow handfuls to the waves; and along the ghats, from Rajghat's far end down to Assi's forsaken scholars, the great dial turned: half a million flames going out one by one, extinguished not by wind but by intention — by hands that knew the hymns for the hour a song is over.

Before the last of them went, they did the thing themselves.

It was Aadhya who announced it was happening — went down the wet steps and bought five leaf-bowls off a boy half-asleep at his heap, paid him double for the impertinence of staying open for them, and loaded each bowl with its wick, its marigold, its thimble of ghee. And they stood in a row at the river's stone hem, the four of them, with the stranger's fifth — "kisi ke liye," the boy had said, unspecified — and lit them off each other's hands, and were quiet then, each with their pinch of flame, while the crowd-light went down like a stage around a closing act, each of them visited briefly by one private thing before setting it afloat.

*Veer* — holding the leaf-bowl with two fingers the size of small bananas, wanting as always to laugh and not daring to — who made the wish the way he had always made all important transactions, with zero negotiation: maa ke pair ka var. Uske andar sab aa jayega. Nikalte hue cheez sab aa jayegi.

*Ira* — who negotiated: *ek hi proof de do. Ek. Bs ek. Koi aur bhi dekh raha ho toh dikha do.* And held the flame; and in the flame, two rings deep, she caught herself staring at — wonder of wonders — the map: its seven rings, centred not below her but above. She put the bowl in the water with great care, as if it contained a very rare illness.

*Aadhya* — who wished, first, automatically, for her mother's health, the way you renew insurance; and then, out of nowhere her own, with an ache under the ribs she twenty days ago would have disallowed entry to: *jo bhi mujhe dekh raha hai, in chaaron ko dekhne de. Inhe choot mat lagne de.* She got both fingertips wet in the leaf's wake; the flame stayed small and did not gutter.

*Rudra* — did not make a wish. He had been raised by drill-command to fan wishes out evenly through the count; but tonight he let one coin of a thought drop, silently, into the only wishing-well of a city that used them up for food: *itni raat tak rahe. Bas. Itni raat tak.*

They let the five bowls go; the little flotilla of them pushed off the stones and stood into the current and sailed away down through the dead lamps like goldfish down a drain the size of the ocean.

"Ek second," said Aadhya suddenly, softly, pulling all their attention back out of the dark from wherever she had withdrawn to consider. She had a difference to note. "Rudra — dekh. Hum log jahan bhi move ho rahe hai na — udhar udhar diye ki taraf bheed jama ho rahi hai. Dekh — dekho. 'Passage' wali cheez —"

Rudra looked.

He had not put it into order until she spoke it; then he knew he had been tracking it for the last twenty minutes: the crowds, when they gathered, drifted over the fairground in slow blind clots; but along the ghatside tonight the clots had seams; along the seams, like thread going under black cloth, a second motion — the diya-lines still lit, interrupted at fixed intervals where people were not stepping, leaving vacancies like buckteeth in the light — and along those lanes of dark the dying lights were not simply going out. They were going out *faster.*

And the vacancies were at the six points he knew.

And under his collar, his mother's taped lines turned over faintly and slept again, as if she had nodded at the catalogue.

"Order hai isme," he said — low, to Veer, who had come up behind. "Battiyan nahi bujh rahi — *kheenchi jaa rahi hain.* Lane se. Point se."

"Ira ko bata," said Veer.

"Sabko bata raha hoon," he said, and turned.

Ira was already looking at him with both fists dug deep into the windcheater pockets, a mouth already full of whatever arithmetic today had handed her — the night the city should have held its greatest single deposit of fire-and-prayer, and the earnings known to be ending before their normal period — she had not yet even reached for paper; she was simply, hard-eyed, *standing in the answer.*

"Gyarah se ghata kar," she said, "ab *aath* din bache hain. Aaj ke baad aur kam. Aaj raat unhone inn sab se hi bhar liya." And she took out her hand at last, and rubbed at her eyes with her whole wrist, the way teenaged mothers do to break intimacy with their tiredness — and across the river, in the dark, the last unaccompanied cone of a vessel-lamp drifted over Manikarnika and sank soundlessly through nothing specific, the way gold coins sink without ceremony through the black lanes of the underworld.

They stood together in the leftover heat of five hundred thousand lamps, and did not say anything more, because nobody needed to, not tonight and not again: because *they were four*, and four, tonight, was a number nobody had assigned them and they had decided — separately and entirely — to be.
