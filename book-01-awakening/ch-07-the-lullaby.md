# CHAPTER 7 — THE LULLABY

Rukmini spent the whole of that day rebuilding what could not be seen.

This was her oldest labor and her most secret one, older than the marriage-photograph over the kitchen shelf, older than the wobbly-legged table, almost as old as her son. Nineteen years of mornings: before the dal, before the stove's first hiss, she stood at the kitchen window with her eyes shut and her thumbs pressed together and rebuilt the knots. Forty positions. Counted twice. The work took six minutes and was as invisible from the outside as a woman listening to a pressure-cooker; Ram had watched her do it, on and off, for nineteen years, and had never once asked, because in this house some threads were not pulled by mutual consent, and because he had his own versions of the same prayer.

But today the knots had resisted. Twice her count had slipped — not her count; her count was perfect, had been perfect for nineteen years; rather, the *knotting itself* had snagged, mid-position, like thread that's gone over the same stretch of cloth too many times. Nineteen years of stitch on the same seam. She had redone position twenty-eight four times and felt, in the snagging, a thinness that had not been there when he was ten, when he was fifteen, a gloss and give like rain-worn steps in the temple ghats.

At the stove, stirring the evening dal, she caught herself humming.

She stopped instantly — hand to her own mouth, ladle raised, the way you'd snuff a candle with two fingers. The melody had escaped. She hadn't hum it — not consciously, not on purpose, not since the first year. It had climbed out of her without permission, the way grief climbs out of old women at weddings.

It was the lullaby. The one from before. The one that had no Hindi words because the grammar it was built in wasn't Hindi's grammar, or Sanskrit's, or anything any wandering scholar would be able to write down in Devanagari. Eleven intervals, no taal she knew would hold them; when she was young she had tried to find them on a harmonium at a neighbour's and failed — the keys simply didn't go that way.

She stood quite still with the ladle going cold, listening for the house. Nothing. Ram's footsteps upstairs in the storeroom, inventorying anchors. The gate shut. The street outside ordinary.

*Don't,* she told her mouth. *It's a door. Open it a crack, and things notice.*

And she put it away — folded it down somewhere beneath the sternum where all the unsingable things lived — and ground the evening's garlic, and listened for the gate's iron sentence, and something vast below the floorboards — not a floorboard away, not a city away, but *under* — turned its hearing approximately toward the scent of her tune, briefly, like an old dog lifting one ear.

The flame on the second burner leaned once, sideways, in no wind at all.

She looked at it.

It straightened.

"Nahin," Rukmini said quietly to the burner, in the voice a mother uses to tell a child *not today*. "Abhi nahin."

---

The afternoon had been its own small campaign of avoidance.

Rudra had lasted exactly forty minutes of the thermodynamics lecture before the chalk's sssss-ing against the board began to arrange itself into the cadence of a count — *in, four; hold, four* — and he got up and left with the universal two-finger-to-chest mime of campus gastric distress, which the lecturer acknowledged with the universal nod of a man paid the same either way.

Veer caught him by the water-cooler. Caught is not the right word; Veer was simply *there*, folded small on the cooler's concrete plinth like a temple guardian trying to sit at a desk, waiting with two steel tumblers of Nimbu-pani, one of which he extended without looking up.

"Pee le."

"Vitamins? Iss umr mein?"

"Pee le, gadhe." Veer took a long swallow from his own. His forearm dressing had been changed; the gauze was hospital-white now, professional, no longer the torn kurta-strip of Manikarnika night. "Kal raat ke baad aaj sirf teen baar maara hai kisi ne mujhe mazaak mein 'hero'. Teen. Itni publicity ke baad college apna dharm bhool raha hai." He watched a first-year spill water, did not help, did not laugh, continued: "Ira ka matlab hai — jo usne kaha tha — weeks. Tu usko lekar kya kar raha hai?"

"Push-ups."

"*Rudra.*"

"Ira ka math aur mere seene ka hisaab," Rudra said, dead-level, "dono apni jagah perfect hain. Par dono ko yeh nahin pata ki jab jo Cheez andar hai woh kholne wali hoti hai, toh jo uske saath rakhta hai woh bhi khulta hai." He drained the tumbler. Set it down. "Mujhe us din ka aadmi nahi banana jis din sab khaata hai. Baaki sab normal rakhna hai. Samjha?"

Veer looked at him for a long count whose length Rudra measured without meaning to — *in, four; hold, four* — then clapped him once, softly, high on the back, the spot two men use in lieu of the conversation, and said, "Chal. Warden ko bola tha teri kharab tabiyat ka invent karta hoon — attendance bacha dega, drama karwa dega." And he went, shoulders filling the corridor frame, and if Rudra noticed that his oldest friend had not once met his eyes during the whole exchange — had looked at the cooler, the floor, the future — he filed it, as he filed everything unbearable, precisely, and away.

Near the library turn, three corridors later, he saw Aadhya.

She was coming out of the periodicals room with a stack of last-year bound journals held against her diaphragm — academic armour — moving pale and straight through the light-shaft, and there was, unmistakable even at thirty feet, the shadow of bruising at the edge of one nostril, faint as thumb-smudge. She took in him; he took in her. Data was exchanged in some half-second protocols of recognition: *you too; yes, me too; say nothing; okay.*

What walked toward him instead of Aadhya was the small crop of her face turning aside first, in a direction that would pass him two strides away.

"Aadhya—" he said.

"Not today," she said, not harshly — gently even, with the tired courtesy of someone detoxing an afternoon — and went past him smelling faintly, unbelievably, like wet humus and camphor, and put down her head and disappeared into the reading-room door's dim geometry, and he stood in the empty knowing and felt, absurdly, that he had been refused entry into a shrine whose sanctum he's spent three days desecrating.

On the Sanskrit wing side, as he cut through for the gate, the banyan's ruin stood behind two new bamboo-poles strung fatally with red cloth — the municipal world's entire arsenal against the inexplicable: a string barrier and a faded notice, *PRUNING SCHEDULED — DO NOT APPROACH — BY ORDER, GARDENS DEPT.* The crows were gone from it entirely. The grey had climbed to its crown's underside like banked water.

Rudra walked the last stretch home under evening skies gone the colour of wet dhoop-ash, and counted — in, four; hold, four — all the way to his gate.

---

Dinner that night tasted of ordinary things and of three silences that didn't touch.

Rudra came in late — near dark, knuckles rewound in their boiled cloth, jaw-bruise yellowing at its edges like old mango. He dropped onto the dining bench with the theatrical collapse of a nineteenth-year-old trying to impersonate exhaustion and landing, without wanting to, on the real thing. His mother set the thali down. His father sat at the other end with the old FM transistor mumbling devotional turns from the corner high shelf — *vaghera, vaghera, gyarah zakhmi, jaanch jaari* — the eight-o'clock voice pressed down flat beneath the bhajan reprise like a ballast stone under a rug.

"Ghat gaya tha kal?" Ram asked, not looking up from his roti.

Rudra reached for the dal. "Veer ke saath the," he said, and the sentence was so perfectly rehearsed in its timing — bite first, answer second, eyes to brass-plate, casual, exact — that it might have been a drill his father had taught him: *lie as stance, part four.*

Ram said nothing, and broke his roti.

The transistor said *—afwah mat failaiye—* and went back to the bhajan, and Rudra's mother set the katori down and watched her family not talk, her hands folded on her thighs, knuckles unbleached for once, ten quiet joints that had held a universe together at dawn and were, for these forty minutes, allowed to be a housewife.

"Ammu says the corridor light is flickering," Rudra said, after a while, to the silence. "Said to tell Appa."

"Mm," said Ram.

"That's the entire message," Rudra said. "Whole thing. Very informative. Zero lightbulbs issued." He chewed. The bruise made the chewing uneven. Forty seconds passed with the bhajan. "Can I have a lightbulb? The corridor at night looks like—" he hunted for the reference and settled, tiredly, on the truth, "—like a corridor at night, Ma. It flickers and I have to watch it flicker. It's a hazard."

"Ungliyan," Ram said.

"What?"

"Tumhari ungliyan," said Ram, still not looking up. "Split. Dobara. Kal subah re-wrap." And he folded down a cartridge of dal and continued eating, as if the entire ghat-shaped thing the son hadn't said had been fully, courteously acknowledged and filed.

Rudra stared at the top of his father's head and felt, not for the first time, the dreadful inverted vertigo of being this man's son: that every word between them had its whole underwater continent, and they swam on the surface talking about corridor lights.

"Achha," he said, and let it go. Too tired to push tonight. The seal-heat was a fine low banked coal under everything, tonight, patient and warm, as if it had decided to observe a rest day — *waiting*, some traitorous lower floor of his mind observed, *like a client.*

Rukmini watched him finish only half the dal and then the roti — and he had always finished all the dal, had done so since the age of nine, the boy was *famous* in three families for it — and her hand went out to his forehead before either of them expected it, flat and cool, past necessity, counting.

"Bukhaar nahin hai," she announced — theater, for the room.

"Ma—"

"Sar mein dard?"

"Nahin, Ma, I'm—" He leaned out of the flat of her hand, gently, the way you'd leave a temple. "Tired hai. College."

She didn't answer that. At the doorway she turned — silhouette with the corridor's flickering light behind her stripping her to edges.

"So ja jaldi," she said. "Main baad mein aati hoon."

He didn't ask what that meant. Some part of him had already begun to drift, the body's dumb untrainable wisdom lowering him toward the floor of the day.

He was on the edge of it — the first black terrace of sleep — when he registered, distantly, with the back of his attention, the small domestic cricket-noise of a chair being dragged in the corridor below his room, the charpai-creak of his father's weight going up it, the old dust-ritic bulb unscrewed, the new one — from where, in this house, at this hour? from some drawer of foresight his father kept stocked like spare spearheads — going home into the socket with a slow thread-whisper.

The corridor light came on steady.

Steady. First time in a month, his mother had told the neighbours, two Sundays ago, over their wall. Rudra lay in the blue dark with his eyes half-open, the gauze of his wound never tighter than now, the coal breathing its four-count breath, and the clean yellow line of that stable light under his door was, in a way no one in this family could have explained on paper, the first unbroken sentence anybody had said to him all day.

*Father speaks only with his hands.* It occurred to him, going under: *the trembling means something is wrong with the light inside him; so tonight he fixed the bulb.*

Half a dream rose to meet that thought and carried it away — the way the lane-sweeper carried before dawn what the city had meant to learn by heart — and the note of somebody's naval song, far off and eleven-noted and not Hindi, folded the whole boy's nineteenth year gently around its key.

---

By ten the haveli was dark except the kitchen, where a single diya on the shelf below the wedding photograph made the brass shine like a row of patient moons.

Rukmini heated the milk slowly, because haste in milk-making is a crime against tomorrow's tea, and because her hands needed a liturgy that belonged to nobody's history but this household's: saucepan, cardamom, the brief agitation as it threatened to rise. She was aware — more aware than she was comfortable ever being aware — of the melody pressing against the underside of her attention like fingers under a door.

*Don't.*

*He's nineteen. Sleeping badly hasn't happened since—*

—since never. Rudra did not sleep badly. That was the cruel joke of the entire machine Ram had built of his son: the body had been honed so fine that recovery was automatic, that sleep came down on him like a curtain at the same nightly minute, that his father had commissioned every night of boyhood against nightmare. And here he was: two nights in three, scraped awake at three, four in the morning, the coal under his chest lit to a low blue-white point —

— and here it was, pulsing at her attention like a knock from the basement, a rhythm she knew better than *Om*: the seal was doing its nightly thing. Expanding a knot, letting the brief tide through, resting again. Holding. Diminutively fraying at its edges, yes — but holding. She'd been repairing its edges at dawn the way street-sweepers repair the city: invisibly, assumed-permanently, no one watching, garbage of the gods quietly carted off before the saints wake up —

*Don't,* she thought again — and found, in her own mind, no further argument the boy's face hadn't already rebutted.

She let the first two notes out over the saucepan.

The milk shivered.

---

Upstairs, in the dark, Rudra lay on his back watching the ceiling-fan describe responsibilities he had never agreed to.

The lane gate had settled; the fm was off; Appa was on the roof — always, lately — doing whatever thing he did there with the northeast sky. Somewhere in the wall a gecko clocked. The coal behind his sternum pulsed in four-count intervals. *In, four. Hold, four. Out, four. Hold, four.* His body had begun running the counts without him again, like an auto running on the meter of a passenger who'd fallen asleep against the window.

Ma climbed the stairs with the milk — he heard the saucepan's small musical clink on the steel tumbler lid. The corridor light over his doorway flicked on one dying instant, blossomed, and quit. Then she was in the room, not saying anything, putting the tumbler on his table, pushing aside the calc-notes with the side of her hand and her free hand found his temple. The contact sank through him like a first rain into last year's soil.

"Peelo."

"Ma, I'm not six."

"Peelo." She smiled at his chin. "Drink it and shut the eyes. Aaj bahut lamba din tha."

He drank it. She sat on the bed's edge. The corridor-flicker apart from them now, the room was blue and still; even the fan had decelerated into sympathy. Tomorrow-and-tomorrow college; his knuckles to re-wrap in the dawn; father's questions on the dining table wearing wifi-password faces; Ira's estimates inside his skull: *weeks, possibly less*—

Something warm flowed through his hair. For the first several seconds he mistook it for his mother's hand moving there. It was not her hand. She was sitting entirely still.

She was humming.

It was so faint he caught only the bottom of it — felt it more than heard it, her chest making a second slower reservoir beside him that the hum rose from in thin dark threads. The melody — he surfaced, blinked once in the dark, listened properly —

— he knew music. Everyone in this city knew music; the ghats were flooded daily in it; morning-singers with tanpuras sat on temple steps tuning their throats into the river-noise; old men with transistor-stutters held whole worlds together with an alap. He knew music, was telling himself two bars in — and he didn't know *this*. The intervals rose and fell in widening and narrowing steps that slid off the grid of any sargam. It wasn't a raga. It wasn't any filmi thing his mother's kitchen whistled during festival cooking — bhajan, bhojpuri, monsoon-oldies. It didn't resolve — and he had been raised since one on the premise of resolution, breath in and breath out; *the interval was not trying to resolve*. It opened. It described something further out than the room. Further out than the rooftops, the streetlamp haze, the ghats' perfumed smoke. The melody travelled an eleven-note arc, turned over in a *seven*-count breath — seven pulses, seven pulses, a cadence that didn't jog so much as *walk*, patiently, through somewhere —

He stopped being able to think in sentences. A part of his attention, far below, was walking the inside perimeter of his chest and smoothing something — the coal. *It was singing the coal down.* The ember under his sternum — the low hum that had slept there at varying volumes since some unremembered year — was, for the first time since the ghat, *settling*, banking down, coiling like a dog allowed at last to sleep after three days standing.

The melody kept going, and each place it went, an image opened in his dimming mind like a hand unfolding.

The third interval: a courtyard much larger than theirs, with no walls, only mist for boundaries, and a woman — not his mother — standing very far away in it with something held against her chest that was too bright to see. The seventh pulse: water, black and endless, going under a stone; a sense, without drama, of someone whispering the way you whisper into a sleeping child's ear: *remember only this, only this.* The eleventh — the door-note, the one that opened — sunrise over some plain littered with small cool cooking-fires no bigger than listenings, smoke going up straight, *unbent*; and behind the smoke, the horizon doing something skies should not be able to do, being *more than one horizon at once*, a fan of silver dawns laid over each other —

He thought, crossing down through the last fathoms before sleep: *someone once stood in a place like that and sang like this and promised to come back.*

He could not have said who he meant. The thought was in him and not of him, like the working of his own heart.

Somewhere in the deep of him, rudderless nonsense rose, wearing his own voice but not his aliveness.

"Ye kya ga rahi ho, Ma?"

A long moment, in which the melody went on and gentler, and his mother's hand resumed as if nothing had happened, padding his hair in small slow tides.

"Old thing," she said eventually — soft, casual, a lie performed by a genius with the single weakness of lying only about this. "Purani si cheez. Mere ghar mein sabko aati thi."

"Words kyun nahin hain?"

"Words honge," Rukmini said. A bigger wave of pressure passed through his temple and left less behind. "Lekin is = not for your ears. Not yet."

*Not yet.* The two soft words towered enormously over the rest of the sentence, a gate too large for its lane. He wanted to ask. Instead what came out — and he could feel the melody coaxing it up out of him with a midwife's patience, and felt mildly outraged about it from a great distance —

"Ma."

"Mm?"

"Galat kya hai? Mere saath." Slurring now at the edges — a boy in the dark of his own bed with his mother's lap-warmth levelling the day into a flat place he could finally lie down on. "Appa behaves like a countdown, aur tum aise dekh rahi ho jaise kuch hold kar rahi ho. Kya chhupa rahe ho. Kal ghat pe—"

The melody faltered. *Once*. One note sat down hard, half a shruti off its path, before she lifted it gently back onto the quiet rails.

That stumble said everything his question had asked for.

He understood — still drifting, fingertips of sleep already up his wrists — that he had not made it easier by asking. He had made it visible. He lay feeling the melody resume stitch by stitch over his believing-heart, felt the dark warm pool of it widening at his elbows, and heard his mother lower her head very close to his — lips almost to the ear, breath moving the hair that the bluntness of his kurta collar made wiry — and say, in a voice that had forgotten all theatre, that was the smallest true voice he had ever heard her use, so low that even the walls, lifelong sworn to her, missed half of it:

"There are people who look for children like you, beta."

He blinked into the dark.

"Promise me," she said, and the melody held one long, open, door-like note under the sentence, "you'll never let strangers see what you can do."

Rudra stared at the ceiling fan. For a moment the fan and its pundit's blurred wheel spun above him in the dark of the room like a question-mark putting itself out.

"Ma," he said gently, in the tone with which you remind a parent the world is ordinary. "Main kya kar sakta hoon?"

The very limits of his nineteen years were in the sentence — the whole unconscious, grand, pathetic proposition of his life: *what can I do; I can do push-ups. I can hold stance until breakfast. I can win illegal lane-fights and get see-eight-weeks of "training." What can I do? I'm nobody's whatever. I'm the guy who loses balance at the third step.*

And Rukmini — nineteen years of holding knots inside her own two hands, eyes closed at dawn, subjecting creation's most volatile heirloom to a mason's patience — looked down at her son with an expression that would have broken the heart of any deity who had ever asked her to be braver than this, and drew her thumb once across the boy's forehead, warmly, and said:

"Sab kuch, beta. You can do anything."

And then, stilled and sustained by the last note, she finished singing him under.

---

She remained with her palm on the warm brow past sleep. She remained listening, temple-trained, to the deep inner tide of him, confirming knot by knot — *the seal had taken tonight's reinforcement beautifully, intake down to safe occlusions:* eleven closed, eight thinning, three translucent. Seconds of gratitude from a mason-mother. One thread let itself be eased, another was purpose-set for the dawn work—

At the bedroom door a hand knocked, quietly, once. Ram's silhouette was there as everywhere these nights: fully dressed, on his way up to the roof, pausing at the edge of his son's darkness like a pilgrim at a ghat who cannot decide whether he is allowed to touch the river.

Rukmini looked at the silhouette. The silhouette looked at the bed. From within the dark of her son's room Rukmini's expression was not readable — except that her hand had stilled.

"Gaa rahi thi," said Ram, finally. It was not a question. It carried the certainty of a man who has been *listening to the house* for twenty years and knows the exact second its gravity changes. He could not have heard a thing; the hum had been threaded so fine. And still he knew.

"Gaana bandh kiya," said Rukmini. Her voice was as even and as soft as the first thirty years of evenings. "So gaya."

Ram said nothing for a while. The moon washed a thin milk of light into the corridor and he was, for one moment, only a young man standing uncertainly at a woman's door. 

"Ram."

"Mm."

"Bahut kam samay bacha hai."

For a long moment the man who had never left the house without locking every lock twice simply stood there — big, tightened, watchful, beginning to tremble in the deeper furniture of his body where no trembling showed, the coal-fed furnace of an unbroken oath glowing dully through his ribs. Then he stepped backward out of the moonlight and the silhouette dissolved, and the rooftop took him like a loaded bowstring accepts its arrow-back.

Downstairs, on the counter, the kitchen-diya's flame leaned sideways for one breath — leaned, as if listening — and righted itself.

---

And Rudra did not dream, for the first time in three nights.

The training-ground fog of his sleep unfolded, and there were no broken stones in it; the courtyard was enormous, rose-lit, the neem-roots holding the whole house like a lap, and in the middle of it — far away, small as a child's hand — a circle of seven burning lamps stood around an eighth unlit one, and the eighth one filled with light slowly, like a cup.

In the dream someone was singing. Eleven intervals. The missing word of the melody, rising now just out of hearing — a word his sleeping body knew so intimately that the mystery of it did not trouble him; it had, after all, been pressed on him with both hands when he was three days old.

He slept on. Below the sill, the street's one streetlamp burned; the lane-dogs' night-check passed; the next door's lichened wall shone faint; and on the facing roof a small pink-faced langur crouched with its child and watched the house as monkeys watch a fruiting tree.

Far below, far below the pucca whiteness of Lanka, the bore in the dark took one second of unlatched attention and sniffed, up at the rim of everything, along the underside of the singing — and then lost the thread of it, exactly at the point where the seventh breath paused — and went on dimming the town.
