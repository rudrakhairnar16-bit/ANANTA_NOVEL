# CHAPTER 4 — THE BURNING GHAT

They did not go home.

The four of them stood in the narrow lane with the dying light pooling around them like something spilled, arguing in the low, urgent voices of people who are aware that the next decision is the one that cannot be taken back. Ira's notebook lay open across her palm, the hand-drawn map of the Pancha Koshi route catching the last of the sun, its red lines glowing faintly in the amber light like threads of cooling iron.

"Manikarnika," Veer said again, as if repetition might make the word less terrible. "You want us to go and stand on the most crowded cremation ghat in the country — during evening aarti — because your map says the ground is hungry."

"The map doesn't say anything," Ira replied, turning the page with her thumb. "The measurements say something. The map is just where I put them."

"Same thing."

"It is not remotely the same—"

"Guys." Rudra's voice was quiet, the way a dropped coin is quiet. Both of them stopped. He was not looking at either of them. He was looking at the map — specifically at the black lines, the redrawn current, the new convergence dragged two hundred metres deeper beneath the ghat. His wrapped knuckles tightened and loosened once, a small hydraulic motion, like a man checking the pressure in his own hand. "Wednesday," he said. "The last shift. What day was it?"

Ira glanced down. "Nine days ago."

"And the one before that?"

"Twelve days before that. It's accelerating. Roughly a geometric progression." She said it as flatly as a weather report. "The next shift, if the pattern holds—"

"Is tonight," Rudra finished.

For a moment nobody spoke. A scooter whined past the mouth of the lane, trailing a ribbon of two-stroke fumes. Somewhere above them, a woman shaken out a bedsheet over a balcony let it fall, and the cotton snapped like a distant flag.

Aadhya watched Rudra's face. She had not been part of this conversation yesterday. Yesterday she had been a girl who touched a dying tree in the morning and argued with a boy over breakfast. Now she was standing in a lane that smelled of marigolds and sewage, next to a boy whose childhood, she had learned three hours ago, had been dismantled and rebuilt as a weapon, and asking which of them got to stand closest to whatever the ground was planning to bring up.

"How do we even get close?" she asked. "Manikarnika is—" She searched for the right word and found only the truth. "Sacred. Crowded. And we are four college students with a notebook."

"Manikarnika also has rooftops," Ira said.

She said it without looking up, already folding the map shut, already turning downhill toward the river, as if the matter had been settled before anyone agreed to anything. Veer looked at Rudra. Rudra looked at the sky, where the first bats were beginning to make their jerky ink-strokes above the temple spires, and then back at the retreating small figure with the leather notebook.

"She's going without us," Veer said.

"She always goes without us. We just usually don't know where she's going."

"And this time?"

Rudra set off after Ira, shoulders rolling once to settle the left one that still ached from the morning's session in the courtyard. "This time," he said over his shoulder, not unkindly, "the map's about me."

---

The rooftop belonged to a four-storey building so old it seemed to lean against the ghats out of tiredness, its balustrade worn smooth by generations of elbows, paan-stains and monsoons. A spiralling iron staircase had got them up. The chai-wallah on the ground floor — an acquaintance of Ira's, it emerged, though 'acquaintance' with Ira was a category no one fully understood — had charged them nothing and only muttered that they should not throw anything from the top.

From the rooftop's eastern edge, the whole shallow curve of the riverfront lay open below like the inside of a cupped hand.

There are photographs of Varanasi in every embassy waiting-room on earth, and every one of them is a lie of omission, because no camera has ever captured the sound. The bells did not cease. Below, at Dashashwamedh, the evening aarti had just begun — a rising tide of brass and flame, hundreds of lamps turning together on their stands, the chants rolling out over the water in waves that struck the far bank and came back softened. Pilgrims crowded the steps in their thousands. The river took the light of the lamps and broke it into a million pieces, each one trembling, each one briefly gold.

And north of all that colour — separated from it by a stretch of dark steps and a few hundred metres, as though joy and duty had agreed to keep a respectful distance — Manikarnika burned.

The pyres never stopped here. Four, five, sometimes eight at a time on the platforms, tended by men whose families had fed the same fire for longer than the Raj, longer than the Company, longer than most dynasties anyone could name. The Dom Raja's eternal flame, housed in its shrine above the ghat, had not gone out in living memory or in the memory of anyone's grandfathers' grandfathers, and every pyre below was lit from it: one continuous thread of fire handed down, body after body, through the centuries like a whispered sentence no one dared to finish. Smoke went up from the platforms in slow grey pillars. Bodies wrapped in white and saffron came down through the lanes on bamboo biers, the bearers chanting — *Ram naam satya hai*, God's name is the only truth — a chant so woven into the fabric of the city that the pigeons scarcely rose at its approach.

Rudra stood at the railing and watched the dead come down to the river, and thought about nothing. He was good at that. His father had never taught it as a lesson, but it had come free with all the others: how to watch a thing without it watching you back.

"The smoke," Aadhya said quietly, beside him.

He looked. He had already been looking, in fact; it was only that hearing it said aloud made it real. "Bending," he said.

"The wind's from the west. Off the rooftops. All the aarti smoke down there blows east, over the water. But the pyre smoke—"

"—goes straight up," Veer finished, frowning. He had not taken his eyes off the platforms since they arrived, shoulders planted wide against the balustrade as though daring the skyline to misbehave. "Straight up. No drift at all. Could be a thermal column."

"Hot air does rise," Ira said. It did not sound like agreement.

Nobody answered. The aarti below swelled to its first crescendo, hundreds of diyas turning in their great slow circles, and down at Manikarnika a fresh bier was being laid upon the southernmost pyre platform — white cotton, saffron silk, marigolds dense enough to dim the shape beneath. A family stood about the platform in the particular posture of the recently bereft: bent slightly forward, as though grief were a physical weight pressing on their shoulders. The son was eldest, by the way he held the clay water pot. Perhaps twenty-five. His face had the stunned smoothness of a man walking through a dream.

Ira's pencil moved across the notebook in short, precise strokes. She was sketching the ghat — the platforms, the steps, the crooked dogleg gully that ran between them where a drainage channel should have been — and overlaying it with lines only she could see.

"It's here," she said.

"The shift?" Aadhya asked.

"Starting now." Ira turned her wrist, palm upward, toward the city's web of lit ghats, as though testing for rain. "The whole network is holding its breath."

"Cities don't breathe, Ira."

"No," said Ira calmly. "But energy flows. And right now, nothing is flowing." She looked up, and for the first time that evening, something moved behind the enormous stillness of her eyes that was not calm at all. "Everything is being *held*. Like a cup being filled. The question is — at what point do you stop filling a cup?"

"You stop when it's full," Veer said.

"Yes," said Ira. "Or the cup breaks."

Below them, the Dom attendants circled the new pyre with the first brands of fire, and the aarti bells downriver climbed toward their second crescendo.

Aadhya's hands, resting on the railing, had gone cold.

She knew this cold. She had met it that very morning, standing at the base of a dying tree, feeling it leak from the earth into her bones. It was the cold of absence engineered — not winter, not shade, but the temperature of something being *subtracted*. It moved up her wrists now, patient as floodwater, and she looked down at her own fingers in the falling dark and thought, with terror's strange clarity, *I should not actually be able to feel this* — and then the part of her that lived nineteen floors below her thoughts stirred, leaned toward the pyre platforms, and whispered:

*It is feeding.*

---

The son lit the pyre.

The fire took the sandalwood cleanly at first — a blue-shivered ripple of flame running through the oil-soaked kindling, then rising into the honest orange that fire wears when it is doing its oldest work. The attendant stepped back. The chant rose. The son circled the platform once with the clay pot dripping, and broke it, and for a moment the scene on the southern platform was every funeral that had ever been there: fire, smoke, chant, windless evening, a family letting go.

And then the flames lay down.

Not diminished. *Lay down.* Every tongue of fire on the pyre flattened in the same instant, like long grass under a helicopter, like a wheat-field bowing — pressed horizontally outward against the platform stones as if the air above the body had suddenly become too heavy to pass through. The chant faltered. One of the men frowned and took a step forward—

—and the fire crawled back *inward*.

It did not rise. It drained. Every visible flicker pulled itself across the fagots toward the silent shape at the pyre's centre and — *into* it, the way water threads into soil, the way colour runs from a wet painting. One long breathless second of impossible thermodynamics, and the platform stood bare of flame altogether, smoking, black, every stick of sandalwood stripped of heat as though winter had fallen on that one square of stone and nowhere else in Uttar Pradesh.

"Kya—" said someone on the steps.

The body sat up.

The white cotton fell away in pieces. Beneath it, the dead man was grey — ash-grey, clay-grey, the matte grey of last week's coals — and the lightless grey webbed across him in branching filaments, sub-dermal, like a gutter-map drawn underneath the skin. The head came up too evenly, too smoothly, with none of the jerky middle-tension of living muscle — the way a puppet's head rises when the right wire is pulled, a motion without intention, without history.

And then the filaments turned green-black, and something passed *through* them from beneath.

Afterward — and this was the part the newspapers never got, the part eleven different eyewitnesses would state in eleven identical trembling statements and no official report would ever contain — afterward, the arriving mourners on the upper steps said that it made no sound. The son said, quietly, forever altered, that it did. He said it sounded like a garden at midnight heard through a wall — a slow, coarse, rustling congestion of roots working in soil — and that as it slid its legs off the platform and stood, marigold petals still falling off its shoulders, the dead man's face turned toward the aarti lamps at Dashashwamedh with a kind of terrible, mechanical *yearning* —

— the way the banyan's roots, that morning, had yearned toward anything in the soil that was still alive.

Then the screaming started, and the ghat did what crowds do.

---

What happens next always happens faster than anyone can narrate while it is happening, and slower than anyone can bear while watching.

Three thousand people on a flight of stone steps decide, in the same half-second, to be anywhere else. The crowd did not move; it *avalanched*. The aarti singers broke off mid-verse. A lamp-stand went over and kerosene ran in a bright stone-level rivulet that ignited with a soft *whump* and cut the steps in two. A fat oil drum rolled to the platform edge and stopped. Down on the burning platforms the Dom attendants — men afraid of no ordinary thing that walked in smoke — scrambled back with brands held waist-high like spears, and their screams were wordless and atavistic and identical.

On the rooftop, Veer was already running for the stairs.

"*Ve—*" Ira started.

"Old man! Bottom right! Steps!" — and he was gone, the iron staircase jangling under him like a struck instrument.

Aadhya saw the old man immediately after Veer named him: white-haired, bent, blown against a railing bare metres from the burning rivulet, one sandal gone, facing the avalanche alone. Veer took the final flight in three strides and got his body in between — took the first impact of the crowd on his shoulder and back like a man breaking a door, planted his arms against the railings on either side of the old man, and *held the river back*. People hit him and went around, the way water divides on a reef. His forearm, braced against the hot rail, began to redden. He did not move.

An animal part of Aadhya registered that this was the textbook definition of a protector, and the conscious part was already past it, already gripping her own wrist on the cold railing where the cold was rising toward her elbows, already—

"Rudra."

Ira's voice, absolutely level, cut through everything. Aadhya turned.

Rudra was standing very still at the rooftop railing. He was not watching the crowd or Veer or the fire. He was watching the grey figure, which had stopped, pyre-marl still smoking on its shoulders, at the foot of the Manikarnika steps. Its head was angled up at their building — angles absent from the repertoire of necks, head nearly parallel to its own spine, filaments brightening faintly, veins lit like the nave of some inverted church.

It was looking at the four of them.

"It can see—" Aadhya started.

"No," Ira said softly. Her eyes were enormous and fixed. "It can't see at all. It's *smelling*." A fractional pause. "And it hasn't come up the steps for us. It's smelling something *specific*."

The grey thing turned its broken-angled head, slowly, scanning along the rooftop with some organ that had nothing to do with eyes, and stopped.

Facing Rudra.

"It smells the seal," Ira said.

Rudra's chest ignited.

It was not pain, exactly. Pain had location, and locality, and by nineteen years of training he knew its whole geography. This was deeper and possessed no address. It was *heat*, first — a landing coal dropped behind his sternum, so sudden that his breath truncated to a bark. Then the heat geometry'd: he *felt* the pattern, the interlocking circuit of lines that had lain across his chest since before his first memory, felt each line go hot in sequence like elements of a stove waking up, ring by ring. The kavach did not crack. It *braced*. He felt it lean inward against something pushing outward from inside his own body.

A match behind a curtain. A sunrise behind a door jamb.

His vision throbbed, gold at the edges.

*In, four. Hold, four. Out, four. Hold, four.*

The breath-rhythm his father had painted into his nervous system before the alphabet arrived now, unbidden, unthought. The pressure slackened a notch. The coal at his sternum dimmed from white to orange. Rudra realized his hand had crawled up his own chest and clamped flat over the kurta fabric, over the seal his mother had set when he was hours old, as if a child's palm could keep the lid on a furnace — and he also realized, distantly, with the cool floating clarity that was his only inheritance he'd never resented, that *the shape below had not moved*.

The grey man was standing still.

Head tilted. Filaments pulsing in slow, fascinated waves.

Like a hound that had found the door under the wind.

"Rudra?" Aadhya's voice, close. She had seen the hand. She had not seen what was under the hand. Her eyes were wide and dark, and for one odd lucid beat amid the roar of a panicking city, all he could think was: *she has stopped being afraid for herself and is now afraid for me, and I have not earned either.*

"Baith ja," Rudra said. His voice sounded like someone else's, low and flattened. "Sit. Behind the pillar. Go. *Now.*"

"Rudra—"

"That's the boy who gets hurt when people argue with him," he said, and stepped past her, *out from behind the balustrade, onto the staircase, down toward the steps and the fire* —

— because if the seal's heat was drawing it, the arithmetic was simple. Take the bait off the rooftop. His friends, two bystanders and three hundred strangers: all behind him, all in the blast radius if the thing came up. Down below, the steps were emptying, the crowd pouring laterally toward the adjacent ghats. The burning rivulet was guttering. Veer had the old man half-shoulder-carried toward the dockside. There would be a corridor of emptiness, probably ninety seconds of it, before the city organized a response—

And the grey man came up the steps to meet him.

Fast. No patter, no rhythm — a dead thing *disposed* of distance, whole metres simply omitted between one eyeblink and the next. Rudra's hindbrain screamed *it's at the fourth step* when he had only just decided where his feet would go; his training filed the scream under *later* and noted dispassionately that the end of the drainage gully a body-length to his right still had standing water in it, that the step-stone by the third platform was loose, that the only worthwhile weapon in reach was a snapped bamboo bier-pole lying charred at the burning platform's edge.

He got the pole and his back to solid stone in the same motion that he got his first good *look* at it.

Not a man. Nowhere near now. The filaments were thick as twine across its cheeks and it wore the fire's black filigree all down its torso. Its mouth was open in the manner of a dropped drawer. There was no speech in it, no request, nothing that wanted anything on a human scale — only a locomotive appetite hung on a stolen armature.

It was close enough now that he could see the colony squirming in the cavity of its chest — the part that was not the corpse, the parasite — a dark compacted *density* there, mass grown inward from every filament like ivy meeting at the centre of a wall.

*A node,* said the dispassionate, voiceless briefing his father ran behind his eyes. *Everything is feeding something else. Find what it's feeding.*

It lunged — and Rudra stepped offline exactly as the courtyard's forty daily takedowns had taught him, pivoted his hip past its grasp, and drove the bamboo butt down into the back of its knee with all eighteen years he owned. Living bone would have buckled. The grey leg made a sound like a wet branch breaking — and *swung back* without pause toward his head.

He dipped under it and felt it pass through the air his skull had just vacated.

*Okay. Slower than me. Stronger than me. Doesn't stop. Doesn't hurt.*

He put the pole's end through its solar plexus with a thrust that would have stopped a bull and shoved it back two full steps onto the loose stone by the drainage gully. The grey feet skidded in the water. Its arms cartwheeled once with the ghastly ease of a marionette; it regained its balance instantly — of course it did — and as it straightened, the green-black light in its filaments climbed from ember to furnace, and the heat-sheared chest of Rudra responded as though someone had drawn a blade across the seal's surface.

The kavach *sang*.

A line of fire crossed his collarbone.

Something at the base of Rudra's skull swelled — the pressure announced itself behind his eyes like water rising against a lock gate. For a quarter-second his sight went to reddened glass, and through it he saw the grey man not as horror but as *structure*: filaments, trunk-lines, all of it converging — at the thing's feet — into a single taut *thread* of stony fluorescent dark that ran down between the step-stones, through the steps, *through the rock*, going away from here, going *down*, going somewhere childishly simple like a fish-fin going home—

—and on impulse beyond his own education, Rudra did the one thing the training never covered.

He stepped in, seized that filament-line *in his vision* with the strange magnetic hand his father's drills had never meant to build — the hand that existed only behind his ribs, under the seal — and *pulled*.

The severing was instant and almost noiseless, a cable snipped between sleepers. The thread sprang upward out of the stone like a cut guy-rope, lashing, and the colony in the grey man's chest went dark so fast the ember-light simply dropped away mid-motion like a switched-off diorama.

The grey thing did not fall.

Rudra dropped it — clubbed the pole's shaft once, flat and terminal, across its temple — and it toppled sideways over the loose stone into the drainage water and lay as still as all its centuries deserved.

The largest filament, after a final twitch, shrivelled to grey ash and then to nothing, and then there was just a body on stone and the fire crawling back to orange in the abandoned platform's kindling as if embarrassed to have been seen misbehaving.

Rudra stood with the pole in both fists, breathing like a ruptured engine, chest burning, eyes red-hot-cooling, and heard the last clang of the aarti bells drifting over from Dashashwamedh — finishing, oblivious, on time.

Below the ghat, in the dark between the platforms, the severed thread's cut end hung for one instant trembling above the stones like a wire in wind —

— and was gone, whipped downward, reeled in through the rock, recalled by whatever immense and patient thing lay on the other end of the line.

Ira arrived beside him first, small, noiseless, notebook already open and pencil writing itself across the paper. She was not looking at the corpse. She was looking at the crack between the stones, inclining her head as if measuring the angle of a vanished ray.

"Not a body-snatching," she said, half to the page. "A *line*. There was a line in the ground. He was—" she sought the cold correct word and with evident distaste selected "— bait."

"Bait," Rudra repeated, hoarsely.

"Or an instrument. A lure." She looked up at him then, eyes unblinking and black in the firelight. "It ran on siphoned energy until the supply was cut. Siphoned by what — is not the question tonight can answer." A fraction's pause. "The seal held."

"The seal—" Rudra got one breath into the sentence and coughed, and could not finish it. He pressed his wrist instead to the coal behind his sternum. The heat was receding in slow rings, ring by ring, exactly as it had come. The gold was departing from the edges of his vision like a tide. Under his collar — he tugged the fabric aside an inch and glanced down, once — the fine geometry of the kavach was visible under the skin for one second, ember-orange, and then fading, intact, unbroken.

But thinner. He could feel the thinness like a courtesy lie.

"—is cracking faster," he finished, quietly.

Ira nodded once, matter-of-fact as weather. "The calculations assumed it would only be tested from outside." She looked at the wet stain where the thread had vanished. "Being pulled from *within* is not the same loading."

"How long." His voice leveled out like poured concrete. "Ira. Weeks. How long."

She did not answer at once, which was an answer.

"Three," she said finally. "Possibly four."

Veer came up the steps with the old man still on his shoulder and blood drying at his forearm where the rail had burned him, took one look at the ash-rimed stones and the pole in Rudra's hands and the way Rudra stood — guard up even here, even now, alone with them, among friends — and something settled into his face that had not been there at breakfast, some door in him closing on the age of ordinary evenings.

Veer said nothing at all. He just passed the old man gently to the arriving, weeping priest, set his wide back between Rudra and the newcomers flooding the ghat, and said over his shoulder, flat as an order: "Ghat paanch minute mein police se ghir jayega. Chalo."

Aadhya had not moved from the pillar. She had watched all of it — the pivot, the bamboo, the thing that could not be stopped until something invisible was cut that she could not quite bring herself to remember clearly, the way memory refused bright events the way eyes refused a welding arc. What she saw, as Rudra turned, was not a warrior. It was a boy of nineteen with bleeding knuckles pressing a palm to his own chest like a mourner at his own pyre.

She touched, without thinking, the dead marigold garland looped over the pillar's corner post.

One crumpled orange bloom uncurled, slowly, under the shadow of her fingers, and straightened toward the evening's warmth.

She pulled her hand back as if burned.

On the rooftop behind them all, forgotten, Ira's open notebook lifted one page in the new wind, and the hand-drawn convergence map — the deep black lines pulled down toward Manikarnika — fluttered like a sail.

Far off down the river, the smoke from the burning ghats continued to move against physics, rising sheer and unbending into the dark, like a thread that had been fished down out of the sky and very slowly, over days, over years, drawn taut.

---

Five kilometres inland, in the courtyard of a crumbling haveli, Ram stood among clay lamps and neem leaves with a tin cup of tea gone cold in his fist, facing north-northeast toward the river in the angle of stone the window of his body happened to afford him.

He had been doing the slow breathing, the nine-count kind reserved for fixing what was old and pain-worn in his joints. Halfway through the third round, he had stopped. Not chosen to stop. The discipline had simply ceased, the way a song ceases when the singer is seized by the throat: the fourth count of the third round had *not come*.

In through the nose. Four. Hold. Four. Out through the—

Nothing. The count would not proceed.

Forty years he had known his body's metronome and it had never once slipped. It slipped now because somewhere to the northeast — through brick, through crowds, through the city's whole dinner-hour racket of horns and bhajans and frying oil — something had been filled to its brim, and then emptied.

The city has a heartbeat. You only learn this by guarding it for a very long time. It was the sum of the temple bells and the aarti's surge and the uncountable small radiances of three million people *eating, laughing, reading their children to sleep*. And just now — one minute gone — all of it had skipped, once, like a single missed stroke in an old and careful heart.

Ram set the tea cup down without feeling the parapet under it.

His hand was doing the trembling. He knew its dialects by now. This was not the age-shake, not the training-tremor. This was the nineteen-year dread — the one that came from knowing exactly what it meant when the fire of this particular city all at once *lay down*, like wheat under something coming through.

He opened his eyes, though he had not consciously closed them. The night was what it had been: neem shadows, the distant vocal joy of the river, his son not yet home.

"Not tomorrow," Ram said to the empty courtyard, very quietly, not as a command, but as the only kind of prayer a man like him had ever been taught to make. "Please. Not tomorrow."

The leaves of the neem did not stir.

Far beneath, with the patience of all extinct and vaulting things, the line that had been cut at Manikarnika grew itself an inch, then an inch more, through the dark of the bedrock, and lay still again — refitted, redirected, and waiting for its cup to fill.
