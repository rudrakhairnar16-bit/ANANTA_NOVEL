# CHAPTER 11 — CRACKS

The morning after the fight on the ghats, Rudra got dressed the way a fugitive gets dressed.

The bathroom was dark — the bulb above the mirror had given up sometime before dawn, leaving the small room to a corpse-cold tube over the sink — and he did not reach to switch anything brighter on. Shirt off, he performed the new liturgy in the dark: cotton medical tape, the wide kind his father kept in the storeroom's third drawer for wrists and ankles; six strips, overlapping, laid in a neat band above the heart, flat against the hairline seam that had opened under the skin above his sternum sometime during the last seconds of the previous night.

It did not hurt. That was the thing he could not get anyone to understand, because there was no one to tell it to: the crack in his mother's work did not hurt at all. It *radiated.* Laid his hand flat against the taped band and the warmth that came back up through the palm was the warmth of a clay tandoor photographed from a respectful distance — banked, interested, faintly cozy —

In the corridor, his mother's footsteps passed once — deliberately, noisily, the way she made steps heavy when she wanted occupants of rooms to know they were occupied — and did not stop. Nineteen years of supervised dawn-drills, and she had decided in the making of this morning's dough that today the surveillance would be performed by trust. He heard her go down. He heard, one room over, the storeroom door's bolt — his father's — unrenovated ancient note of it — not slide at all. Nobody anywhere was doing inventory.

That was how he knew they both knew.

He tore off a seventh strip, sealed the ends, and pulled the kurta over the whole business in one movement, checking twice in the mirror the kurta's collar-line, holding the collar's edge away from the throat so he could look down inside, from above, like a man checking a well.

Tuesday. There would be a lecture at eleven. There would be his mother's questioning palms at breakfast. There would be, alone on the porch railing, his father's small hammer, beating out its daily judgement on something.

"Rudraaa—" from the kitchen: the long, three-note call of a house about to feed someone.

"Aaya," he called back, and completed the tape's ragged mummery with a shrug, and went out to be a good person's ordinary son.

---

Breakfast: parathas, left-hand side of a square of old newspaper; the FM transistor declaring the ghatside *jaanch jaari* between two turns of bhajan; his mother, attentive, furiously ordinary, her glance touching his collarbone twice; his father, silent over his own steel plate, so untalking today that the silence took a chair.

"Kal raat hawa tez thi," Ram said finally, without looking.

At the doorway, the rolling pin stopped for one half-second and then exactly resumed.

"Haan," Rudra said. "Aarti ki wajah se shor bhi zyada tha."

"Hmm," said Ram, and folded the newspaper, and said nothing else for the rest of the meal — nothing about the pillar that was, by now, on the front page of the late-edition city supplement under the heading *GHAT PE PURANI IMARAT GIRI, EK GHAYAL* — nothing about the black-shuttering video links that had spent the past two days blinking out of the world like streetlights along a road at switch-off time. Nothing. He ate with the even attention of a man executing something.

It took Rudra until the bus to name what had felt strangest about it: the newspaper had been folded *inward*, its photograph section folded to the inside, by hands that did that every day of the year except today.

The bus did the city's morning reading for him.

It crawled past the numbered series of Varanasi's ordinary Tuesdays — a shuttered cable office; three women in white-at-Assi pilgrimage dress arguing about e-rickshaw change; the municipal van with its loudspeaker asleep in the lap of a clerk — and at every signal the conductor's paper money slapped against the old rolled ticket-spine in his satchel like a small drum-major of currency. At the Lanka crossing the city opened its daily parenthesis of dust, and through the bus window Rudra watched two schoolboys in the standard khaki-half-pants livery of their class act out, in the safe margin of the pavement, the entire ghat fight of the night before last.

"Dekh — *aise* pillar gira tha! Ek ladke ne aise maara tha—" the elder one demonstrated atop a waiting scooter — "aur fit pitthar se phatke bahar aa gaye —"

"Jhooth!" said the younger, worshipfully, both hands over his mouth.

"Sach! Mere mamu ke chacha ke ladke ke dost ne dekha tha—!"

Outside the window, Varanasi rolled on with the whole cargo of its morning — bhajans from a speaker-shop being tested in bursts; peasants' sacks of onions crossing under rope-bridges of wire; a short convoy of saffron prayer-flagged tractors — and inside it, inside his kurta's tape of concealment, the seam above his sternum lifted once against the cotton, enquiring, and was denied. The boys' pillar-myth had already run twenty-eight hours ahead of the paperwork. *This is how it happens,* he thought. *The truth is assigned to your cousin's cousin's friend; the newsprint fact is buried near the achaar recipes.*

"Chal — aarti waale din ka hindi homework!" the conductor said, rapping the pole; and the two schoolboys snapped to; and Rudra looked out over both their heads at a city that worshipped stone and iron and would go on doing so, and did not let himself wonder what else it bowed to when no one was around to invent a cousin.

---

The lecture was in the old gallery-style hall — Heat & Mass Transfer, the third-year core — with its bench-tiers rising to the back and its warm woolen dim: a hall built in an era that chose teak over window-light, with the ceiling fans turning somewhere overhead in valedictory slow motion and the projector's blue-white cone cutting down through the dust-haze to slide after slide of conduction equations.

There is a specific mercy the institution extends to the frightened: timetables. At eleven o'clock every educated boy in that hall settled into a groove that had been worn by fifty consecutive batches of the frightened, slid notes open, abandoned their private load-bearing terrors, and became, for one hour, the audience of conduction-through-composite-walls. Chalk went on with its pale arithmetic. A ceiling fan completed a slow bureaucratic orbit. Someone's tiffin, illicitly opened three rows down, released the suddenly planetary redolence of pickle into the teak-dark air, and fifty noses inhaled at once with total forgiveness — the collective animal function of a class that has agreed to survive thermodynamics together.

Rudra sat mid-tier with his notebook open. He was, as always, a page ahead of the lecturer. This was why lectures were dangerous for him lately. Prepared meant free, and free meant counting, and counting meant listening — listening to the warm little seam under the tape, which today had a pulse like a second small throat.

He was on the count's twenty-fourth loop when the projector flickered.

Only that. A power blip — the cone shuddered, threw the Fourier slide sideways for one glare of over-white, went momentarily cavernous — the hall's two hundred faces lifting from their notebooks into a shared half-second of dark. Two hundred people exhaled their stock "lo" of the power cut, and the tube-lights gate-crashed awake along the wall-fans —

— and in that one borrowed second of dark, before the lights took, Rudra's eyes burned.

Not hurt. Not flash. *Burned.* The coal behind the tape, a bed of ember under its own patient's ash-keeping, rose against the crack like a dog rising in the dark when the door-chain slides — and in him, effortless, as if it had always lived in his own two mirror-lenses, the red thread opened behind his eyes and filled them: no drama, no pain, just — *red-gold*, the colour of the bottom of a lamp-lit well —

He clamped them shut in the same breath. The lights came on. Another boy two benches down said "Fuse gaya kya" to nobody; the lecturer, who had been writing with his back to it, turned around forgivingly to the hall, chalk poised: "Ho gaya—" and went on writing.

Rudra kept his hands over his half-lowered face as if rubbing tiredness away. Time passed like spilled water.

When he opened them slowly and deliberately for vision like a man re-emerging from swimming, nobody was looking at him.

Except Aadhya.

Three benches down, two columns left, in profile to the hall — she was not watching the board; she was watching him, motionless, notebook still open to a clean page with its last line of writing abandoned mid-word, her whole face leached to the colour of bone-white paper: because she'd happened to glance up in the same half-second, by mischance, by sympathy, by the small sixth sense with which she had begun to map his weather — whatever the reason, she had seen it plainly: his eyes, in the dark, burning like two new coals.

She had seen. There was no way she had not.

He gave her the only armour he owned. He looked at her one full second — flat, ruined-casual, openly *satisfied* that he'd caught her staring — and turned his head away forward to the board like a man grandiosely uninterested, scribbling three garbage words on the page to support the act, feeling her not-leaving glance settle on him like five grams of rain.

Both of them spent the remaining forty minutes extremely visibly not writing anything down.

Around them, the hall returned to its covenant of chalk. The lecturer filled in, on the blackboard's rightmost corner, the little boxed inscription students had learned to watch for — *IMP for ESE* — and a hundred and something pens resumed copying the universe's instructions. Veer, three benches back and one tier up, who had attended none of today's class so far and attended precisely none of what followed, had lifted his head once at the flicker and put it back down. By anybody's casual inspection the episode had filtered into hall-lore before the bell even rang: one second of voltage sag, one boy rubbing his eyes, two hundred people permitted forever to pretend no one had seen anything at all.

---

He had the escape lines prepared: he had them before the class bell released the two hundred, he had them by the time his bag was over his shoulder. He had excuses arranged in order of plausibility first-impression — projector glare, hay-fever, piya hua nahi hoon wala joke — like a man stacking ammunition along his steps.

It helped nothing. She fell into pace beside him at the lecture-hall landing like someone who'd taught her legs the whole evacuation plan three corridors up: not running, not hurrying, simply *there.* Which was, in its way, infinitely more terrifying than pursuit.

"Wait," she said.

People were flowing past them down the staircase — the college's standard animal — and in the crowd the two of them stalled outness, occupying a detergent-smelling piece of landing; she lowered her voice by one degree as the bottom stairs thinned. "Tumhare aankhein—"

"Projector," he said immediately — too immediately, the word coming out machined. "Screen se direct seat pe light aayi. Aadhi class ne toh —"

"Class ki nahi, *andar se* thi," she said. Very plainly. No tremor in it at all: that was the frightening part. She was not asking. After all — she had armed herself with the same thing he had: she had spent seventy-two hours since the ghat-fight collecting her own incident-details, cataloguing every impossible thing in Braille. She read his face the way he'd taught others to read the counters of his opponents: by the sound it made under the wrist. "Us light mein tumhari aankhein wahan se *de rahi thi*. Main janti hoon screen reflection kaisi dikhti hai. Ye wali nahi thi."

He had prepared nothing at all for that.

The landing was empty; somewhere on the floor above a door was kicked open, metallic, dismissive. Three seconds went past. Through everything he kept the faint smile with which his childhood's first trainer — his father's repeated cold disappointment — had fitted him, like armour changing its voice twice a minute.

"Toh tumhe eye-checkup," he said, with a long string of patience. "Discount de dunga. Srinivas ke peeche wali lane mein ek doctor hai — naam likh ke de deta hoon."

"Rudra."

"Agar tumhe logon ki aankhon mein time-pass dikh rahi hai toh problem meri nahi hai—"

"Uss din ghat pe," she said. Man to man. "Jab woo pillar gira — uske theek pehle — tumhare *haath* pe bhi yehi tha." She tapped her own sternum, once, gently, the way a botanist touches a known plant. "Main peeche khadi thi. Maine dekha. Wo — bhi — andar se tha."

Under his collar, taped flat, the seam seemed to pulse once, happily, *at her touch from three feet away.* He hated his own body with a quick white clean flare of a hatred he had only ever reserved for the coaches who'd called his father cruel.

"Aadhya." Even; extremely clear. "Meri aankhon mein kuch nahi tha. Mere haath pe kuch nahi tha. Lecture chal raha tha aur main so raha tha. Jab main sota hoon toh puri duniya mujhe miraculous lagti hai — par mujhe chhodo."

"Jhooth," said Aadhya, softly, and did not even make it an accusation. She let it stand as the room's temperature. For a second the two of them regarded one another with the elegant mutual devastation of two negotiators who each know the other's card-count.

The hall went on breathing above and below them.

"Theek hai," she said at last, and stepped back — one step, two — giving him back the layout his face was begging for; her voice fell half a note into something older, a register she hadn't used on him in three years: "Jab tumhe batana ho, tab bata dena. Kal wale — woh log — teen the. Unka koi bhai-bahan toh nahi hoga jo wapas na aaye. *Teen ka matlab mujhe bhi pata hai kya hota hai.* Main—" she changed course with immense private effort, visibly, like a truck changing lanes; smiled, one-sided, ruinously fond — "—bas. Lecture miss kar rahi hoon. Bye."

And she went down the stairs one flight exactly as a friend, and did not turn at the bottom to look up, leaving him alone on the landing with twenty-four counts of breathing and the tape's small tandoor warmth beating up through his kurta like something saying *yes, yes, keep talking like that, harder.*

---

He found Veer in the canteen's last bench, with his right sleeve rolled to the elbow — attending to nothing at all in particular and doing so immensely, a colossus meditating over one cold samosa and a steel tumbler of chai gone wrinkly with standing.

Rudra sat. Put down his bag. The seam-pulse under his collar matched, by hundreds of metres of air and the arrangement of old brick, the scorch of the exhaust fan above the vendor-back; the college noon went on; nobody was out of the ordinary but themselves. They did not look at each other for forty seconds. Veer turned his fist over once — palm open, palm closed — the split knuckles dark with Mercurochrome.

"Kal raat main soya nahi," Veer said, quietly.

"Hum dono ne," said Rudra.

"Hath jala nahi. Garam tha bas." A terrible awe had entered Veer's voice — a thing looser than fear, attendant to fear the way a junior priest attends on a ceremony. "Jab jal raha tha, us waqt, Rudra — mujhe darr na tha. Gussa nahi tha. Kuch nahi tha. Sirf — ek cheez poochna thi main tumse." He glanced sideways. "Tum pehle se jaante the. *Hum mein kya hai.*"

Rudra said nothing. In the steam off the chai, under the ripped sleeve, the old pyre-burn on Veer's arm — the one from Manikarnika, fractal and glossy — was tinged a very faint tan-gold under the flesh, the way old pottery shows salt where the water dried.

"Achha tum dono ne nahi bataya toh main samajh gaya," Veer said, with the loyalty of the unanswerable. "Par — ek baat. Kal woh jo mujh mein tha — *woh mujhe nahi lagta bahar se aaya hai.* Pehle se pada tha. Katora bhara kar chhupa rakha tha kisi ne. Didi jab school mein pit-ti thi mere liye ooncha sada hathiyar uthane par—" he finally tried a grin; it fitted him like dog-tags; "—tabhi se shayad." He flexed the fingers open-shut, once, the size of small garlanded idols. "Par kal — pillar ke age main *roka nahi jaa raha tha*. Samajh? Kuch naye aaye the aur unka hisaab se nahi, galti se pillaron ke darmayan tha. Mujh mein aur koi — *hai*, Rudra. Tum dono — hum chaaron — ke paas kuch hai. Aur tumhein kya karna hai usse? Baithne do ya nikalne do?"

It took Rudra a while. He looked at his own wrapped knuckles. He heard, through four closed boxes of ribs and tape, the coal saying *out, out, out.* He thought of his father fitting his forearms with the corrected bone-bruise of ten thousand mornings, and of his mother's voice singing *children like you*, and of Aadhya standing three feet downstairs forty minutes ago reading his glare like a map.

"Baithne do," he said. "Abhi. Jab tak samajh na aaye kya hai. Baithne do."

Veer nodded, slow and sad, the nod of a man whose mother had told him he was God's particular weapon and who had always suspected otherwise but never wanted it proven.

"Sasta nahi hoga," he said, at length. "Baithne dena cheezon ko."

"Kabhi sasta hua hai?"

"Pata nahi. Main toh sasta hi khareedta hoon sab." The grin came back then, his own, taking the bench with him into motion; he stood, stretched to a size the canteen's plastic chairs sincerely doubted, and saluted the canteen-boy for the bill. On his way past, cotton-sleeved shoulder an inch from Rudra's collarbone, he stopped.

"Ek kaam aur. Tune tape lagaya hai gale ke neeche — dikh raha hai. Mat karo yeh. Dudh ka doodh karegi Ammu. Aur woh badi wali —" Veer lowered his voice, all veneration, "—dhyaan rakhti hai. Chhodna mat."

---

The second confrontation of the day happened at the notice-boards near the girls' common room, administered by Ira, method one.

"Baki sab jhooth bol rahe hain toh tumse expect karti hoon kam se kam *informed* jhooth," she said, simply, and handed him a ruled sheet torn from a bound register. "Movie-wali baat nahi hai. Saansein, pulse, temperature, hum dono ke haath ke daag, woh pillar, wo telescope-wala coil — meri ek theory hai. Ghar pe padh lena." He glanced at the paper; beneath its blank top half, the drawings — tiny, diagonal, unmistakable — of the cup-and-nodes chart from beside her bed, made from memory in double-battery light. At the bottom, in her surd's handwriting: *11 days till zero. Tumse door kuch nahi hoga. Samet lo.*

"Itni si baat sach bol do toh yeh —"

"Na," she said gently, already turning away. "Sach de dene se toh tum dono uso banaate ho jo ho. Main uske liye taiyaar nahi hoon abhi." A corridor-moment; she halted, did not turn around. "Woh ladki Aadhya sahi keh rahi hai. Batana tumhe. Par tum dono ke saamne ek naya asli aadmi khada karna zaroori hai pehle — jo *raaz nahi* ho. Mummy baba nahi. Koi aisa jo bole agle hafte ki lottery mein tum sab ya tum sab nahi hote. Uske baad tum khud ke bhi malik banoge."

And she nodded once — to herself, probably — and left him among the notice-board staples, beside a climbing-club poster, a lost-ID-card plea, and a Sanskrit-seminar schedule curling like birch-bark, off all of which his face — whole, untorn, his mother's tap-tap joinery holding at rumour-grade — waited silently to be noticed by the world.

---

That night he did not go to the ghat. He did not go to the roof. He ate two rotis less than usual, informed his parents of an assignment of ampleness, and went up to his room and locked the door and waited until the house conducted its nightly descent into stillness.

Only then did he move the lamp.

In the dark — the corridor bulb having one of its flickering centuries again — he undressed to the waist, carried his study lamp to the wall's long mirror, angled its small hard gaze across the glass, and stood before himself. Shirt off. Tape off — peeling slowly, adhesive refusing, detaching in protesting small increments, until it came away with the last of the medical smell.

He looked.

On his chest, over the sternum's keel, the hairline was no longer a hairline. Over the last thirty-six hours it had unpacked itself, gently, into a geometry: a ring of small angular fractures — a small mandala of hair-thin **lines**, laid out in symmetrical spokes across his breastbone, something deliberate and pre-structured and diagrammatic, the way a tile cracks along any pattern of glaze-stress it was *kilned* with. His mother's nineteen-year-old joinery — he knew that now with a certainty that arrived voicelessly in the stomach, the way the names of old wounds arrive — had never been spread over him like a blanket but built over him like architecture, and the architecture was coming down *along the blueprint, beam by named beam*, a temple whose dome was about to know sky.

And in each thin seam: a fine gold-red line. Open like a kiln door. Looking out.

His eyes caught his own attention in the mirror — where the eyes of most boys meet them with the flat human soft-brown of all childhood, these were still burning red-gold — *still*, ten hours after the lecture hall — a deep, steadily banked, majestic ember that did not go out no matter how many counts he ran, and the face above the burning looked utterly, pathetically afraid of itself —

—and he saw it.

And he did the bravest, and the most terrible thing of all: he stood there and looked at himself. Two hands' worth of breaths went by. In the un-astonished dark, the reflection faced him down with the first and last teacher's patience of true disaster: there was no other side of this; there would be no day the other side of this; it would happen or it would be described, and in either case it was first, last and always his.

"Hello," Rudra said at last, quietly — to the construction — in what he did not know was the way exactly his father used to address silent weapons in storage.

It did not answer. It was not there to chatter. It was not there because someone had hidden it in him; it was there because it was *him* — vaulted, bricked-in, re-floored, re-painted, nineteen years deep in masonry — and it was merely coming back to the surface, where it had never not been.

He pressed his forehead to the glass and breathed — *in, four; hold, four; out, four; hold, four* — and behind his lids, deep in the architecture of the chest, the seam flexed like a door testing its hinges, twice, inquiry, patience — *not yet.*

He thought of Veer, three roofs away probably, lying wide awake with his arm outside the blanket, terror-smiling at the memory of how *good* it had felt. He thought of Ira feeding tomorrow's alarm-gates into her machines across town with the dry tenacity of someone keeping time for a universe that didn't ask. He thought of Aadhya on the landing — *"teen the; unka koi bhai-bahan toh nahi hoga jo wapas na aaye"* — how she'd fooled the whole landing conversation into being about lectures and had in fact been delivering, softly, the obituary of his version of events; how she had stood and smelled of the banyan leaf-mould even now, three nights later, like anyone who has brought back too much of a shrine on their skin.

He owed all three of them something; the exact denomination had not yet been decided.

In the morning he would tell none of them. He would bandage and joke and run with one-tenth acceleration in lane jog that nobody could measure, he would let his mother tap his pulse without changing his own, keep his head above his own assurances, and he would, absolutely, go to the mela if his mother later ordered one into existence — because it was exactly because there was no distant repairable frame anymore that the arithmetic of concealment was suddenly boring, like a game everyone had played as a smaller child.

But that was morning. Tonight, now, glass to forehead, he permitted himself the other kind of honesty available to a nineteen-year-old, the out-loud variety:

"Darr lag raha hai," he told the seam — first person, singular, present tense. "Theek hai? Bol diya. Darr lag raha hai." He breathed. The fog bloomed and shrank. "Ab tum bhi bolo."

The seam did not answer. But it banked, very slightly, like a dog that hears its name and — correctly, per contract — does not get up.

His mother hummed two rooms away, very faintly, a tune with words no one wrote down.

The tape lay on the floor where it had finally desisted. Rudra switched off the lamp, and stood in the red-edged dark and looked at his own eyes until he could no longer see them, and then longer than that, and then he dressed and put on the smile behind which he had spent the day, so that no one tomorrow would have to know what the house of his body was doing behind its locked, ordinary door.
