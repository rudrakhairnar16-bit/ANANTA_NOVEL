# PROLOGUE — THE FIRST SIGNAL

The universe did not begin with light.

It began with a breath — a single, shuddering exhalation from a being who was neither male nor female, neither god nor mortal, neither beginning nor end. Ardhanarishwar. The name itself was a contradiction, a joining of two halves that had never been separate. Before the Lokas crystallized into existence, before the Devtas learned to govern, before the first human drew their first breath on the soil of Prithvi, there was only this consciousness — vast, silent, and utterly alone.

And then came the fracture.

Not from within. From without.

A being — once human, once mortal, once desperate — tore through the fabric of time itself and stood before the primordial consciousness with blood on his hands and rage in his ten fractured minds. He had drunk the essence of gods. He had watched civilizations crumble while the divine maintained their precious balance. He had come to demand an ending.

The consciousness refused.

The being attacked.

And in that single, catastrophic moment — when mortal fury met divine indifference — Ardhanarishwar split. Nar from Naari. Light from shadow. Order from entropy. The seven Lokas erupted into existence like shards of a shattered mirror, each reflecting a different facet of the original whole. The Maha Devtas materialized, confused and newborn, already burdened with responsibilities they had not chosen. The Devtas followed, then the Kuls, then the humans, then the insects, then the bacteria, then the atoms — all cascading outward from that original wound in an unbroken chain of consequence that would eventually be called the Divine Cycle.

The being who caused it all watched his creation unfold with something that might have been horror, or might have been satisfaction. He had wanted to end the cycle. Instead, he had birthed it.

And before the last fragment of Ardhanarishwar's unified consciousness dissolved into the architecture of reality, a voice — neither male nor female, neither angry nor forgiving — spoke a single prophecy into the void:

*"Main tere ant ke liye khud aaunga."*

I will come myself for your end.

The being laughed. Or perhaps he wept. In the darkness between the newborn Lokas, the distinction no longer mattered.

---

Millennia passed. The Cycle turned. Gods rose and fell. Kuls warred and intermarried. Humans built cities on top of the bones of older civilizations, oblivious to the cosmic machinery grinding beneath their feet.

And in the third Loka below the Earth — in the lightless corridors of Sutala, where the air tasted of iron and ancient stone — a boy knelt on cold rock with a blindfold pressed against his eyes.

He was young. Nineteen, perhaps, though in Sutala's timeless dark, age was a meaningless metric. His wrists were bound. His breathing was controlled — in through the nose, out through the mouth — the way someone had trained him since before he could walk. His heartbeat was steady despite the hands gripping his arms, despite the rough stone scraping his knees, despite the whispered voices of the figures dragging him forward through the corridor.

There were three of them. He could tell by the footfalls. Two holding his arms, one walking behind with the soft, deliberate tread of someone accustomed to violence. They did not speak to him. They did not need to.

The corridor opened into a vast chamber. The boy felt the space change — the air grew colder, the echoes longer, the weight of the ceiling above him pressing down like a physical force. He was pushed to his knees. The hands released his arms.

Then the blindfold was pulled away.

Light — harsh, white, unnatural — flooded his vision. He blinked, tears streaming, and slowly the chamber resolved around him: towering pillars of black stone, veins of luminescent mineral running through the walls like frozen lightning, and at the far end of the chamber, a throne carved from a single piece of obsidian.

A man sat on the throne. He was tall, gaunt, dressed in robes that seemed to absorb the light around him. In his right hand, he held a sword — not raised, not threatening, simply resting across his knee with the casual familiarity of a man who had used it so many times that it had become an extension of his arm.

The man studied the boy for a long moment. His eyes were ancient, far older than his face, and they held the particular weariness of someone who had seen too many versions of this exact scene.

"I hope you performed better," the man said.

His voice was quiet. It carried no malice, no cruelty, no theatrical menace. It was the voice of a man stating a fact, the way a doctor might deliver a diagnosis or a teacher might return a failed exam. The indifference was worse than any threat.

The boy said nothing. His jaw tightened. His hands, still bound, curled into fists.

And then — so faintly that the figures guarding him did not notice, so briefly that the man on the throne might have imagined it — the boy's eyes began to glow.

Not with reflected light. Not with the luminescence of Sutala's mineral veins. With something deeper. Something older. A red-gold fire that burned behind his irises like a star collapsing inward, carrying within it the echo of a seal that had held for nineteen years and was now, finally, beginning to crack.

The man on the throne leaned forward.

The glow faded.

The chamber returned to silence.

But something had shifted — a frequency, a resonance, a signal sent out across the seven Lokas like a ripple across still water. In Varanasi, a woman standing in a kitchen dropped a steel glass and did not know why her hands were shaking. In a realm above the clouds, a usurper king looked up from his throne with sudden, inexplicable dread. And in the deepest darkness of Pataal, where the bones of an entire civilization lay buried under millennia of silence, something that had been dead for a very long time began, ever so slightly, to stir.

The Cycle had noticed.

And the Cycle, as always, would respond.
