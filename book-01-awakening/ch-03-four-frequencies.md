# CHAPTER 3 — FOUR FREQUENCIES

Veer occupied space the way a boulder occupies a riverbed — immovably, unapologetically, and with the quiet, gravitational authority of something that had been exactly where it was for a very long time and had no intention of relocating for anyone's convenience.

He was already at the cafeteria table when Aadhya arrived for her afternoon break, having claimed the bench with the best view of the entrance and the worst view of the kitchen (he hated the smell of the cafeteria's dal, which he described, with the culinary precision of a man who had eaten home-cooked food his entire life, as "insulting to the concept of lentils"). He was built like a temple guardian — six-foot-two, broad across the chest and shoulders, with the kind of dense, functional musculature that came from years of wrestling, swimming, and the particular brand of physical labor that his family's Rakshak lineage had disguised, for generations, as "traditional exercise." His skin was dark, his jaw was square, and his eyes — warm, brown, perpetually amused — held the specific, infuriating calm of a person who had never once in his life doubted his own right to exist.

"Sit," he said, sliding a plate of jalebi across the table. "You look like you've been fighting with a tree."

Aadhya sat. She took a jalebi. She did not ask how he knew about the tree, because Veer knew everything that happened on the BHU campus with the quiet, comprehensive awareness of a man whose grandmother had once described him as "the boy who hears the walls breathing."

"It's not fighting with me," she said. "It's dying. There's a difference."

"Sure." Veer bit into a jalebi with the enthusiasm of someone who believed that sugar was a food group. "And the difference is?"

Before Aadhya could answer, Ira appeared.

She materialized the way she always did — silently, suddenly, as if she had been standing there for hours and the rest of the world had simply failed to notice. She was small, barely five-foot-one, with sharp, angular features and eyes that were too large for her face and too still for her age. Her hair was cut short, practical, the kind of haircut that suggested she had taken scissors to it herself in a bathroom mirror and considered the result adequate. She wore a faded blue kurti over jeans, and in her hands, she carried a leather-bound notebook that she held against her chest like a shield.

"The pattern shifted," she said, without preamble, sitting down next to Veer and opening the notebook to a page covered in dense, meticulous handwriting and hand-drawn maps.

Veer leaned over to look. Aadhya leaned over to look. Neither of them understood what they were seeing.

The map showed Varanasi — the old city, the ghats, the narrow lanes, the temples — but overlaid on the familiar geography was a web of lines that Ira had drawn in red ink, thin and precise as spider silk. The lines connected specific points across the city: Manikarnika Ghat, Dashashwamedh, Assi Ghat, the Kashi Vishwanath temple, the Durga temple, the Sankat Mochan temple, and dozens of smaller shrines and intersections that Aadhya didn't recognize. The lines converged at a single point, marked with a small, dark circle.

"Manikarnika," Aadhya said, recognizing the convergence point.

"The Pancha Koshi Yatra route," Ira said. Her voice was quiet, flat, the voice of someone reporting data rather than sharing a discovery. "The ancient pilgrimage circuit. Five kos, roughly fifteen kilometers, circling the entire sacred zone of Kashi. I've been tracking energy readings along the route for three weeks." She tapped the red lines. "These are ley lines — or something like them. Energy channels running beneath the city's surface, following the pilgrimage path. They've been stable for centuries. But in the last ten days, they've started shifting."

"Shifting how?" Veer asked.

Ira turned the page. The next map showed the same route, but the red lines had been redrawn in black, and the convergence point had moved — not by much, perhaps two hundred meters, but enough to break the symmetry of the original pattern. The new convergence point was still at Manikarnika, but deeper, lower, as if the energy was being pulled downward, beneath the ghat, beneath the river, beneath the bedrock of the city itself.

"Down," Ira said. "The energy is flowing down."

"Down where?" Aadhya asked.

Ira looked up. Her large, still eyes held something that Aadhya had never seen in them before — a flicker of uncertainty, quickly suppressed, like a candle flame cupped against the wind.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But whatever is pulling it down is the same thing that's killing your tree."

Aadhya's chai glass froze halfway to her lips. "How do you know about the tree?"

"The tree is connected to the ley line network. Eastern branch, third node. When the energy started flowing downward, the tree's root system lost its connection to the spiritual reservoir. It's starving." She paused. "You already knew this. You felt it when you touched the trunk this morning."

The cafeteria noise seemed to recede. Aadhya stared at Ira, her heart hammering, her carefully maintained composure cracking along fault lines she had spent three years trying to seal.

"You were watching me?" she whispered.

"I was watching the tree," Ira said. "You happened to be attached to it."

Veer, sensing the rising tension with the instinct of a man who had spent his entire life mediating between volatile forces, intervened with the subtlety of a sledgehammer.

"Okay, so we have a dying tree, shifting ley lines, and energy flowing into the ground like a cosmic drain," he said, counting on his fingers. "That's three things. Three things is a pattern. Patterns are bad. I hate patterns." He looked at Aadhya. "Also, you need to talk to Rudra."

Aadhya's jaw tightened. "Rudra thinks I'm overthinking a tree."

"Rudra thinks everything is overthinking because Rudra's entire survival strategy is to pretend that nothing is happening until it hits him in the face hard enough to break his nose." Veer's tone was affectionate but firm, the tone of someone who had known Rudra long enough to diagnose his defense mechanisms with clinical precision. "But he knows more than he's telling you. More than he's telling any of us."

"How do you know that?"

Veer hesitated. He glanced at Ira, who gave an almost imperceptible nod — the kind of micro-gesture that suggested a conversation the two of them had already had, in private, at a time and place Aadhya had not been invited to.

"Because I've known him since we were six," Veer said quietly. He set down his jalebi and leaned forward, his voice dropping to a register that was barely audible above the cafeteria's roar. "And I know what his father does to him."

Aadhya went still.

"His father trains him," Veer continued. "Every day. Since he was one year old. I'm not talking about karate classes or cricket practice, Aadhya. I'm talking about combat training that would break most adults. I'm talking about a man who made his five-year-old son run barefoot across broken stone until his feet bled and then told him to keep going because 'the ground doesn't care about your pain.' I'm talking about a man who dislocated Rudra's shoulder when he was twelve during a sparring session and didn't take him to the hospital because 'a healer who can't set his own bones is useless.'"

Aadhya's hands were cold. "That's... that's abuse."

"It's complicated," Veer said, and the weariness in his voice was so genuine, so heavy, that Aadhya felt the word "abuse" shrink in her mouth, suddenly inadequate. "Ram uncle isn't cruel. He's not drunk, he's not violent for the sake of violence, he's not the kind of father who hits his kid because he had a bad day at work. He loves Rudra. I've seen it — the way he watches Rudra when Rudra isn't looking, the way his hands shake after a hard session, the way he stays up all night sitting outside Rudra's door like a guard dog. But he's terrified. He's terrified of something, Aadhya. Something he won't talk about, something that makes a man who can bench-press a motorcycle look like he's about to cry when he thinks no one is watching. And he's training Rudra for it. Whatever it is. He's been preparing his son for a war that hasn't started yet."

Veer paused. The cafeteria noise rushed back in — the clatter of plates, the shouting, the tinny sound of a Bollywood song playing from someone's phone.

"Three months ago," Veer said, "I saw Rudra's back when he was changing after a swim. He has scars, Aadhya. Not from fights. From training. Burn marks, impact bruises that healed wrong, a line across his ribs that looks like it was made by something sharper than a fist. He's nineteen years old and his body looks like a veteran's." He met Aadhya's eyes. "So when he tells you he's fine, when he tells you you're overthinking, when he deflects with sarcasm and walks away — understand that he's doing it because he's been taught, since before he could talk, that showing weakness is the fastest way to get someone you love killed."

Aadhya sat in silence for a long time. The jalebi on her plate had gone cold. The chai in her glass had formed a skin. Around them, the cafeteria churned with the oblivious energy of students who were worried about deadlines and crushes and the upcoming Diwali break, and Aadhya felt a sudden, vertiginous sense of dislocation, as if the ground beneath her feet had shifted — the same way the ley lines on Ira's map had shifted — and the world she thought she understood was revealing itself to be a thin, fragile surface stretched over something vast and dark and impossibly old.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

Veer shrugged. The easy, boulder-like calm was back, but his eyes were serious. "Because the tree is dying. The ley lines are shifting. Ira's maps are showing energy flowing into the ground toward something none of us can identify. And Rudra — who has been trained since birth to fight whatever is coming — is pretending nothing is happening." He looked at her, then at Ira, then back at Aadhya. "I think the war Ram uncle has been preparing for is already here. And I think the four of us are going to be in the middle of it whether we like it or not."

Ira closed her notebook. The leather cover was worn smooth from years of handling, and on the back, embossed in faded gold lettering, was a symbol Aadhya didn't recognize — a circle divided into seven concentric rings, with a single point at the center.

"The convergence is accelerating," Ira said quietly. "Whatever is pulling the energy downward, it's getting stronger. I estimate we have weeks, not months, before the pattern collapses entirely."

"And then what?" Aadhya asked.

Ira looked at her. Those large, still eyes held no fear, no excitement, no emotion at all — just the clear, dispassionate gaze of someone who had already seen the answer in a dream and was simply waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

"Then the ground opens," she said. "And what's underneath comes out."

The afternoon light was turning gold when the four of them left the cafeteria — Aadhya, Veer, Ira, and, trailing behind them with the reluctant, irritated energy of a cat being dragged to a vet appointment, Rudra.

He had not wanted to come. Veer had essentially physically hauled him out of the library, where Rudra had been pretending to study organic chemistry and actually staring at the same page for forty-five minutes with the unfocused intensity of a man whose mind was somewhere else entirely. His knuckles were re-wrapped, his jaw still bruised, and his left shoulder was moving with the careful, restricted range of motion that Aadhya now recognized, thanks to Veer's revelations, as the aftermath of a training injury rather than a casual accident.

"I don't understand why I'm here," Rudra said, for the fourth time, as they walked through the campus gates and into the narrow lanes of old Varanasi.

"Because Ira has a map," Veer said.

"I don't care about Ira's map."

"The map shows energy patterns that match the anomalies you've been sensing for the past two weeks," Ira said, without looking at him.

Rudra's step faltered. Just for a fraction of a second — a micro-hesitation that most people would have missed, but that Aadhya, now armed with the knowledge of what his body had endured, caught with the precision of a surgeon spotting a tumor on an X-ray.

"I haven't been sensing anything," Rudra said. His voice was flat, controlled, and entirely unconvincing.

"Your kavach has been fluctuating," Ira continued, still not looking at him. "I can see the energy pattern from here. It's centered on your chest, roughly at the sternum, and it's been destabilizing at a rate of approximately three percent per day for the last ten days. By my calculations, it will reach critical failure within six to eight weeks."

Rudra stopped walking.

The lane was narrow, barely wide enough for two people to walk abreast, lined with crumbling brick walls and overhanging balconies draped with laundry. The air smelled of incense and sewage and the sweet, cloying scent of marigold garlands hanging from a nearby temple doorway. A cow stood in the middle of the lane, chewing cud with the bovine indifference of a creature that had achieved a level of spiritual detachment that most humans could only aspire to.

Rudra stared at Ira. His expression had changed — the sarcasm gone, the dismissiveness gone, replaced by something sharp and focused and suddenly, terrifyingly alert. It was the face of a soldier who has just heard the click of a landmine under his foot.

"What did you say?" he asked. His voice was very quiet.

Ira finally looked at him. "I said your kavach is failing. You know what a kavach is, Rudra. Your mother placed it on you at birth. It's the only thing standing between you and the energy signature that every sentient being in the upper three Lokas has been trying to track for nineteen years." She tilted her head, studying him with those enormous, unreadable eyes. "The question is not whether it will break. The question is whether you'll be ready when it does."

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the cow stopped chewing.

Rudra's hands were clenched at his sides. His jaw was tight. His eyes — those dark, restless eyes that Aadhya had always interpreted as bored — were burning with a intensity that she now realized had nothing to do with boredom and everything to do with the effort of containing something enormous, something that had been sealed inside him since the moment he was born and was now pressing against the walls of its prison with the relentless, patient force of a river against a dam.

"Who told you about the kavach?" he asked.

"Nobody told me," Ira said. "I saw it."

"In a dream?" Aadhya asked, remembering the conversation from the cafeteria.

Ira's gaze shifted to Aadhya. For the first time, something flickered in those still eyes — a flash of vulnerability, quickly buried.

"In every dream," she said. "Since I was a child."

The four of them stood in the narrow lane, the golden Varanasi light falling around them like a benediction, the distant sound of temple bells marking the approach of evening aarti, and the weight of something ancient and enormous pressing up from beneath the ground beneath their feet.

Rudra closed his eyes. When he opened them, the burning was gone, replaced by the flat, controlled calm of a man who had just made a decision he had been dreading for a very long time.

"Fine," he said. "Show me the map."

Ira opened her notebook. The seven-ringed symbol on the back caught the light, and for a moment — just a moment — it seemed to glow.

The Cycle was turning.

And the four of them, standing in a lane in Varanasi with a dying tree behind them and a broken seal inside one of their chests, were about to step into the current that would carry them — willingly or not — toward the center of a storm that had been building since before the first human drew breath on the soil of Prithvi.

The ground beneath their feet hummed.

None of them noticed.

But something, far below, in the lightless corridors of a realm that had been sealed for millennia, did.
