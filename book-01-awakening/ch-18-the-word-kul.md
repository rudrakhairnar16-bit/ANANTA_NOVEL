# CHAPTER 18 — THE WORD "KUL"

The college, in the morning, was completely itself. This was the most frightening thing about Saturday.

Sweepers swept the quadrangle. The peons cocooned dead leaves into gunny sacks with their morning grumbles; two girl-students arrived early for library seats, complaining about the mid-semester time-table; the guard at the west gate was arguing with the milk van about parking. The pale ring in the grass had left, on drying, only a faint salt-coloured circumference — the sort of blemish the city produced every monsoon and the municipality never treated. Nobody from the hostels had heard anything. Nobody at the chowki had filed anything. By ten AM a gardener with a rake had redistributed the topsoil over the sunken hollow so evenly that the earth itself appeared to have decided to settle the case out of court.

The four of them arrived before the gardener's second pass, at dawn, and thus got their one chance at the hollow before the city administered it.

They stood around it in the grey light in the formation they always took now without discussing it — Ira at the compass bearing, Veer with his body between the others and the lane, Aadhya crouched nearest the earth, Rudra with his hands held away from everything, carefully, as if his hands were tools that had to stay on their hooks until told otherwise.

"Neeche khula tha aur phir mudh gaya," Ira said quietly, notebook out. "Yahan jo gehraayi dikhi thi raat ko — ab woh nahi hai. Mitti *wapas le aayi* apni gehraayi. Yeh normal nahi hai. Jameen audit karti hai, apne hisaab se."

Aadhya, whose palms were bandaged in white cotton strips her mother had not asked about, put two fingertips on the hollow's edge and went still in the particular way she had begun going still since the night of the banyan — listening-not-with-ears. When she stood, she was very pale in a new and entirely unconnected direction.

"Ye mitti bol rahi hai," she said. "Bahut chup-chup. Jo niche gaya — woh *jalaa ke* gaya. Ek cheez gira ke gaya. Wahan —" she pointed into the hollow's heart — "waahin kahin. Haath mat dena seedha. Ek mitti ka dher hai jisme lakdi ki tarah — nahi — *haddi* ki tarah kuch hai."

Veer found a fallen neem branch and worked the hollow crust carefully, the way he dug worms for his grandmother's rose pots, with the patience of a boy raised by careful women. What came up, in the sifted grey palm of the earth, was a shard the length of a finger — not stone, not wood — dense, matt, faintly warm in a morning when everything should have been cold, and curved as though it had once belonged to the inside curve of something that stood upright.

"Yeh toh—" Veer turned it in the light. "Yeh toh *haddi* jaisa hai. Magar aisi haddi pehle kabhi nahi dekhi maine. Yeh toh bilkul —" he sought the word, and failed, and handed it across — "dekh."

On the shard's inner face, on the smooth curvature of it, where no carpenter's tool could conceivably have reached by accident, was a carving. Small. Precise. Unarguably deliberate. The blade had cut it the way devotion cuts — careful with the line it loves.

The carving was a symbol first of all: a circle of seven small notched marks, and in its centre a heavy dot — done so neatly it might have been printed — and across that dot, as though challenging it or guarding it, a stroke in the shape they'd all seen before only in Ira's red-pencil nights: the crossed double-line her dream-map returned to again and again at the map's centre without ever explaining itself.

And under the symbol, in neat old Devanagari, carved small enough to be missed by anyone not already half-praying, was a single word:

कुल

*Kul.*

They crouched over it in a ring in the dawn quiet, and nobody said anything at all, because four of them at once understood exactly the same thing: that a word had arrived which was going to be a door, and that doors of this weight had whole houses connected to them, and that they were, whatever they did from this morning onwards, already standing in the atrium.

---
Ira had the dream-map out of the notebook's back cover before any of them had finished breathing. She had re-drawn it four times since the chamber-vision nights — four versions, the way you re-address a letter to somebody you're not sure is still at the house — and it lay now folded into a stiff square she kept, always, against her ribs inside the windcheater.

She laid the square on the parapet edge and laid the shard on top of the square, and bent over them both.

"Ye symbol," she said, and her voice had the flat thickness it had when it was doing its real job, "—— yahi hai. Exactly yahi." Her fingertip hovered one inch above the carved double-cross, trembling on air. "Sapne mein jo dikh raha tha mujhe. Main naap rahi thi — main *naapti aa rahi thi* saaton chakkar bana ke — ye puraasa saat — ek bhaari — ek tiri. Second subah se aata tha mujhe. *Question was wrong*." She corrected herself in English because the Hindi side of her refused to complete the sentence: "Sapne mein bhi main likh nahin sakti thi iska matlab. Lekin shabar — shakl — main apni aankhein band karke dobara bana sakti hoon. Main isko *jaanti* hoon. Is liye mujhe darr nahi lag raha. Zimmedaari lag rahi hai."

"Question kya tha?" Rudra asked, at the entirely ordinary voicing. He had his chin down and his face set into the special mild indifference he wore for some things, and the sharpness one heard in it anyway, the way one hears fire in a matchbox.

"Ye map *kis sheher ka hai*, yeh question tha. Ab —" she touched the shard's edge once, with her nail, the barest contact — "ab lag raha hai, map time ka bhi ho sakta hai. Jisme *samay* bhi zamin hui ho." She glanced up for the first time: "Samajh rahe ho? Ye sheher ka naksha nahi ho sakta. *Ye naksha hai kisi lambi ghadi ki.*"

A silence moved through the four of them like weather through corn.

Aadhya took the shard with her unharmed fingers and turned it slowly.

"Kal raat," she said, "jab maine ghass uthayi — toh mere andar kuch bola tha jise main padh nahi paayi. Ab ye shard mere haath mein hai aur—" her brows came together; the new gold thread on her smallest finger pulsed once, visible for a second under the skin like a minnow in a still pond, and she closed her hand around the bone "—aur ab bol raha hai. Ek awaaz jaisi, jo bahut door se aati hai. Naye panne ki mitti jaisi."

"Kya keh rahi hai?" Veer asked.

"Ek dhyaan," Aadhya said. She accepted her own approximation twice over, then came out with the true word only once: "*Seema.*"

Ira wrote it as one word under a circled heading. Her hand didn't pause. She was the only one among them on whom words continued to have regular measurable properties.

"Boundary," she said quietly, stubbornly hitting the translation to make sure she couldn't become frightened of it: "ya *margin*. Wo shard kisi seema ka upyog karti hai. Yeh dosh nahi — *nishaan* hai. Marking. Kisi ki *hisabdari ka panna* gira hai humlogon ke angan mein."

"Veer," Rudra said.

All of them turned. Veer was squatting with two fingers balanced on the shard's top curve as if on a table of marble, and his face had taken on a look none of them had clocked on him before — not fear, not banter, not his usual bluster — the look of a boy back-arching under the sudden true weight of a memory he'd believed since childhood he had no business keeping.

"Mujhe yeh word pata hai," Veer said at last, slowly. "*Kul.* Main ne kabhi padha nahi hai. Suna hai." He swallowed, and his voice settled low, the way it had when he'd told them his fear on the roof — the voice of a boy who came off a boat from every fear he ever had: "Meri dadi. Jab main chhota tha. Shaam ko chhat pe hum baithte the — chaat — chanda — us samay woh jhoothe nahi bolti thi humko. Ek hi shabd woh bolti thi jisse woh darwaza band karti thi. Yehi."

He raised his eyes to them. In the college's broken-dawn silence, with the watchmen off in distant idleness and no teachers anywhere, the four looked at each other precisely as required, knowledgeable and unprized by any other science:

"Usne bola tha — kuch log hain, beta — barson tak — jo apne bacchon ke naam se pehle ek *shabd* bolte hain. Woh shabd usi bachche ki poori zindagi ka *pat-paddh* hai. Main itna bada hoke use samajhne gaya hun. Main aaj tak nahi samjha. Ab yahin tak samajh aaya hai — ki woh shabd bani hui *ladi* hai — zanjeer nahi — ladi — sookhart latke hue pedon ki." He set his two hands down the way old men close arguments. "Meri dadi ne bola tha woh shabd *pukaarne se* nahi hota. Sirf — *pehchaanne se* hota hai."

"Veer beta," Ira said carefully, "tumhari dadi— humse abhi — milengi?"

And Veer answered exactly what you'd expect from the oldest vocabulary of a character built in weight of neem and water-outlined revisits:

"Dadi ko pehle pata chal gaya hoga. Ghar jaate hi pakode bante honge unke yahan — par baat wahi hoti hai jo woh nikaalengi. Chalo mere ghar."

---
Veer's grandmother's house stood three roofs back from the river steps, behind a neem so large its shadow submitted rent to two buildings. It was the sort of house where the staircase knew the inhabitants by weight.

She was sitting on the chowki on the upper landing with a brass katori of whole wheat, sorting it grain by grain in the sunlight — an act of patience that was in that house not poverty but discipline — and she did not look up when the four of them climbed the stairs behind her grandson.

"Char bacchon ki baraat hai," she said to the wheat. "Jholi nikaal ke aaye ho? Ya kuch lene aaye ho?"

"Dadi," said Veer, and his voice in her house was an entirely different instrument — deeper, slower, centuries back — "dikha do na."

She looked up then. Her eyes went, not to Veer, but straight to Ira — to the windcheater, and the wrist-strip, and by some calculation no one was party to, to the notebook's shape against Ira's ribs.

"Tum ho," she said. It was not a question, and it was not a greeting. It was a ledger entry being read aloud for the first time. "Jo naapti hai."

Ira froze at four steps from the landing.

"Maine sapna dekha tha tumko," the old woman went on, quite practically, in the manner of someone reporting the weather of twenty years ago. "Bahut pehle. Ek ladki dikhayi di thi mujhe, saat bhaago mein banti hui ek bel ke neeche khadi thi. Aur ginti karti thi zor se — sonchti thi tab — ab samajh aayi — kya gin rahi thi woh: *din.*" She gestured with her chin at the free side of the chowki. "Baitho. Sab. Veer — paani laa mehmaanon ko, aur haan, wo matraa waali chai bana."

"Dadi—"

"Baitho."

They sat. The sun made a hard rectangle on the floor and slid. Veer brought steel glasses and the special chai that came out of that kitchen like copper-coloured medicine, thick with tulsi and some rough bitter root the grandmother pounded herself, and when everyone had drunk a first scalding swallow — which was, in her house, a form of signature — she held out her hand.

"Dikhao."

Veer looked at Aadhya. Aadhya drew the shard from the cloth they had wrapped it in, and set it in the old woman's palm.

For a long time the grandmother did nothing but hold it. Holding it, something changed in her posture that none of them had the vocabulary for: she became, without moving at all, *heavier in the room*, the way an anchor becomes heavier when the tide decides something.

"Kahan mila," she said. Not a question. A door.

"College ke aangan se, Dadi. Raat ko wahan kuch—" Veer stopped. Some loyalties, in that house, preceded blood.

"Kuch aaya tha," Rudra finished for him, quietly. "Aur gaya. Wahi gira ke gaya."

She nodded slowly. Then she lifted the shard to the light of the hard rectangle on the floor, and angled it, and read the carving once, and closed her hand over it — closed it so completely that for a second her hand looked like a fist around a coal.

"Kul," she said.

The word in her mouth was not the word it had been on the bone. On the bone it had been a carving. In her mouth it was a bell.

"Mere mooh se is ghara aangan ke baad sunai diya ye shabd — pachpan saal ho gaye honge," she said. "Pacos — pachpan." She was briefly and visibly an old lady across the far corner of memory, and then she returned. "Is shabd ko hum bachpan mein sunte the isi ghar mein, Veer ke par-dada ke mooh se. Aur bade hote hote humein samjha diya gaya: *yeh shabd galiyon ka nahi hai.* Ye shabd un logon ka hai jinka hisaab zameen se zyada gehri kitabon mein likha jaata hai."

"Dadi," Ira said, and her voice was the most careful version of itself anyone had yet heard, "aap bata sakti hain — kul *kya* hota hai?"

The old woman looked at her for a very long second — measuring, the way she measured wheat.

"Sikhaayenge," she said finally. "Sikhaaungi main jo meri maayake ne mujhe sikhaaya tha. Lekin aaj nahi." She held the shard up once more to the light, and her mouth worked for a while at nothing — and then she gave it back, directly into Aadhya's bandaged palm, closing the girl's fingers around it with her own. "Aaj sirf itna. *Kul* ka matlab hai: *dharaa.* Jo dharaa tumhe rok ke rakhti hai, aur jo dharaa tumhe beheti hai. Danw paerhne ki jagah hai ye shabd. Ise galat jagah bolne wala —" her eyes made a slow round of the four of them, and somewhere in the round, not hurriedly, she included the neem outside and the river behind the neem — "— usko wahan mandir ke raaste bheed milti hai jahan aadmi akela hi jaata hai."

She turned to her grandson last of all.

"Veer. Teri taang ke baal jhadne lage the jab tune jaangh pe bhaar daala tha race ke din, aur tu mujhse chhupaaya kar raha tha." She said it with the flat precision of a gauge. "Ab bol. Us din se tere haath mein jo girvi hai woh phir kisi se sambhalti hai ya nahi?"

Veer looked at his own broad hand, wrapped in its wrist-band of gold threads only Ira among them was old friend enough of to see in daylight.

"Sambhalti hai, Dadi," he said. "Kaaj ke saath."

"Aur bolta hai woh haath?"

"Bolta hai. Peeche nahi hat-ta."

"Hmph." She nodded once, a storekeeper accepting goods. "Toh ab dhaarilo khatam: jo gin rahi hai wo zyada bhaari baith gayi hai uske haath par. Uska hisaab sharir ko dena padta hai. Hanuman ji ko naa-paaune ka ilaaj nahi aaya tha, unko parbat uthaana aaya tha. Par utha ke dikha — *wahi ilaaj tha.*" She absorbed Veer's whole silence and then she turned back to the wheat with an air of a woman formally adjourning court.

"Parso aana," she said. "Sab ke sab baithna chhat pe mere. Aur likhwa lena apna naam meri raaz waali diary mein — us din se jo pakadna shuru karoge." Her fingers resumed over the wheat, grain by grain. "Aur suno. Jo shard mila hai tumko — dikhana kisi ko nahi. Usme kaaeta hua jo shabd hai — woh kisi gaayab aadmi ka pehchaan-patra hai. Aur gaayab aadmi se zyada khatarnaak —" she nodded to herself, as one confirms an invoice — "gaayab *parivaar* hota hai. Kyunki kunba kabhi poori tarah gaayab nahi hota. Basant rukh sirf — apni chaal badal leta hai."

Outside, down the staircase that knew them by weight, the four young people descended into the afternoon carrying a word that was too heavy for their pockets, and not one of them noticed — although Ira, at the last turn, paused a half-breath on the step, without being able to give it a name — the exact temperature of pride and dread with which a boy climbs down out of his grandmother's sky-port knowing she had looked at his gold-threaded wrist and priced it, exactly, at blood.

---
That night Rudra told no one anything particular. He had a gift, inherited and exercised, for the posture of a person who had already filed the matter under *settled*: he walked the others as far as the Assi crossing with his hands in his pockets, made the circular joke about the chai's bitter root with exactly the right amount of mocked self-pity, said "kal milte hain, pakka normal sunday" — the crew's new euphemism was three days old and already threadbare from affection — and went home by the short lane as indifferently as a letter posted.

He was home by eight. He lay down by ten. At eleven-fifteen, when the household's clocks had all consented to darkness, he got up again without a sound, put on the kurta he had thrown over the chest-board, and went out through the side door that Ram's house kept for deliveries.

The city at midnight was a different trustee. The ghats slept upright, the lanes sweated their lamps out one by one, and the temple-tanks lay as flat and black-led as overturned bowls. Rudra walked it with the stride of a boy reentering a document. He took the long way past the Panchganga stair and read its darkness the way he now read everything — once with eyes, once with the bare skin of attention — and the buried heart-knock answered twice out of nine empty beats, which was, he had learned, its way of saying: *recorded.*

The library was closed, had been closed six hours, and the gate lodge was dark. He stood across the street from it, in the shutter-noon of a paan stall's dropped flap, and looked up at the high black clerestories of the reading hall, and weighed, with the flat arithmetic his father had taught him without ever naming it, the exact price of what he was about to not do.

He did not cross the street. He was about to not do a thing — that was all. Somebody else was awake in the chains of this, and the somebody was not a ghost: he had seen — and kept seeing, in his head's reruns — a word cut into bone, and the word had two syllables, and they belonged, by some deed of blood no librarian could countersign, to the blank place in his own register.

The line of the street gave him back nothing. A dog passed without any hurry at all. A night cart with covered wheels rested under the awning of the shut paper shop in a posture more of hiding than of rest, and it was only when he had looked at it for the third time that Rudra's eyes went up from the cart's axle to the covered upper frame, and met, through the half-gap between tarpaulin and frame, the single maintained shine of an eye that had been arranged, absolutely deliberately, to be only half-hidden.

The eye did not drop. It observed him a section longer than comfort, and then, before it closed, it did the thing his whole life had trained him to do with doors and with silence: it *did not hurry.*

Rudra stood in the paan stall's shade for the count of ten rainless heartbeats. He counted them. Then he walked home by the long way, the way that made three turns and let him stop twice, empty-handed, for water; and the cart, when he risked the final turn's wall-mirror of a dark shop glass, was gone, and its wheel-marks began exactly nowhere on the dust.

He wrote down nothing. He narrated the whole thing to himself in his mind, in the third person, as though keeping minutes for a meeting of one, and at the end of it he concluded — privately, coldly, in a way that would have frightened his mother to the floor if she had heard the tone of the silence it was concluded in — *so they are already watching the library too,* and the word he was not saying, the word with the two syllables and the bell in it, moved in him with the slow swing of something that had already decided to live in his house whether he learned its name respectfully or bled for it later.

Home. Side door. The chest-board. He lay down.

Across the courtyard's well of dark, on the far side of the house where the night reached first, Ram Yadav lay awake on his rope-bed facing the open door of the store-room, in which, folded in oilcloth and stacked in plain view behind the awls, sat thirty-eight years of the explanations he had sworn to die without speaking. He had not slept during his son's excursion. He did not speak of it the next morning either. This was the second lie that the house, in its whole history, had ever successfully kept from its mother.

---
Sunday morning the jamun went in.

It had rained a little in the night, not enough to report, exactly enough to make the courtyard's blind corner yield under the spade. Rukmini sat on a string charpoy pulled forward to the planting site with her sore foot on a wooden stool, in command, with a steel lota of river-water for the anointment and a list of instructions that had, within the first hour, abandoned any pretence of being horticultural and become simply a roster of everything she had wanted for years to say to a yard of earth with an audience.

"Neeche patthar," she directed. "Uske oopar gobar rakho — itna hi, dono taraf se. Ab paudha — dheere — Rudra, zara peeche — jadd na dab jaye." She caught herself — a pause with no object — and revised, in the direction of the sky over the compound wall: "Chalo. Ab paani. Thanda nahi chahiye — aaram se... bas."

Aadhya arrived at eight with a box of sweets procured on the half-empty excuse of having received very good internal-marks news, which her mother had faithfully forwarded by messenger that morning. Ira arrived at eight-fifteen with her notebook and camped cross-legged on the second charpoy. Veer arrived slightly after eight-fifteen with his grandmother's blessings in the shape of a dhoti still smelling faintly of camphor — he had carried them in secret across five lanes and delivered, with the panache of one who is winning at deception, their ordered portion as: "Dadi ne bheja hai, Mausi — bole, parso sab ke sab wapas aana, *jamun ke neeche* baithenge." (This was the alibi. It was the best alibi had been produced in that city in a decade: it could be confirmed, and confirming it would taste of tulsi.)

Rudra dug. Rudra held the sapling upright while the soil was negotiated back around it in layers, and Veer, meticulous for once without being asked, worked the water in, bowl by brass bowl, around the new roots in the posture of some ancient apprentice of rivers — and Aadhya came and placed her bandaged hand over the seam between sapling-stem and soil at the very end, with no one assigning her to it, out of all of the conventions invented for the morning by nobody: and the crew stood with them, and their shadows crossed each other's on the upturned soil, and for the proceedings of the whole universe there did not exist at that instant any digit of time that could have been called dull.

"Oye," said Veer abruptly, halfway down the water row. "Ira. *Ginti* bol de aaj. Kitne pe khade hain hum?"

She looked up at him from the second charpoy without lifting her whole head; she measured the sky the way she measured everything for three seconds beyond politeness, and said, in a voice flattened so exact into actuality that it almost did not have any fellow loneliness to answer on the sound-skin, it was an angle to hold the festivities upright for:

"Chaar, aur nishaan se aadha udhar" — she did not count the other three's expressions — "kal ki subah aayi count mein paanch se chhaar ho gaya tha. Aaj subah phir chhaar hi the."

Her eyes, when she raised them from the sky, were dry.

"Ginti ke bahane keh rahi hoon," she said, exactly flat. "Ruke hain chaar din. Mela ek din gira diya tha humse. Phir chhe rahe. Phir paanch. Ab chaar — aur chaar aaj subah bhi *chaar hi* the. Matlab jo khinch raha hai usko aaj aaram mila — kisi din ka sukoon bhi usko deta hai kuch to. Isliye aaj ka din rukke rakha humne. Isliye paudha laga rahe hain, murti nahi. Paadhe yaad karte hain. Murti pooja karti hai."

Her voice did not break, and none of the others made it break. Rukmini, who had been watching the sky over the wall through this whole explanation and had no other explanation for it than the plain one — that the children knew something heavy was coming and were piling their joy against the door of it while it could still be stacked — rose slowly from the charpoy and came and stood with her hand on Ira's head, as on a temple bell, and the old and the young of the house stood in the courtyard together with everything that joins everything and this is more than either of the developers of the world know how to rehearse.

The water was worked in. The leaves' beginnings lifted an inch, and returned.

"Done tha course of the day," Rukmini said, rising with effort from her stance of the hand on Ira's head, slipping into the cost accounting of breakfast: "ab nashta. Chai. Poori." She put her hand flat against Rudra's arm for a second in passing and said, into his ear only, smiling, in the language the house used at all other table-services: "Kal bhi kal aata hai. Aaj aaj hai, beta."

He nodded at her. And because she was who she was, her eyes went up for the one fragment of a second that comprehended the whole measure of him — followed the wrist, the bandage on the right-hand knuckles, the shadow that had carried away over his sleep from Friday night — and came back. It said nothing. It reserved the right.

The sun rose over everyone, absolutely without participation in anything whatsoever.

---
Sunday afternoon they took the shard up to the roof — Veer's roof, out of habit's gravity — and sat with it in the mild sun with the euphemistic air of a committee no one had convened.

"Dadi ne bola tha," Veer said, turning the shard's wrapped bundle on his knee, "gaayab parivaar." He paused over the word as one pauses over a hot rim. "Main baar-baar soch raha hoon: haddi mein shabd kaata gaya, aur shabd ka matlab hai *dharaa* — yaani kisi dhara ka tukda." He tipped the cloth open an inch and looked at the carved Kul without touching it. "Toh jis cheez par ye likha hai — woh cheez kisi ki *dharaa* thi. Kisi ki zimmedari ka tukda tha. Ye shard mere hisaab se gift nahi hai — *transfer* hai. Kisi ne gira ke gayi. Ya chhod ke gayi. Angoothi girne se angootha nahi mitta."

"Wo sabse door jaati baat hai," said Ira. She had both the dream-map square and the shard side by side on the parapet, in a composition she kept adjusting like a surgical tray. "Main match kiya rahi hoon. Naksha ka centre-stroke aur ye bone ka cross — mil rahe hain *nau dasamlav nau nau percent.*" She looked up. "Matlab meri raaton wali drawing — woh main bana nahi rahi thi. Woh mujhe *bheji jaa rahi* thi. Isi khopdi se. Ya isi se mili hui kisi aur khopdi se."

"Toh tune ab tak poori map kyun nahi banayi?" Rudra asked.

His tone was light. Ira answered seriously, because seriousness had begun, with both of them and the other two, to be the only tone that finally cost nothing:

"Map poori karte hi andar darr ka ek joog jagta tha," she said simply. "Socha tha apni neev-aparichit si zindagi chalegi aise hi. Ab pata hai khatam karke dekhna hoga. Isliye — parso se poori karti hoon. Kal nahi, *parso se.*" She returned the shard to Aadhya with the ceremony developed between them over one weekend: two hands, eye-level, no apology. "Isko sambhalne ka haq tumhara hai ab, Aadhya — tumhari nazar ne ise dhool se uthaya tha."

Aadhya closed her hand around the cloth. "Maine mummy ko kaha hai koshish kar rahi hoon scholarship ki," she said, almost to herself — and everyone knew, without anyone's saying, that she meant she had begun telling her mother *true sentences whose truths hid in different rooms*, the fire-escape of daughters.

Rudra leaned with his back to the water-tank's curve and contributed, through the whole afternoon, his complete catalogue of one nods, two affirmative grunts, and one good joke. Of the midnight lane, the covered cart, the eye arranged to be half-seen, the wheel-marks beginning nowhere, he said on the roof not one word. He rehearsed telling them four times, and each time some watchman stationed behind his ribs — appointed nineteen years back, permanent staff — put the file back in the drawer.

It was his first secret from them. He had all of nineteen years' stock of other people's secrecy to copy the shape of it from, and the fit was excellent, and he hated it with a cleanliness he did not yet have a category for. At the edge of the roof, when the others were laughing at Veer's imitation of his grandmother measuring wheat like a station-master, Aadhya passed him on her way to the stairs and said, without stopping, without looking, entirely to the air between them:

"Kal bataana jab taiyaar ho. Jhoothe theek-aane se bura hai."

He was still composing a face when she was gone down the hatch, and the face he found was the one his father wore at doors.

Monday, the last suspension before the attempted universe of the week, began at five AM.

The old training ground stood two bends behind the last ghat, in a bend the river had abandoned in some ungrieved century: a walled dust-yard with two lamp-posts, one dead neem, and a padlocked pavilion whose key the municipality had lost in 1974. Ram had discovered it, or it had made itself available to him; for nineteen years it had received, twice a week before dawn, exactly one man and one boy and had agreed, by a treatise of dust, to forget them both by sunrise.

Rudra arrived at five with his cheek cooled by fog and found his father standing in the ground's exact centre with two lathis planted upright in the earth like a gate, and no tape table laid out — no stool, no cloth, no roll of bark-tinged wrap.

The tape was not there.

"Aaj andar se karte hain," Ram said.
