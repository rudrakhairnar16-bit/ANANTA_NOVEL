# CHAPTER FIFTY — THE EIGHTH GATE

---

> *"Ginti ka kaam ginna nahi hai — ginti ka kaam ginne wale ko dena hai."*
>
> *— THE WATCHER'S TABLE-BOARD, Turanan, folio of the second hand (final precept)*

---

*(Counting's work is not the counting. Counting's work is to hand the counter onward.)*

---

The morning after Rudra's return opened like every morning — the hullers, the standpipe tea, the deora-cocks, the third lamp's day-shift, the pit-boys dousing their knuckles in karha water to harden them, the habit below breathing four-in four-out against the underside of everything. Nothing announced itself; the district's grammar had outgrown announcements. But the *keeper of arithmetic*, in the windowless room behind the coalsmoke-hut, sat down this day without opening his ledger, and this was such a breach of thirty years' practice that the *second* keeper — his assistant (whose whole qualification was *that she never once knocked*) — actually knocked, and found him with both palms open on the desk, and the ledger's green thread lying beside them, unknotted.

"Ghar aa gaya," said the keeper, without looking up. *(It came home.)*

"Kaam khatam?" she asked. *(Work finished?)*

"Kaam shuru," he said. *(Work beginning.)* And he took up the green thread and — to the open page — tied a *closing* knot, the kind the district's tally-men tie in grain-knots when the season's tally is settled *but not emptied*, and pushed the ledger across the desk toward her, and said the sentence every predecessor-in-office says once in a lifetime and no assistant is ever ready for:

**"Yeh tumhara hai. Mera hisaab unnees baras tha. Ab tumhara shuru. — Aur suno: pehli entry mat likhna. Pehli entry khud likhi hai."**

*(This is yours. My account was nineteen years. Now yours begins. — And listen: do not write the first entry. The first entry is already written.)*

When she opened the ledger to the first fresh page, she found he was right. It was written there, in handwriting she did not recognize — handwriting she would not *learn* to recognize for another seven years, when the same hand turned up on a patrol-report from a district she had not yet been taught to reach — and the handwriting said:

**"Aath. — Chal raha hai."** *(Eight. — It is running.)*

Below the sentence, where the tally-column should have been, was not a number but a name. She does not tell us the name. The register does not ask names from anyone whose ledger has just changed hands; the register's ninth precept says the keeper's first withheld entry is the keeper's own. But the register holds it in reserve, and the district — though no door was knocked on and no drum was beaten — knew by evening that the seventh gate had an eighth opposite it, and that the counting arithmetic was now carrying two ledgers where for nineteen years it had carried one.

---

It was Ram who saw the second ledger's *effects* first, because Ram was the district's old hand at what registers don't say. The register has been careful all along to give Ram very little; the register now pays something back. Ram Vashisht had that morning gone to the west-gate whitewash tally, as he went every morning of the siege eleven days running, and found the beam *unchanged* — where the gate-keeper had entered "Aaj aaya: kuch nahi" *(come today: nothing)* — Ram stood and read it both times, and understood, with the slowness of a man whose whole life had been an exercise in arriving early, that the word *aaya* now had two meanings, and the district was going to spend the next nineteen years finding out whether the second one was *good*.

Then he did the thing his register-file has always known him to do: he didn't write anything. He walked home through the chauk, past the ghee-stained window, up the haveli's three steps, past the second verandah where Sitara was checking lamp-ash, and found Rukmini standing in front of the kitchen fire, holding flatbread like an answer.

"Usne ginti de di?" he asked.

"Tumne ginti *le li*?" she asked back, which was — from her — how *good morning* sounded on the morning the account changed hands.

"Nahi," Ram said. "Ginti uske paas chali gayi. Toh ab ginti *humare paas* kuch nahi rakhti." *(No. The count went off with him. So the count keeps nothing, with us, any more.)*

She set the bread down exactly in the middle of the thaali, and said — for the register's and the district's and the reader's benefit, since all three are owed a clear sentence at this hour — the sentence the register now *hands on*, because Rukmini's sentences have, all through this book, been the only sentences that never need to be transcribed again:

**"Toh ginna band. Milna shuru."** *(Then stop counting. Start meeting.)*

---

That meeting — the first meeting *not as counting* — happened that evening at the haveli's west window, with no lamp between the people who came. Rudra sat on the floor, where during all these years nobody had let him sit, because household duty is the district's finest arithmetic and he had been under it since before he could pronounce his own full name. Sitara trimmed the wick. Rukmini held the flour-sieve, motionless. Ira stood by the door. Veer stood by the other door — Veer always held two walls at once; it was his manner of keeping-books. Aadhya, who came in last, sat directly across from Rudra, close enough to see that the flutter near his lip — the seven-stamp the habit had pressed there, in the arithmetic-arithmetic of the below — had *not* gone away with the light of day.

"Woh nahi gaya," Rudra said, when he finally spoke, and the register enters his sentence *unmetered*, which is how the register enters a heartbeat: **"Woh mujhme baitha hai. Kabhi-kabhi ginti ke bahane — mujhe uthata hai."**

*(It did not leave. It sits inside me. Sometimes, on the excuse of counting — it picks me up.)*

Nobody answered for one full breath. The register, whose business it is to supply grammar at the moment grammar is wanted, supplies only this: the *house* answered, through its seventeen roof-beams, which creaked all together, once, *upward* — the roof-beams' own four-count, upward — and then were still. And the register notes — because it must, because it is the register — that all five people inside the haveli heard the roof-beams *count*, and none of them asked what was being numbered. The district's thirty-one years had taught them when a number belongs to them and when it is only passing *through*.

Finally Ira, from her ledger, whose pages were still clean, read the eighth line — the line the ledger had kept unwritten for her since the night the lamp at the east window of the haveli stopped being necessary:

**"Woh pehle se tha. Tumne use baitne diya."** *(It was there before. You let it sit.)*

The smallest gasp — Sitara's, unnoticed by herself — was the total grant the district's keeper-of-practice would give. The line trembled her hand one notch, and she made one more charcoal entry on the table's underside — *one*, this time, not five or eleven or *seven* — so that the table top had, one over everything, a single notch — and when Rudra stood up, *he stood up on seven*, and when the register looked, afterward, at where he had been standing, the notch in the table's underside had *become eight*.
