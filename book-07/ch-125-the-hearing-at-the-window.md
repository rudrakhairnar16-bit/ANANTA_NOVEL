# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-FIVE — THE HEARING AT THE WINDOW

---

> *"We did not all reach the window a second time.*
> *Our question did. We had to learn the difference."*
> *— Ira's later register, the hearing without the four*

---

"Jaaongi," Sitara said. "Isi sawaal ke liye. Tumhe lene waapas nahi aaongi."

Rudra heard the answer before the conditions could become a more comforting promise.

She would take the limited question after the morning check, if the people and return arrangements could be made. She would say whose question it was and who was actually standing before the ledge. If they were not heard, that too would be part of the account she brought back.

Vedant made his own choice aloud. He would go for the same short inquiry, with the care and walking limits he had described. Not to demonstrate that either mentor could still do without help.

Rudra thanked them without adding another request.

The nurse's two minutes had nearly gone. They ended the call, and he let her settle him before the evening care. He did not remain awake to supervise a journey from its first telephone number.

---

The arrangements belonged to a new booking.

Sitara spoke to the ordinary travel contact after her own rest. The quoted vehicle had six separate occupied belt places, counting the driver, with room to secure the small bags and folded rest stool. No bed was requested. The driver was told the route, the waiting place, the latest outward departure and the return destination before accepting the day.

One attendant agreed to carry the stool, supplies and plain question cover. Two qualified clinical workers accepted separate roles: one along the short inspected passage, the other remaining Earth-side with the vehicle. Both heard that calls had failed beyond the first bend. Neither was being sold a road-only appointment and discovering another realm after payment.

The accepted block ran from half past seven until half past three the next day. The quote included their day fees and the vehicle's return, not an unlimited wait for an answer. A delayed hearing would be missed before the return margin was spent.

Sitara checked the available reserve and the three authorised contributions with the people who had offered them. Each agreed to the actual amount within the quote; the transfers were received into the travel account before she paid the deposit. The booking contact acknowledged that payment against tomorrow's balance. Treatment money stayed where it belonged.

She sent the accepted practical arrangement to the receiving contact and to Dhruv. Both acknowledged it after their own duties allowed. The return check for the two mentors had a place and staff who knew the current plan, not only a promise that somebody would be around.

No worker from the previous day was summoned by being mentioned in an old receipt.

---

On the hundred-and-eighty-sixth morning, the current checks came before pickup.

Vedant reported the forearm's unchanged altered feeling and the absence of a new problem after rest. Sitara gave the covered hand's condition. Neither was cleared for lifting. The planned short walking work remained within the reviewed limits, with the accompanying clinician authorised to stop it if the findings changed.

They ate, took the prescribed care and waited until the new staff had received the actual handover. The jobs began with names and roles spoken between the people doing them.

The receiving worker handed Sitara the plain cover only after checking Rudra's instruction. The original question and the additional sheet were both inside. Sitara read the outside contact, checked the two sheets and put the cover in a dry pocket she could use without loading the protected hand. The attendant carried the rest of the small supplies.

Rudra was resting after his morning care. Nobody woke him to ask whether they should keep an agreement already made.

The vehicle arrived at half past seven.

Before departure, its driver showed the six actual belted places. The stool and bags were secured clear of the passengers. The two mentors took the supported positions checked for them; each clinical worker and the attendant used a separate belt. The driver fastened his own last.

There was no chair bay this time, and no empty place somebody expected an injured friend to fill at the last moment.

They left at quarter to eight.

---

The road was the same road, not shortened by the smaller party.

Beyond Mirzapur, shutters were rising outside repair shops. A school van stopped where a child was still being hurried into shoes. Sitara watched the delay with an affection which did not require the morning to know where she was going.

At the agreed rest stop the driver parked clear. The workers checked the ordinary needs of the passengers, and nobody used the pause to propose saving time by leaving the care behind at the vehicle.

They continued only after everyone was seated and belted again.

The ridge came into view at twenty past nine. The driver took the agreed firm waiting place, turned while there was room and stopped with the road out still clear. The Earth-side worker checked the phone connection there and gave the arrival report to the receiving contact.

Only then did the attendant take the folded stool out.

Across the short strip of scrub, the natural opening looked no more like an appointment than it had two days before.

---

At twenty-five past nine, all four people going on the inspection named their limits again.

Vedant and Sitara would check the known line in both directions. The attendant would carry the stool and supplies, not be asked to lift either mentor over a problem. The accompanying clinical worker would stay with them. If the short walking inspection itself used the effort available for the morning, there would be no second crossing for the hearing.

The Earth worker and driver remained outside. They knew the inspection's return time, the later visit's maximum and the road departure: out of Swarg by half past eleven, leaving the approach no later than quarter past twelve. A morning appointment was not allowed to borrow the afternoon's way home.

They entered on ordinary feet.

At the tighter bend, Vedant stopped on the broad ground before looking around it. The floor was clear. Sitara examined the shallow slope in the onward direction without using her covered hand on the wall. The attendant followed with the folded stool close enough not to strike the rock.

There was no latch to operate, no sign waiting for a lineage.

Beyond the bend, the attempted phone call failed again.

They went only as far as the familiar first court and the window's shaded waiting strip. The bright paving was clear, the turning space unchanged. A worker was drawing a newly washed length of cloth along a line; water darkened the stone beneath its hem.

Sitara looked at that ordinary wet patch before taking a step nearer. The clear way to the awning remained on its other side.

They did not follow the cloth into an uninspected room.

---

The return showed the slope from the other direction.

They walked it, checked the footing at the two bends and came back to Earth at two minutes before ten. The waiting clinical worker received their present accounts before they began discussing the view.

Vedant's arm had stayed supported. The familiar finger feeling was unchanged, no new pull from trying to steady another person. Sitara reported the same covered-hand condition and ordinary walking fatigue, not a reason to begin carrying the supplies herself. The attendant put the stool down where the Earth worker could offer a real rest.

The phone connected beside the vehicle. The Earth worker sent the completed inspection report through the agreed contact and received its acknowledgment. Four had returned; the later crossing had not happened yet.

They drank and sat through the rest they had planned.

Sitara took the cover out only to check that the sheets were dry. The attendant offered to hold it while she rearranged the pocket. She accepted, then asked for it back before he went to move the water. Neither had to pretend that a small paper was too sacred for ordinary help.

At twenty past ten, the accompanying worker checked them again and asked separately whether they wanted the second walk.

Both said yes within the same short line and return limit. The attendant and worker also chose to continue the accepted visit. Nobody's earlier agreement had been used to silence the new question.

They set out again at twenty-five past.

---

This time the hearing waited at the end of the checked route.

They paused at the broad place without making a new ceremony of it, then continued at the slower pace which kept the two mentors comfortable. At the opening, the light had shifted a little. The wet cloth now hung clear of the ground, lifting at the bottom whenever air moved along the court.

The bench stood where it had before. Nobody was kicking a bucket beside it this morning. That did not improve what had happened there two days earlier; it merely meant the place held a different minute now.

Sitara found she had been looking for the woman who had made room for the friends. There was no reason the woman should spend every morning beneath the same bundle. Sitara thanked the empty place silently and let the absence stay ordinary.

They reached the window at twenty to eleven.

The receiver was finishing with a person who wanted to know where a delivered bundle had been placed. He pointed towards another waiting area, called for the answer rather than guessing, and returned to the ledge only after the person had understood.

Then he looked at the four visitors.

"Woh ladka?"

"Aaj care mein hai," Sitara said. "Yeh usi ka sawaal hai. Hum saath aaye the. Unki permission se yeh chhota byora laaye hain. Apni jagah unhe khada hua nahi bolenge."

She showed the original copy and its offered time, then the plain cover's contact name. She did not hold up a treatment sheet to make absence acceptable.

The man compared the question with the one he had kept. He read the correction on the additional sheet, where *we* had become *I*, without asking her to make it look cleaner.

"Poochh leta hoon," he said.

He spoke through the doorway behind him. A woman's answer came back, low but clear enough to be an actual reply rather than a meaning the visitors had to find in a gesture.

She could hear the question now.

---

The woman came to the window. The receiver set the question he had kept beside the two sheets Sitara had brought.

Sitara had expected to recognise importance. A different way of making space, perhaps, or the sudden silence with which other people might announce someone they feared. The man at the ledge only moved his stack away from her elbow. A person waiting behind them continued arguing softly about a bundle.

The woman looked at the two sheets Sitara had brought.

"Inki taraf se aaye ho?"

"Haan. Chaaron ka sawaal hai. Yeh byora Rudra ka hai. Hum jo khud jaante hain, woh alag bolenge."

"Baith kar bolo."

The attendant opened the carried stool for Sitara in the checked shade. The receiver brought the other stool nearer for Vedant, who asked him to leave the arm's space clear. The clinical worker stood where she could hear either mentor without blocking the queue.

Nobody had to produce the absent boy to make those ordinary arrangements possible.

At quarter to eleven, the woman read the original question aloud.

The words about understanding the Devtas' work remained. No demand to be taken before a ruler had appeared in their place during the two days the paper waited.

"Us kaam ke saath mera bhi kaam raha hai," she said. "Jitna usmein jaanti hoon, utna bata sakti hoon. Main saaton ki taraf se jawaab dene nahi baithi."

Sitara said that was the kind of account they had come to ask for.

---

They began with what had brought the question there.

Sitara gave the father's limited report without giving his name. A fight long before the son's birth; a statement about failure in the balance; the son hearing it later and wanting to understand it through people familiar with the work itself. She made the extra distance in the account audible instead of smoothing it away.

The woman asked whether the father claimed to have tested every Loka.

"Nahi. Rudra ne unse poochha tha," Sitara said. "Kal usne humein unka diya hua jawaab bataaya. Us hisse ko bataane ki permission hai."

The woman nodded at the distinction and let her finish.

Vedant gave the more recent reason for asking. The young man had been attacked and injured on Earth. Vedant had been present for part of the encounter, then taken away for his own care before it ended. What happened after his departure belonged to the account his student had actually given him.

He did not turn a brief visit into a recitation of someone else's wounds. Nor did he offer the unfamiliar shape Rudra had named as a thing the listener ought to recognise from a description.

"Us hamle se saaton ki haalat tay nahi kar rahe," he said. "Par uske baad sawaal aur zaroori laga."

"Theek," the woman said. "Wajah aur saboot ko ek shabd mat banao."

She set the additional sheet beside the question, where both could be read.

The attendant shifted a little to let the next person reach another part of the counter. His bag remained at his own feet. There was work happening beside the hearing which had not agreed to stop for it.

---

"Santulan sambhaalne ka kaam," Sitara asked. "Uska matlab sirf hamla rokna nahi hai. Yeh samajhna hai."

"Sirf ladaai jeetne ka naam nahi," the woman said. "Saat Lokas ke beech jo santulan hai, use banaaye rakhne ka kaam hai. Ek jagah ko akela dekh kar poori baat nahi ho jaati."

She did not unfold a map or place seven objects on the ledge. The explanation stayed with the work named in their question: a continuing balance among places, not seven separate victories waiting to be entered beneath one person's name.

If a threat in one place had been stopped, that answered something real about that threat. It did not tell the listener that everyone responsible for maintaining the balance had finished their work.

Vedant looked down at the supported arm while she spoke. He knew the temptation to hear a larger duty as a dismissal of the small one already done. He had used it against himself often enough: useful, but not enough; survived, but still behind.

"Jo ladaai khatam hui," he said, "use chhota karke nahi poochh rahe."

"Main bhi use chhota nahi keh rahi."

The answer was given without asking him what strength he possessed. He found he had prepared for that question and had to put the preparation away.

Sitara asked whether *maintaining* meant there was nothing left to learn once an attacker had been resisted.

The woman shook her head.

"Tumne kaam hi toh poochha hai. Kaam ko ek dushman ka doosra naam mat samjho."

A few paces beyond the awning, the wet edge of the cloth tapped softly against its support. Sitara heard it between the woman's sentences and knew she would remember that sound with the answer, though the sound proved nothing about it.

---

Vedant asked about the word which had made the journey necessary.

"Divine Cycle mein nakaami ki baat. Kya uske baare mein aap kuchh keh sakti hain?"

The woman left the question sheet flat beneath her hand.

"Haan. Is vyavastha mein nakaami hai. Santulan ko bina mushkil chalta hua nahi keh sakte."

For a moment neither mentor spoke.

Sitara heard the answer as a second voice, not an improved version of the first. It had not come through Ram, through a son's retelling or through the frightening brilliance of a fight she had not watched to its end. The woman sitting before them had made a statement of her own.

What supported that statement still mattered. But she had actually made it.

"Aap bhi yahi keh rahi hain," Sitara said. "Ki sirf koi use todna chahta hai, itna nahi. Nakaami pehle se hai."

"Haan. Dono baaton ko ek mat karna."

The familiar distinction sounded different in another place. Not larger. Less lonely.

Vedant had not realised how much of the inquiry he had imagined returning with only a more careful way to say they did not know. There was something to carry home already. He did not need to pretend it was everything in order to value it.

---

The receiver reached for a stack beside the woman's elbow and stopped when he saw it would disturb the papers.

She lifted her hand, let him take the stack and waited until the movement finished. Nothing in the conversation required the ordinary person doing the rest of the window's work to become invisible.

"Nakaami hai toh kaam bacha kya hai," Vedant said, hearing the dangerous simplicity as he spoke. "Aisa jawaab lekar bhi nahi lautna."

"Nakaami aur poori tarah khatam hona ek baat nahi," she replied. "Tum khud keh rahe ho ki jo bacha hai, use aur todne ka khatra hai. Us khatre ko chhota mat karo."

She was speaking to the distinction in the account, not assigning a face to the unnamed attacker or declaring the motive of every person who had fought the visitors.

Sitara thought of Rudra asking where learning began. He had not asked for a reason to abandon the people still living within a damaged order. He had asked what stopping someone could not teach him by itself.

She kept the next question within that need.

Could the woman promise what would happen to the whole balance if the danger they had been told about was stopped?

"Nahi. Tum jis nateeje ki guarantee maang rahe ho, woh mere paas nahi."

She did not replace the missing guarantee with its opposite. No rule that resistance would repair everything; no rule that it could repair nothing. She had not tested the future by agreeing to hear a question about the present.

Sitara nodded.

The honest limit did not erase the answer which had preceded it.

---

"Hum do alag baatein lekar lautenge," Vedant said. "Ek, jo kaam aapne samjhaaya. Doosri, nakaami ki baat aapne bhi kahi."

"Aur teesri," Sitara added, "ki usse apne aap theek hone ka jawaab nahi mila."

The woman listened to the beginning of the read-back without asking them to admire how little she had promised. She corrected one phrase when Vedant made the work sound as though it existed only after a fight.

"Banaaye rakhna," she said. "Tumne pehle bhi wahi poochha tha."

He changed the phrase aloud.

Maintenance was not the name for an interval between important people striking each other. It was the work for which the friends had originally asked a hearing. He could see how easily a fighter might hear every other task as preparation for, or cleaning up after, his own.

He did not need to make that habit the shape of the answer.

The clinical worker asked whether either mentor wanted a position change before they continued. Sitara shifted on the stool without leaning through the covered hand. Vedant had the arm's support adjusted, then answered that the old feeling was unchanged.

The woman waited through those answers.

From farther down the court came a laugh, followed by the sharper sound of someone calling for a bundle to be moved out of the way. Sitara could not see who had spoken from where she sat. She left the sound without a judgement attached to an unseen face.

The court had not turned into a lesson staged for them. She was hearing an answer while other people continued having a morning.

---

The attendant looked at the accompanying worker's watch when she showed it to the two mentors.

They were approaching ten to eleven. There was still time for the last few questions, not for an invitation to disappear into another building and begin again.

Sitara glanced at the cover lying beside the sheets. The only name on it was Rudra's, in the worker's large ordinary handwriting. The boy was resting somewhere beyond a failed phone connection, not present to seize the answer and make it mean whatever would let him stand.

She wanted to bring him the statement without that temptation built into it.

They had asked the woman what she knew. Before letting the new voice become a line in someone else's record, they needed to understand where it stood.

---

The woman asked what they intended to tell the people who had stayed behind.

Not what they intended to do with the whole balance. The smaller question, about the words which would leave this window in their mouths.

Sitara gave the answer carefully. The person familiar with the maintainers' work had heard their actual question. She had described that work as keeping the balance among seven Lokas, not merely defeating an attacker. She had also said that failure was already present. She had not promised what would repair it after a threat was stopped.

"Mere kehne ki tarah bataana," the woman said. "Tum saaton ko dekh kar aaye ho, aise nahi."

"Haan."

Permission to repeat the limited answer was real. It did not make the listeners eyewitnesses to everything the speaker knew. Sitara wanted Rudra to hear that he had gained something without inheriting an impossible set of eyes along with it.

Vedant asked for one phrase again, so that the words he remembered would not be entirely his own preferred ones.

The woman repeated the distinction between failure and complete destruction. He listened without trying to turn it into a warning large enough to replace the original question.

There had been a time when he would have thought bringing back an answer required bringing back an instruction. A task, a drill, some next effort which would make the news useful before anyone had time to feel it. He could recognise that habit without appointing it to lead the return.

The four friends had asked to understand. He could begin by giving them the answer before telling them what he thought they should do.

---

A shorter visitor came to the far end of the ledge while they spoke.

The receiver had put the relevant sheet too high. The visitor asked for it lower. He brought it down and held the edge until the person could read without standing on toes, an adjustment so ordinary that Sitara might have missed it on another morning.

She could remember the empty shade beside Rudra's chair and the bright fall beyond the court. Neither sight had required her to decide that every person here would behave the same way.

At this window, the person's height changed where the page was placed.

Two days before, another man's preference had been offered as a reason to move Rudra into the sun.

Knowing that the balance was failing did not tell her who either of those people was. It did not make their separate acts into one act, or make the kind one a receipt cancelling the other.

She watched the visitor take the sheet away.

The woman waiting with them had let that small transaction finish before continuing. Her quiet did not require Sitara to award her a rank or invent her whole character from one patient minute.

Sitara returned her attention to the question they had brought.

---

She asked for their own two sheets back when the woman had finished with them. The receiver's original could remain where it belonged; there was no reason to remove the window's account in order to keep theirs.

The woman put the question copy and the additional first-person sheet together, and slid both towards Sitara. The attendant held the plain cover open while she put them inside with the hand available for light use. He took the cover for a moment while she settled her sleeve, then returned it to the same dry pocket.

No new seal had appeared on the paper. Nobody signed a guarantee beneath the word *balance*.

Vedant did not ask for a document which would make every person on Earth obliged to accept the conversation. Two listeners could describe where they had sat, what they had asked and what the woman had answered. That was a stronger beginning than inventing the authority they wished the page possessed.

The clinical worker quietly gave them the remaining hearing time.

Sitara's legs were comfortable after sitting. Her hand still needed its protection. Neither fact required her to stay until there was a new symptom, and she told the worker she was ready to begin returning once the last source question was answered.

Vedant agreed. No longer audience was being planned around the next doorway.

The attendant closed the water bag and kept the stool available until Sitara actually rose. A decision to leave did not put him at the opening before his work beside the window was finished.

---

There was room for one precise question before the account was complete enough to carry.

Sitara had watched how easily a careful report could lose a person: a father disappearing behind his son's certainty, a friend becoming a witness because a sentence began with *we*. A second voice would not automatically cure the same mistake. If it merely repeated the visitors' account back to them, they would need to say that too.

The woman had not asked them to stop resisting danger. She had not offered a simple replacement for what Ram had told his son. She had said that there was already failure in the order whose work they were trying to understand.

Sitara wanted the next line to be honest about the ground beneath that statement. Not the court's pale stone, not her own relief at having something to bring home. The source beneath the new answer.

She let the empty space after the woman's last sentence remain long enough to ask it plainly.

"Aap jo nakaami keh rahi hain—woh hamaare bataaye hamle se alag bhi aapke kaam mein saamne aayi hai?"
