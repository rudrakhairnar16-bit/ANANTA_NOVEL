# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-FOUR — THE ACCOUNT BENEATH THE ANSWER

---

> *"The source did not vanish when we named the source beneath it.*
> *The sentence became longer. So did our responsibility for repeating it."*
> *— Ira's later register, preparation for the offered hearing*

---

"Adhira ne bataaya tha," Ram said. "Usi ladaai mein."

Rudra listened without reaching for the paper in the cupboard.

His father gave the substance, not a speech recovered whole from the old ground. Adhira had spoken of failure already entering the balance among the seven Lokas, and of the destruction Kaal intended. Ram had heard it in that encounter. He had not travelled through all seven and tested their condition separately before coming home.

"Aapne unse suna tha."

"Haan. Maine tumhe jo bataaya, uska yeh aadhaar hai."

There was no new voice behind the phone. Only his father's, slightly tired now, saying where the knowledge had entered his life. Rudra had wanted the answer to open a view. Instead it placed another person inside the sentence he had been carrying.

He could not remove that person merely because Adhira had been an enemy.

He could not turn an enemy's words into seven places he himself had seen either.

"Toh wahan aise hi poochhoon," he said. "Ki yeh baat ek purane encounter mein aapko bataayi gayi thi. Humne khud saaton ko nahi dekha."

"Waise hi. Aur jo tumne khud dekha hai, use alag rakhna."

The nurse touched the stand to keep it from slipping nearer his elbow. Rudra answered her question about the position before speaking again.

"Aap apni pehli baat waapas nahi le rahe."

"Nahi. Uski had bata raha hoon. Theek hone ka tareeqa us baat se mujhe nahi mil gaya tha."

The distinction stayed small enough to understand. A reported danger did not become unreal when someone asked how it had been learned. Nor did knowing the source make Rudra the person who could promise its repair.

Somewhere along the corridor a cup settled hard against its saucer. Then the room was quiet again.

---

There was very little time left.

Rudra asked whether he could give this basis to the same friends and mentors who had heard the earlier account. Ram agreed. It belonged beside what he had allowed them to hear, not in a separate silence which would make their notes misleading.

"Aur hearing mein?" Rudra asked. "Naam aur ghar ki baat nahi. Bas itna ki mere pita ne ek purani ladaai mein saamne waale se suna tha. Us baat ko wahan ke kaam se samajhna hai."

Ram considered the actual request.

"Itna keh sakte ho. Mera suna hua, tumhaara dekha hua nahi."

No recording of the call was offered. No permission to unpack the rest of a parent's history on a stranger's ledge. Rudra said he would keep the account within those parts, and ask the listeners before asking them to carry it farther.

At the other end, Ram answered someone near his bed.

When he returned, Rudra did not use the interruption to begin another question. The interval had reached its end. His father said he needed the next care, and his son said he would rest too.

"Kal ka jaana abhi tay nahi," Rudra added, then corrected himself. "Parso ki hearing. Mere jaane ka faisla abhi nahi hua."

"Waqt mila hai," Ram said. "Apna shareer uske naam mat likh dena."

Rudra looked at the arm resting above the blanket.

For once his father's warning asked him to keep something for himself.

They said goodnight and ended the call.

---

The nurse moved the phone away and checked the place under his back which the journey had tired. He told her that the ache was familiar, that he wanted a different fold before trying to sleep. The first change was not comfortable. She listened, adjusted it with the other worker and waited for a second answer.

"Ab theek hai."

More comfortable, not cured.

He watched the cupboard door while the workers finished. Behind it lay the ordinary sheet which had survived a realm crossing without becoming a relic. He knew one more thing to put beside its question now. The knowledge was not a new thing the paper could do for him.

His father had survived the old fight before there was a son to inherit its silences. Rudra could not walk into that return and ask the man who had just come home what he had heard. He could ask the man who was alive now, and let an answer remain an answer even when it needed another source beside it.

The room settled into its night noises.

At the next check he woke, answered and slept again. Once the ankle throbbed enough for him to call; the nurse examined the position and followed the plan already prescribed. He did not wait until morning to make the night sound easier.

By dawn the tiredness of the trip had become less sharp, but the back still disliked the same supported angle. His elbow remained swollen. When the clinician came after breakfast on the hundred-and-eighty-fifth morning, Rudra gave those findings before asking about tomorrow.

He had a real time at a real window.

He did not yet have a body which could spend it.

---

The clinician did not begin with Swarg.

She asked about the journey they had actually made: the time seated, the position which had begun pulling, the rest on the far side, the return and the ache afterwards. Rudra gave her the uncomfortable parts as carefully as the successful ones. Getting back within the agreed time had not made the hours weightless.

She examined the elbow and checked what the fingers could do without force. The grip was still unreliable. At the back, a fuller breath brought the old catch; the night’s ankle complaint had settled with the checked support, not with an explanation of the earlier faint.

Tomorrow’s proposed repeat was not within what she could recommend on these findings.

Rudra watched her put the pen down.

"Agar sirf sawaal poochhna ho?"

"Wahan pahunchne aur waapas aane ka kaam phir bhi hoga."

He had known the answer before asking. Part of him had wanted the importance of the errand to alter its weight in the vehicle, to shorten the road or make his back interested in the difference between a question and a sightseeing trip.

It did none of those things.

The next review would be based on what changed, she said. Not on whether the person waiting in another place was important enough. Today’s movement and rest stayed within the current plan. He was not to test the possibility by sitting longer after she left.

"Theek," he said.

It was not a graceful answer. It was an answer she could use.

---

After the examination he lay quietly while the ward continued around him.

A worker came to collect an empty tray. Somebody argued over the television volume and lost by degrees. The comb beside the cupboard looked unreasonably prepared for a day which was no longer happening.

Rudra asked for it to be put with his own things.

"Kal ke liye?" the worker asked.

"Nahi. Bas gum na ho."

She put it away without asking what had been cancelled. He was glad not to explain it once more, then ashamed of the gladness, then tired of making every small feeling into another duty.

He shut his eyes.

He had gone to Swarg. That fact remained. He had disliked a man's certainty that his body could be left in sunlight, and liked the water falling where nobody had built it for his benefit. Staying here tomorrow would not turn yesterday into an accident someone else had lived.

Still, he had wanted to be there when the answer came.

The wanting was allowed to hurt before it became useful.

---

Later he asked for a practical message to go to the others: his own review did not support another journey tomorrow. Nobody should decide their attendance on the assumption that his had already been arranged. He wanted to hear each person's answer when they had one.

The receiving worker read the message back, checked the existing contacts and sent it. It contained no account of the private call. Rudra rested before hearing the replies.

Veer's came first, after his knee appointment.

"Main kal nahi jaaunga," he said. "Knee ka kaam idhar hi hai. Wahan phir jaana hai mujhe. Dono baatein ek saath hain."

There was a scrape behind his voice, a chair being moved by someone else. He waited until the sound stopped before finishing.

"Tum bhi nahi jaa rahe, isliye nahi. Apna bol raha hoon."

Rudra played that part twice, not because the words were unclear.

He sent back that he understood. He did not thank Veer for staying as though his friend's treatment had become a favour to him.

Aadhya answered after her hand care. She wanted tomorrow's review kept where it was. The altered feeling had not disappeared during yesterday's good hours, and she did not want to bargain the appointment against being present for somebody else's answer.

"Jo sunna hai, sunenge," she added. "Haath ko attendance nahi deni."

Rudra smiled, then let the smile go without writing her choice into a group decision.

---

Ira's reply was shorter.

She would not travel tomorrow. Her ordinary eye needed the quieter day she had discussed at review, and she wanted to keep it. She could listen briefly later if they needed to prepare a question aloud. Not write it tonight, not hold a place for herself by producing a record nobody had asked for yet.

Rudra answered each message separately.

The four of them had reached the same practical outcome for different reasons. That mattered more than a sentence saying the team had decided to rest. A team could make it very easy for one person's limit to hide beneath another person's louder one.

He asked whether they would still like the further source-basis answer from Ram. It was permitted, narrow and did not require a meeting. All three chose a voice message to hear after their own rest. Sitara and Vedant could be asked separately when free; Dhruv would not be awakened to complete a list.

The nurse brought his afternoon care before the phone came back.

He told her the back had begun asking for a position change again. She helped arrange it within the plan and checked the protected foot before leaving him with the supported earpiece.

The offered hearing had not vanished.

For the first time since reading the time, Rudra considered whether it might be possible to keep the question without sending his own body after it.

---

He recorded the answer without trying to make it shorter than its source.

"Papa se poochha tha ki unhe kaise pata chala. Adhira ne unhe us purani ladaai mein bataaya tha—mere janam se chhe saal pehle. Papa saaton ki alag jaanch karke nahi aaye the. Yeh unka diya hua aadhaar hai; pehli baat waapas nahi li. Apne aap theek hone ka jawaab ab bhi unke paas nahi. Unki permission se yeh bata raha hoon."

He listened to the playback. The nurse checked the recipients with him: the three friends who had asked for it. No family recording underneath his voice, no nearby patient's name caught in the pause. He approved the send.

Then he asked for separate brief requests to Vedant, Sitara and Dhruv. The same permitted clarification was available if they wished to hear it after their own care. It was not an instruction to join a meeting.

The phone went aside before anyone answered.

Rudra ate. The meal required help, an irritating negotiation with the side of his mouth which still disliked the spoon's edge. He asked for a smaller amount on it and found the rest easier. Nothing he had learned about the Lokas made the request beneath him.

By the time the replies came, he had stopped rehearsing them.

All three mentors had chosen to hear the short account. The receiving worker sent the approved recording to their checked contacts. Their later acknowledgments arrived separately; Dhruv said he would remain with his own leg care tomorrow, not make a field plan from a message. Vedant and Sitara asked for a short conversation later, after the friends had finished preparing what they actually wanted asked.

That was an offer to discuss it. Rudra kept it that size.

---

The four friends found an interval before Ira's next rest.

Audio only. Four phones, not four chairs drawn up in one ward. Aadhya had hers placed where neither hand held it. Veer said he had ten minutes before his next ordinary task. Rudra's nurse agreed to less than that if his back began pulling again.

"Sabse pehle kya poochhna hai?" Aadhya asked.

Rudra began with the enemy's intention. He stopped halfway through the sentence.

"Nahi. Woh Papa ne bataaya. Jiske kaam ki baat poochhi thi, usse pehle wahi kaam samajhna hai."

He wanted to know whether the person at the hearing, from what they themselves knew, could say anything about the balance already failing. Not whether the visitors' wounds looked serious enough to deserve a larger answer.

"Aur unko kaise pata," Ira said.

"Haan."

"Sirf woh bhi kisi aur ka kehna suna dein, toh uski jagah bhi rahe."

Her voice was even. She was not asking that testimony be thrown away until it could become an object on a table. She wanted the next person's account to arrive with the same care they had finally given Ram's.

Veer asked whether they needed to send the notebook.

"Nahi," Ira said at once.

"Maine tumhe bhejne ko nahi bola. Notebook ko bola."

"Usko bhi nahi."

Aadhya laughed, and the little argument released something which had been tightening around the call. They did not need to transport an injured person's whole record to make one question serious.

---

Rudra asked permission to use the recent assault as a limited part of his own account, if someone needed to understand why they were asking now. He would describe what he himself had experienced and label anything a mentor had seen separately. Not put his friends into the encounter as witnesses because they were listening today.

The others agreed to the question staying shared without becoming a shared memory of that fight.

Aadhya wanted another distinction kept.

"Wahan jo bucket waala tha—usko bhi saaton ke santulan ka saboot mat bana dena."

"Nahi bana raha."

"Pata hai. Yaad rakhne ko bol rahi hoon. Mujhe dono yaad hain. Paani girna bhi, girta hua paani bhi."

The kick and the bright fall beyond the court. Rudra understood which two sights she meant. He had been tempted to make one explain the other, to choose a view simple enough to know how to feel about.

"Aur jisne jagah di," Veer said.

"Woh bhi."

Ira asked them to stop describing the glare now. She had begun picturing it too hard. They stopped without turning her request into a reason she should have said no to the whole conversation.

There were only a few useful lines left to settle. Their first question about the maintainers' work. The reported failure, with its route through Ram left visible. The recent assault, not evidence about all seven. A request for the listener's own basis before any conclusion was carried back.

Rudra repeated those lines aloud. Each friend could change their own part; nobody added a demand for a cure before the hearing had even begun.

When the time ended, they let it.

He lay with the room quiet again, no notebook in his hands and no set of signatures beneath the question. The others had still done work with him. He had heard it happen.

---

Vedant began the later call by asking whether Rudra wanted advice or somebody to listen to the question he had already made.

"Dono mein farq hai?"

"Aaj tum bataao."

Rudra gave him the few lines the friends had settled. Sitara listened from her own place, after saying she could stay for this short interval. Neither asked Ira to join again and certify the words.

When Rudra finished, Vedant repeated only the practical part which had changed: none of the four young people was travelling tomorrow. The time at the window existed independently of that decision.

"Aage kabhi phir poochh sakte hain," he said. "Ya hum mein se koi sawaal lekar jaa sakta hai. Dono abhi mumkin baatein hain."

He did not say that the second possibility had already become his duty.

Rudra asked about their own reviews before asking about the journey.

Vedant's forearm still needed protection. The altered feeling in the fingers remained; stronger gripping and lifting were not available because yesterday's walk had gone without a new injury. His legs had tolerated the short route with its pauses. At today's assessment he had discussed another bounded walking visit, including a separate check of the line and a rest between the two efforts.

The clinician had kept it conditional on the same short distance, present findings and an accompanying qualified worker. No carrying, no fight, no longer excursion offered at the far end. If anything changed in the morning, the plan changed with it.

Sitara gave her own account next.

The covered hand was still out of lifting. She could manage the limited walking under the same current arrangement, but would not take responsibility for making an inaccessible room accessible by strength. If the offered window became a flight of steps or an hour standing in the sun, she would leave the question and return.

"Meri wajah se mat jaana," Rudra said.

Sitara was quiet for a moment.

"Tumhaara sawaal sunne ke baad apna faisla kar sakti hoon. Usmein tumhaari wajah bhi hogi. Sirf wahi nahi."

He had been trying to leave her free and had almost forbidden her a reason.

---

They considered the work which would be needed, rather than treating two willing people as a whole vehicle and a care plan.

Ordinary belted seats would suit the proposed passengers. No lying patient, no main chair, no demand to fold a hurt body into the empty place beside equipment. One uninjured attendant could carry the rest stool and the small bags without either mentor using a protected hand as a handle.

They would need qualified help along the inspected line and a separate qualified person remaining Earth-side. Phone coverage had failed past the bend. An Earth worker who could not hear them was not the care accompanying them; an accompanying worker could not also be waiting beside the vehicle.

The driver would be a driver.

All those people would have to accept a new job with its actual hours and charges. Yesterday's fees had bought yesterday's work. Rudra asked Sitara whether the old reserve was still distinct from the money already settled. It was. A new quote would decide what further amount was needed, and nobody's treatment money would be silently moved into it.

Vedant said he could contribute an agreed part of a new short inquiry trip. Sitara offered a part too, subject to the quote. Rudra asked for his own share to come only from the amount he could authorise in the existing travel account. They would check it before a deposit, not invent three payments by naming three people on a call.

It was a small, awkward conversation. Rudra preferred its awkwardness to the ease with which somebody could otherwise have paid and left the obligation unspoken.

The nurse came to check his position. He asked the mentors to wait while he answered her, then admitted he needed a pause before finishing.

They ended this interval and agreed only to another brief contact after rest.

---

In the quiet afterwards, Rudra tried to picture the ledge without his chair beneath it.

He had thought of the question as something the journey had pulled out of him: pain, force, a name from his father, the long effort of arriving presentable enough not to be dismissed before speaking. If someone else could carry it, had he made too much of getting there himself?

The thought was unfair to yesterday and useful for tomorrow.

Going had mattered. Seeing the woman make room beneath her cloth had mattered. The person at the window had spoken to him, not to a form sent ahead. None of that meant the next answer had to demand another journey from the same injured body.

He asked for help drinking before the cup became a test of that conclusion.

Later the worker opened the cupboard at his request. The copied question came out with its fold still deep through the middle. She set it on the stand and read the offered interval aloud again: tomorrow morning, quarter to eleven, at the same window.

No condition on the sheet said that the boy whose name stood beside the question must be brought back hurt.

It did not promise a substitute would be heard either.

That was something the people who went would have to ask honestly when they arrived.

---

The worker brought a clean sheet when Rudra asked, not a clinical form.

He dictated slowly. Their original question came first, unchanged: who they could speak to in order to understand the work of those maintaining the balance. Below it, a short explanation of why they were asking.

His father had heard the claim about existing failure during a fight six years before Rudra's birth. Rudra had received that account from his father, with permission to repeat this limited basis. Neither he nor his friends claimed to have inspected seven Lokas. A recent assault belonged among his own experiences; it had made the inquiry urgent, but did not establish the condition of the whole balance.

No hidden enemy's name went onto the page. No parent's birth name, injured friend's care record or address. He did not need to describe his new form to make the question worth reading.

The worker read the short account back.

"Yahaan 'humne dekha' aa gaya," Rudra said.

She stopped at the line. He had run two sentences together while dictating. Together they made it sound as though the friends had witnessed the recent assault, instead of hearing the part he had chosen to tell them.

They changed it to his own account and read it once more.

It was not an impressive document. Large writing, space left under the final line, one correction with no attempt to hide the old word. Rudra preferred it to a smooth version which would arrive certain of something its speaker had never claimed.

The original copy of the question stayed with it. Two ordinary sheets, not a file of proof.

---

He asked for the pair to be kept ready for Sitara to collect if she chose the errand. Not sent without asking, not handed to an unnamed driver who would somehow know what belonged at a window beyond the phone's reach.

The worker put them together in a plain cover and showed him where they would wait. Rudra asked her to read the contact name on the outside.

His own first name. Nothing more.

That was enough to identify the question. It was not an order for a vehicle to collect him.

He dictated a separate practical update for his parents: his review did not support another trip tomorrow; he was staying in receiving care and resting. The offered question might still be carried by others if those people chose and the arrangements could be made. Nothing had been booked yet.

The message was read back and sent through the agreed family contact. He did not include the friends' findings in his own report. His parents could hear what he had decided without being appointed to approve everyone else's body.

Ram's later acknowledgment was brief. He had heard. They could talk another day after care. Rudra left it there.

---

By the final short contact with the mentors, the sheets were out of sight again.

Sitara had rested after her review. Vedant had settled his forearm before joining, with the phone set down. Rudra told them where the plain cover would be kept, then gave the contents aloud so that the errand could be considered before anyone accepted an object.

They heard the corrected first-person line. The permitted anonymous account from his father. The request to understand the listener's own basis, not obtain a declaration they could carry home as somebody else's command.

Vedant said he would consider the same limited walking work with the current care and helpers, and would not use the offered time to extend the route. He could also listen to the answer, so that one person's memory would not have to carry the whole conversation alone.

Rudra asked him to keep his own observations separate when he spoke. Vedant agreed. He had not stayed beside the bank through everything which happened after his ambulance left. An account from his student did not put him back there.

Sitara asked whether the friends wanted a recording.

"Nahi," Rudra said. "Pehle jis se baat ho, uski permission. Waise bhi humne aawaaz pakad ke laane ko nahi kaha."

A faithful short account would do, with corrections made openly if the two listeners remembered a part differently. If the person at the window would not hear substitutes, the mentors could say so and return. They were not being asked to fetch Rudra out of care to keep the appointment alive.

He said that last part without waiting to be warned.

---

The nurse came near the stand and held up two fingers. Two minutes left.

Rudra nodded, answered that the position was still tolerable and looked at the small dark mark near the ceiling where the fan's movement never quite reached.

Yesterday he had crossed a realm in a chair. Today it felt harder to imagine letting a question leave without him. Not because he believed Sitara would change the words, but because some old part of him still confused being absent with having failed to do the work.

The people at the other ends were not his extra hands. They had their own reasons to listen, their own injuries and an afternoon they were free to keep. He had heard those reasons before making the request. The question belonged to four friends without making any one of them its necessary price.

There was no name to reveal now, no strength with which he could improve the answer. Only a request another person could accept within its limits, or refuse without becoming less trusted.

Rudra let the next words be smaller than an instruction.

"Sitara ma'am, kal hamaare bina hamaara sawaal le jaayengi?"
