# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTY — WHAT WE HAD NOT ASKED

---

> *"The road acquired a pilgrimage before it acquired a destination.*
> *At the bedsides, the account was smaller: who had spoken, who had listened."*
> *— Ira's later register, after the second visit*

---

"Maine suna tha," Aadhya said into the phone. "Par poora nahi."

She had listened to Rudra's message after her hand care, with the phone supported and the volume low. His question had not brought the court back whole. It had brought back the awkward angle of a bucket held while two other people argued about it.

She stopped the recording and began again. The first sentence sounded as though she had heard everything and was withholding the rest.

"Usne poochha tha ki paani bench ke peechhe rakh de kya. Woh maine suna. Tum bhi wahin the. Uske baad usne apne din ke baare mein mujhse kuch nahi kaha."

She was giving the meaning she remembered, not inventing his exact words. She told Rudra that she had asked him to put the bucket down because the water had not spilled by itself. He had done so. Later he had rubbed the cloth over his shoulder, picked up the weight again and gone towards the falling water.

That was where her account of his actions ended.

"Aur kisne uski baat suni, mujhe nahi pata. Humse pehle ya humare baad kis se bola, woh bhi nahi. Main yeh nahi bol sakti ki kisi ne nahi suna. Maine usse uska din nahi poochha."

Outside her part of the ward, a trolley wheel complained at the same place in the floor. She waited for it to pass rather than speaking louder.

"Jo dekha tha, woh galat hi tha," she finished. "Uske baare mein sahi bolne ke liye uski baaki zindagi banaani nahi padegi."

She listened once, found nothing she wanted to correct and sent the reply. Then she asked for the phone to be put aside. The question did not need to occupy the rest of her night in order to have mattered.

---

Rudra heard it the following morning, after the review.

Day two hundred had begun without the satisfaction of a new permission. His back needed the familiar protection; the grip had not grown dependable in his sleep. He answered about the previous evening's pull, the elbow, the supported ankle and knee, and the absence of a new episode like the exertional faint. The team kept the present plan. Not having fainted in bed was not an explanation of why he had fainted before.

There was time for ordinary listening after breakfast and the next comfortable position.

He let the message finish before asking for it again.

On the second hearing he remembered the worker's question. He had heard it himself, then left it beneath the larger indignation: the kick, the visitor objecting, the man by the post trying to make a reason out of where things ought to stand.

He had been looking for a missing speech.

The man had already spoken within the few minutes they shared.

That did not turn a practical question into a full account of the life behind it. Neither did it make the question too small to count as his voice. Rudra could remember it without pretending it was the only thing the worker had ever wanted to say.

"Dobara nahi," he told the nurse when she asked whether to replay it.

He wanted the words to remain ordinary words, not an examination he could pass on the third attempt.

He asked for a brief reply to be recorded while the phone was there.

"Haan, maine bhi suna tha. Tab us sawaal par dhyaan nahi ruka. Tumne jitna dekha, utna bataaya. Theek kiya."

He checked that it had gone to Aadhya, then let the nurse return to the work waiting beyond his bed. He had not asked her to find the worker, promise a visit or keep a running conversation alive while he considered what to think.

The cupboard held the two hearing sheets. Neither contained the answer to this smaller question. They did not become useless because they had not been intended to contain it.

---

Aadhya received the acknowledgment after her own morning review.

The hand still needed watching where sensation could not be trusted. The return from the court had left her tired, not newly injured; the clinician checked that distinction rather than accepting the word fine. She let the care finish before asking about seeing Rudra for a few minutes later.

Not another drive. Not a passage.

The rooms were in the same facility. Even so, wanting the visit was not the same as knowing what walking there and back would cost today.

She asked for that question to be considered in the current plan.

Veer, told separately that she was asking, wanted to come if his own review allowed it. He did not offer to carry anything. Rudra was asked whether he wanted visitors, and said yes after checking that they meant an actual small visit, not a fresh meeting about what to do next.

"Bas milne aana," he said. "Aaj kisi ka kaam samajhne ki hearing nahi hai."

Aadhya smiled at the message. For once, the smallest description of a plan sounded like the most inviting one.

The ward team found a small interval before the next round of care, not an empty morning.

Aadhya walked the short agreed stretch with the worker, stopped where they had said she would stop and returned for the current check. Her hands had not been used to steady another person or manage a door. Veer's review included the assisted transfer and the few steps already allowed in his plan. The knee tired; he said so before somebody could mistake a grin for an answer.

For the visit, they agreed on a ward chair.

"Main aaya toh wheelchair mein meri entry par comment mat karna," he told Aadhya.

"Entry par nahi. Kal ke exit par material hai."

The worker waited until they had finished, then asked Veer the practical question again. He answered it this time without a joke. They could make the visit with the chair and help, if the receiving place was ready and he still wanted it after rest.

He did.

An attendant accepted the chair work for the outward movement and return within a twenty-five-minute interval. A ward worker would accompany them; the visit itself could take no more than ten. The arrangement belonged to their current ward care, with its ordinary account and duties, not to yesterday's hired outing. Neither of the clinical workers who had already left was called back to make a social morning possible.

Rudra's nurse confirmed the space and his present position. His care would not be postponed to finish a story. Aadhya and Veer rested before leaving; the attendant checked the chair and took the bag which actually needed to travel. There was no box of court mementoes inside it.

Ira was asked separately whether she wanted to hear them for a few minutes.

She chose audio after her own care, from where she was resting, with the left covering undisturbed and the ordinary right eye closed. The choice did not make the short corridor trip obligatory for her. A worker accepted the brief phone setup at each end and a time to stop. No friend had to keep an injured hand under a phone so the others could pretend nobody had needed help.

---

They reached Rudra after eleven.

The attendant turned the chair where the receiving worker indicated, clear of the bed and the way through. Aadhya sat on the offered chair rather than the edge of the mattress. The accompanying report was very short. Rudra's nurse acknowledged it, checked that Veer was comfortable in the agreed position and told them when the ten minutes would end.

It was not the sort of entrance Rudra had imagined when he first heard they had gone without him.

No dust from an impossible road. No glowing object wrapped in the scarf. Veer had a crease pressed into his sleeve and the expression of somebody who knew what the first joke would be.

"Board dikha," Rudra said.

"Wahin chhod diya."

"Evidence hata diya."

Aadhya looked at him. "Bachchon ki cheez thi. Teri inquiry ki property nahi."

"Meri inquiry bahut unfair ho gayi hai."

Veer settled a little more comfortably with the worker's help before answering.

"Usko bhi lagta. Haraaya usne, aur mujhe yahaan interview mil raha hai."

The phone connected to Ira's accepted call. She could hear everyone; she said the volume was enough. Rudra greeted her without asking whether she could see them. There was nothing in the room which required her to prove she could.

"Interview nahi," she said. "Post-match excuses."

Veer denied having brought any. Then he began explaining that he had noticed the girl's intention one move too late.

"Bas," Aadhya said. "Excuse aa gaya."

Rudra's laugh started too broadly. He let it go small before it caught the back, told the nurse when she asked and waited while she checked his present comfort. Yesterday's pull had been enough instruction; he did not need another one to make the joke authentic.

---

For a minute the argument was only about whether seeing something late counted as nearly seeing it in time.

Ira said it did in Veer's personal syllabus. Veer said that syllabus had produced excellent results elsewhere. Rudra asked for the marksheet. Aadhya pointed out that nobody present was qualified to certify one.

They were not recreating the game. No squares had been drawn on a bed sheet, no extra work given to a worker's hands. The child had won in her own court and kept her own afternoon. Here, four friends could quarrel about the loss without taking anything more from her.

Rudra looked at Veer while Ira was speaking.

The shoulder was still protected. The knee had come in a chair. None of that had been edited out of the returned story. He had imagined enjoyment arriving like a prize given only to the person who could go all the way; Veer had enjoyed eight minutes and left before a ninth became something he had not agreed to spend.

He asked whether the girl had seemed disappointed when the game ended.

"Doosra poochha tha," Veer said. "Maine kaha, aaj ek hi. Phir woh log apna khelne lage."

There was relief in how little explanation the answer needed. Nobody beyond the passage was waiting for them to become well enough to finish being guests.

Ira left the audio call first, as she had chosen.

"Mujhe college waala chapter mat suna dena," she said. "Woh kal bhi boring rahega."

Rudra said he would preserve it for her. She ended the connection before he could ask which part was boring, and the worker at her end put the phone away. There was no unfinished duty hidden in the goodbye.

Aadhya noticed the college page on Rudra's stand.

"Correction ho gaya?"

"Ek word ka. Baaki duniya filhaal waise hi galat hai."

She asked him which word. He told her, then complained that the corrected version looked less impressive than the mistake. Veer said that was usually the difficulty with accurate notes. For a few moments they talked about the page rather than the country he had not visited yesterday.

There were still things they could do badly together without anybody dying.

Rudra had not realised how much he missed that category of time.

---

The nurse reminded them that there were three minutes left.

Veer did not start another account. He asked Rudra whether he wanted anything moved before they went, then remembered his own shoulder and corrected himself.

"Matlab inse keh dein," he added, indicating the worker. "Main khud furniture service nahi khol raha."

"Pehle game consultancy band kar," Aadhya said.

Rudra wanted the page left where it was. The water needed to stay on the other side, where the nurse had already arranged it for help. There was nothing heroic or lonely about saying that no useful rearrangement was required.

He asked whether he could tell his mother the ordinary part of yesterday's visit later: the cloth being mended, the queue, the game, Veer's actual attempt to win. It was their morning to describe, not simply his because they had described it to him.

Aadhya agreed to those parts of her account. Veer agreed too, with the stipulation that his tactical error should not acquire a second error in the retelling.

"Ma ko rules nahi sikhaaoonga," Rudra said. "Bas haar bataaoonga."

"Selective reporting."

"Permission waapas le raha hai?"

Veer considered it with exaggerated seriousness, then shook his head.

"Nahi. Bata de."

They had not offered the hearing papers, another person's private care or Ira's time for somebody else's curiosity. Rudra would tell his own first-visit impressions for himself; the small pieces his friends had just allowed him were enough to add to them.

He wanted his mother to hear about a morning which had not existed only because he needed answers.

---

The worker called the end while Aadhya was still smiling.

She stood after checking that the route around the chair was clear. Veer's comfortable position was checked again before the attendant turned him towards the doorway. Rudra did not try to sit farther forward to watch them go.

"Baad mein," Veer said.

It was a goodbye without an appointment attached to it.

The attendant took the chair back along the same corridor. Aadhya walked with the ward worker at the agreed pace; a door was managed for her rather than becoming a demonstration for the fingers. Veer gave the knee's present feeling during the short pause they had allowed for. Nothing required a detour or an extra circuit because the first ten minutes had been pleasant.

At their own places, the receiving workers heard the short report and asked for their direct answers. Aadhya's hand had not developed new sensation. Veer's knee still needed the rest which had made the chair sensible in the first place. Accepted care continued for both. The attendant returned the chair to its ward place, handed over the bag and finished the movement inside the agreed interval.

The people who had helped went to their next duties. Yesterday's full fees remained settled; today's brief ward assistance had not reopened that booking or cancelled the ordinary care account.

---

Rudra's next position change came without visitors.

He answered about the back before asking for the college page to be lowered. The nurse protected the elbow and wrist through the movement, then checked the supports at the ankle and knee. A few successful minutes of company had not made those tasks optional.

When she had finished, he thanked her and asked to rest without the phone for a while.

The room gradually lost the voices he had been holding in it. At the edge of sleep, he could still hear Veer trying to make an excuse sound like technical analysis. It pleased him more than another promise of strength would have done.

Elsewhere, Ira slept without making an entry about the visit. Aadhya let the hand rest. Veer asked for the little adjustment his knee needed and accepted the check which came before it.

None of them had brought home a way to make tomorrow painless.

For today they had brought themselves back, and the people helping them had been allowed to finish the work they had actually agreed to do.

By afternoon, the road had begun improving the journey.

At a tea counter away from the wards, a customer mentioned the hired vehicle he had seen going out the previous morning. Somebody else had noticed it on the return. Between the two observations, an ordinary vehicle had acquired the reputation of having gone somewhere worth discussing.

"Mannat rahi hogi," a man said.

"Tum saath gaye the?" the tea seller asked.

"Nahi. Bas keh raha hoon."

That qualification survived for perhaps one more cup.

A customer leaving with a small parcel told the next person that a family had made a special trip into the hills and returned before evening. Asked which family, he could not say. Asked where they had gone, he named no place; he had no place to name. The story moved on with a prayer in it because the teller understood a prayer better than an unexplained drive.

There was nothing in the telling about another realm, a window, a bucket or a child's game. Nobody at the counter possessed a hearing paper or knew where the passengers were receiving care. The visible road had given the district two ends of a journey. The middle was being furnished from habit.

The tea seller washed the used glasses.

His correction would travel less far than the invented pilgrimage. It still existed, spoken to the people who had actually been there to hear him.

---

Rudra did not hear that version of the day.

He woke to a worker asking whether he was ready for the next part of care. The answer took precedence over the thought he had carried out of sleep. After the check and the ordinary help with the position, he had lunch, rested again and asked whether a short family call might fit later.

The people at the other end needed to choose their time too.

A request went to Varanasi. Rukmini answered after her care that she would like to speak that evening if he was still comfortable then. Ram was resting; she did not wake him so a call to their son could count as a complete family occasion. Both teams accepted a short interval, with the phone supported and the right to stop if either person needed to.

The acceptance was for conversation, not a summons to travel.

Rudra let the wait remain a wait. He did not ask a mentor to bring out the hearing sheets or turn the college page over to make room for another list. The separate account for the old damaged ambulance was still not settled; that practical fact had not been carried away by yesterday's driver's paid departure. It could remain a real unfinished matter without becoming the subject of every free hour.

What he wanted to tell his mother was smaller and, for once, mostly happy.

He tried to remember the game well enough to say what had actually amused Aadhya. Then he remembered that he had not watched it, and stopped composing the scene as if he had.

"Veer says" would be an adequate beginning.

---

The evening call connected after the next checks and a proper rest.

Rukmini was in her own supported position. Dadi helped set the phone where it could be heard, confirmed that the water and call bell were within the arrangement Rukmini needed, then gave them privacy. Care remained available at both ends. Rudra had the stand angled so he did not need to lift the weak hand or turn against the back support.

"Aaj dost aaye the," he said after their practical questions. "Das minute. Yahin."

His mother asked whether it had tired him.

"Ab rest kar liya. Tab bhi check hua tha. Achha laga."

He told her that the travellers had returned the previous afternoon and were in their own care. The day's hired work had actually finished, not merely been postponed until the next visit. She did not receive their private clinical details in order to be reassured about him.

Then he said, "Veer game haar ke aaya hai."

"Achha. Isliye bulaaya tha?"

"Nahi. Yeh toh family ko jaan-na chahiye."

She laughed carefully. The small sound loosened something in the room without requiring either body to follow it into a bigger laugh.

He gave her the account his friends had allowed: a child making room, pebbles on cloth, Veer trying honestly and losing to a row he had watched arrive. The cloth workers had kept working. A man in fine clothes had waited for a turn which had not moved nearer because his wrap was expensive.

"Aadhya keh rahi thi," he added before describing the repaired awning.

His mother listened. She did not turn an ordinary game into a test of Rudra's destiny, or say the children must have recognised something in his friend. When he stopped to answer the nurse's brief question, she waited through it.

"Wahan bhi log kaam ke beech khel lete hain," she said when he was comfortable again.

"Haan. Humein laga tha bas humein hi serious rehna padta hai."

He was making fun of himself. She heard it and let him.

"Tumhe apne waale din kya achha laga tha?" she asked.

Rudra considered giving her the view.

He could describe it. The pale paving, the water coming into light, the flowers which had looked almost insulting after the chair was told to move. Those were his own recollections. He did not have to borrow Aadhya's eyes for them.

"Saaya mila tha," he said at last. "Pehle waale ne hata diya tha. Doosri jagah ek aurat ne apna samaan hata ke baithne diya. Phir unhe kaam ke liye jagah chahiye thi toh hum uth gaye."

"Thakaan mein baithne ki jagah bahut achhi lagti hai."

"Haan. Bas utna bhi."

He had once thought the sentence would sound small beside the word Swarg. Tonight it sounded like an accurate answer to his mother's question.

He told her about the water too, within the part he had seen: the kick and the weight left waiting while other people argued. He did not assign ranks from clothes or turn the well-dressed man's objection into a certificate for everyone who lived there. His mother's silence did not ask him to make the story tidier.

"Achha bhi laga tha," he said, almost defensively.

"Woh tumne bataaya."

"Galat cheez bhi wahin hui."

"Woh bhi suna."

He felt the defence loosen. She was not making him prosecute the whole place before he could admit he had liked sitting beneath its cloth.

For the first time, the account of that morning could end without a verdict large enough to cover the sky.

---

The nurse at his end said there were two minutes left of the eight both sides had agreed.

Rudra answered her check before turning back to the call. The back had not changed. The phone could remain where it was; he did not need to reach after the voice.

"Aapne khaaya?" he asked his mother.

She had. She asked what he had left on his plate. He objected to the assumption, then admitted one thing, and she said she was not going to send instructions about vegetables through two care teams.

"Aap bhej sakti ho," he said. "Woh log meri side lenge."

"Tumhe lagta hai."

He could hear her smile.

Their ordinary conversation had a shape he knew well: meal, sleep, the shirt which was easier to change with help, the reminder that an easier hour did not cancel the next care. He had often mistaken that shape for the whole of her. Motherhood had made her familiar before he had thought to ask whether familiarity was the same as knowledge.

She knew the child who had come in dusty from the courtyard. She had already answered for what she had seen and failed to stop there; this pleasant call had not taken that answer back.

He knew which spoon she disliked and how she looked at him when he said he had eaten outside.

There was so much before those things which he had never asked her to describe.

---

His father's earlier name had once seemed to put Ram at an impossible distance.

The distance had become less simple after the telling: a baby sent away, people who had raised him, a man who had fought before his son was born and still brought fear into a home which needed more than his victories. Learning that history had not made the courtyard harmless. It had made the father more than the courtyard.

Rudra had let his mother remain almost entirely inside the house.

Part of that had been how she chose to speak. Part had been the habit of a child for whom a mother's past was merely a longer version of waiting for him to arrive.

He looked towards the cupboard, then away. There was no question to be routed through a window. No form to open, no right paper he could insist must already contain her.

The man with the bucket had a life which was not his to summon. His mother had one which he could ask to hear. Those were not the same relationship, and he did not need to make them the same to notice how often he had filled a silence with the role someone performed for him.

He wanted to ask without making the asking sound like an accusation.

Or a demand to be reassured that nothing important existed before their family.

---

"Ma," he said.

"Haan."

He could have asked what she thought of the hearing, whether she was frightened for him, when she would be well enough to come. There were real questions there. None was the one he had just discovered he wanted.

Her water was near. His body was supported. There was a little time left, and the people who would help them end the call had not been asked to disappear.

He thought of how readily he could picture the kitchen in Varanasi and how little he knew of where she had been a child. He did not picture a hidden room, a secret sign or a face waiting to recognise his. He had no new evidence about her in the folded cloth or in his own blood.

He had a mother who had listened to him describe a place.

Before the rest of the interval could become another practical question about tomorrow, he asked the ordinary thing he had somehow never made ordinary enough to say.

"Aap bachpan mein kahan rehti thi?"
