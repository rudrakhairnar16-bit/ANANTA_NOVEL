# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-FOUR — THE PERSON BESIDE HER

---

> *"A name gave the anger an address in the past.*
> *It did not give us a map of the present."*
> *— Ira's later register, beside an unfilled column*

---

"Daksh," Rukmini said. "Meri behen ka saathi tha. Jis waqt ki baat kar rahi hoon, usmein woh uske saath tha."

Rudra repeated the name once.

The room did not change around it. The support beneath his wrist remained necessary; the small sound beyond the curtain continued to be a worker moving a tray. There was no recognition in his body which could tell him more about the man than his mother had said.

"Sirf naam nahi bataa rahi," she added. "Uska saath tha. Par usse meri behen apne kiye se alag nahi ho jaati."

He understood. Naming an ally did not remove the sister from her own betrayal.

Nor did it tell him exactly what either person had said in a room he had never seen. His mind wanted the room. A face, a gesture, a table with the false accusation laid upon it. He could feel how easily an invented picture would become something he thought he remembered being told.

He let the picture go before it acquired details.

---

"Aap mujhe us waqt ki baat bataa rahi ho," he said.

"Haan."

"Aaj ka haal isse apne aap nahi pata chalega."

"Nahi. Uske liye aaj ki alag baat chahiye."

It was not a correction of her testimony. The past alliance was real in the account she was giving him. He did not have to turn it into a report of the present in order for it to matter.

He had arrived at Swarg with a question about suffering and the answer his father had been given. He had come home with two limited hearing sheets and the memory of people doing their ordinary jobs. Now a private family history was beginning to inhabit the same place.

The three things could be kept together without being made identical.

The man at the window had not named this ally. The boy with the ball had not spoken for a court. His mother was not a political bulletin merely because she had once been driven from there.

"Naam se main aage bhaag raha hoon," he admitted.

"Kahaan?"

"Jahaan mujhe abhi ka kuch pata nahi."

---

Rukmini let that answer stand.

She was not asking him to become less angry before she would believe he loved her. Rudra was grateful for that too. People often spoke about anger as though the only two choices were to obey it immediately or to discover that one had never properly felt it.

He could be angry while lying where the nurse had helped him settle. The feeling did not need a clenched hand to prove it existed.

"Mujhe bura lag raha hai," he said.

"Pata hai."

"Aapke liye. Aur yeh sunke ki main itne din—"

He stopped.

The next sentence would have made his not knowing the centre of what she had survived. There would be time to ask about being kept outside the truth. He did not need to steal this minute for that different hurt.

"Abhi aapki baat sun raha hoon," he said instead.

"Tumhara sawaal bhi rahega," she answered.

He looked at the phone.

It was not absolution. She was admitting that his question did not disappear when hers entered the room.

---

The eight-minute interval reached its chosen end.

This time the name did not become an excuse to negotiate another half hour. Mother and son said goodbye while the thread was still live between them. The call actually closed. The helper put away the phone after the finite setup work, and Rudra attended to the care which had been waiting for its proper turn.

He answered about the wrist, the back and the unsupported effort he had not made. His ankle and knee were settled with the help he still needed. The name had not cured the weak grip or supplied a reason to test it.

In Varanasi, Rukmini took her own next care and rest.

In Mirzapur, Rudra listened to the small room until the urge to contact somebody had become an urge he could examine instead of obeying.

He did not call a mentor to ask how quickly the natural passage could be reached.

He had been taught that an enemy's name was useful information. It was. But usefulness was not the same as an instruction, and the present clinical review had not become a military council because his mother had spoken.

---

Later, after supper, he asked for the light to be reduced.

There was nothing he wanted to read tonight. No list he needed someone else to write before sleep. He could remember the name without making a record of a conversation his mother had kept between them.

The name was Daksh.

It did not make every other unanswered thing simpler.

For the first time since hearing it, Rudra allowed the questions to remain several questions. He did not have to put a single man's face over all of them to keep them from being lost.

The next morning's page had an easy sentence and one which Rudra read three times without keeping.

He asked for the stand to stay where it had been placed after the check. The difficulty was not in reaching the words. He knew where his thoughts were going whenever he left them unattended.

At the end of the short interval, he said he had not taken in much.

The nurse did not tell him that meant he should try twice as hard after lunch. The page was put aside. His present symptoms were checked, the supports settled, and the ordinary next part of the day began.

He disliked how relieved he was to be permitted an unproductive morning.

Then he remembered that he had been the one to say he needed to stop. Nobody had supplied the sentence for him.

---

Rukmini chose a ten-minute afternoon conversation after her own care and rest.

Rudra accepted after his rest and the current review. The brief phone help was agreed, both were comfortable in the supported positions, and the connection began without one end waiting on an injured person to hold the device for the other.

"Kal raat kisi ko phone karne ka mann tha," he said.

"Kiya?"

"Nahi. Aapki baat nahi bataayi."

"Aur apni?"

He had not expected the question.

"Kisko kya bolta, yeh samajh nahi aaya."

---

His mother did not offer him another person's availability.

She said he could want company without giving her account away. If he wanted someone to know a particular part of it, they would need to decide that particular part together; today's conversation was not permission to circulate the name from yesterday's call.

"Bas gussa aa raha hai, bol sakta hoon," he said.

"Haan. Kisiko sunna ho toh."

He almost smiled. Even inside this difficult subject she had left another person the right not to be ready that afternoon.

"Mujhe aapke liye kuch karna hai," he said.

"Abhi meri baat sun rahe ho."

"Woh kaafi kam lagta hai."

"Tumhe kam lagne se main tumhe ladaai pe nahi bhej doongi."

He looked down at the hand resting where it had been settled. It was not shaped into a fist. There had been a time when he would have been ashamed to let her see it like that while speaking about someone who had hurt her.

Now he was beginning to be ashamed of a different thing: how readily he could make an injured woman responsible for finding him a heroic use.

He did not need to punish himself for noticing. He needed to stop handing her that work.

---

"Meri baat sun rahe ho," she said. "Meri jagah faisla nahi le rahe."

He heard the difference.

His mother's desire to see home again remained hers. The lie used against her remained a lie. Daksh's past alliance remained part of the account. None of those truths needed a promise that he would settle everything before he could rest tonight.

But rest was not a way to make them disappear either.

"Main bhoolna nahi chahta," he said.

"Yaad rakhne ke liye har waqt isi mein rehna zaroori nahi hai."

She was not asking him to forget what he was learning about her. She was giving him room not to spend every quiet hour proving that the knowledge hurt enough.

He had done that with his father's story too: tried to make the right intensity of feeling into a debt which could finally be paid. It had not brought back a dead person or repaired the courtyard mornings.

He told her, a little defensively, that he still had questions about being kept outside so much of their lives.

"Haan," she said. "Woh tumhaare hain. Mere saath galat hua tha, isliye tum unhe chhod do, yeh nahi keh rahi."

---

The ten minutes ended with both questions still alive.

They said goodbye. The connection closed before the next care; phones were put away, the finite setup help finished. Rukmini rested after her own check. Rudra gave his current answers, accepted the protected movement and did not reach towards the stand as it was moved aside.

There was a pull when the position changed. He reported it immediately, let the nurse attend to it and waited until the support was comfortable again. It did not become a new secret to preserve the good ending of a difficult call.

Afterwards he lay still and watched a patch of afternoon travel along the curtain.

He could ask somebody to sit with his anger. He did not have to make that person carry the story which had caused it.

The thought was modest enough that he nearly dismissed it. Then he considered how much damage he could do by choosing a larger one merely because it felt more equal to the size of the hurt.

He let the modest thought stay.

When the worker came to help with the water, he asked for that help and no other errand. There was no need to find a messenger for the private name. For now, it belonged where his mother had chosen to tell it.

After rest and a fresh comfort check, Rudra asked whether Aadhya wanted a short call that evening.

He gave the request its proper size. He was angry about things he was still understanding and did not want to explain the details. He wanted a little company, not someone to tell him what to do. If she did not have room for that, he could wait.

She chose four minutes after her own care and rest.

He accepted. The supported setup was agreed with the ward help, and when the call began both had answered the present questions their own people needed to ask. Aadhya's right thumb and index finger were not made reliable by her willingness to listen.

"Chaar minute," she said.

"Haan."

"Advice mere paas waise bhi kam hai aaj."

He smiled before he answered. "Inventory dekh li?"

"Khali dabba hai. Company hai."

---

For a few moments, he had nothing to say.

He was used to arriving at Aadhya with some part of himself arranged for her: a joke sharpened enough to hide the tremor in it, a practical question which would make the contact look necessary, a confession already edited into the version least likely to make her worry.

Tonight she had agreed to hear the unproductive beginning too.

"Gussa aa raha hai," he said at last. "Aur uske baad sochta hoon ki isse kuch hona chahiye."

"Abhi?"

"Bas hona chahiye. Warna jaise main—"

"Feel karne ka proof de rahe ho?"

He let the words settle.

"Shayad."

She did not identify the object of the anger or ask him to confirm a guess about his mother's private life. Knowing a birthplace and a name had not made her the recipient of every sentence which followed them.

"Mujhe dekhna tha scarf ka edge theek hua ya nahi," she said. "Main khud karne lag jaati toh bhi haath ko meri baat se farq nahi padta."

He looked towards his own supported wrist.

"Hua theek?"

"Haan. Karwaaya. Thoda alag blue hai."

"Pasand hai?"

"Haan. Yeh mat bolna ki tumko dekhna padega tab opinion doge."

He laughed softly enough to keep his back from objecting, then answered the nurse's brief check before he replied to Aadhya.

"Tera opinion kaafi hai."

---

She told him only what she wanted to tell: one edge repaired, the work accepted and paid for, the scarf back in her bag. The woman who had done it had left. There was no favour he needed to return on her behalf, no thing for him to inspect in order for the choice to become complete.

He liked hearing about a problem whose size had not changed while it was being solved.

Aadhya said she had nearly asked for the new thread to match perfectly, then decided she did not want to wait on perfection for that little thing. It had been her choice about an ordinary scarf, not a conclusion she was offering him about his family.

He was grateful she did not make the comparison for him.

"Abhi bhi gussa hai," he admitted.

"Hona band karwaane ke liye phone nahi kiya tha."

"Nahi."

"Toh theek."

There was no challenge in it. She had heard the feeling and the request he had actually made. She did not need him to arrive at gratitude, a plan or a better mood by the end of four minutes so she could believe she had been good company.

He wished he knew how to give that to himself as easily.

---

At the limit, Aadhya said she needed to stop.

Rudra thanked her for the time, not for solving anything. She said goodbye. The connection ended; both phones were put away and both took the next care and rest without a private second conversation being smuggled into a farewell message.

He had not spoken his mother's wish, the false framing, the sister or Daksh's name.

He had spoken his own anger. Somebody he loved had heard it and remained a person with a scarf, a hand which needed care and a four-minute limit. She had not become the container for everything he could not yet hold.

For a while he lay listening to the ordinary room.

His mother did not know every detail of the call and did not need to. She had allowed him to speak about his own feelings; he had chosen someone willing to listen. He had not passed one woman's story to another in order to make the two kinds of love easier for him to manage.

It was possible to leave people separate and still feel less alone.

---

After supper, he slept sooner than he expected.

The anger had not been discharged into a promise. The history had not become harmless. But there had been an interval in which it did not have to occupy every part of him, and nobody had accused him of forgetting.

He woke briefly when care was due, answered honestly and let himself settle again.

The next morning would have its own questions. It did not need to be borrowed tonight merely because he could imagine it.

Day two hundred and seven began without the name being spoken.

It had not gone away. Rudra knew that before the review began. He gave the nurse the actual night's account, including the brief waking for care, and the present state of the back and hand. The unanswered faint remained on its own uncertain side of the history; a family name could not be written into its cause.

Breakfast was followed by rest, and then he chose the page again.

He did not start at the beginning to make yesterday's failed effort look more diligent. He asked for the stand at the checked angle and found the sentence where his attention had left him.

The first line stayed. So did the second.

The third was difficult for a reason belonging to the third line. There was an argument in it which he did not quite agree with, and he wanted to know whether the next paragraph would defend it.

For a few minutes, he was a student irritated by a paragraph.

It was an unexpectedly complete occupation.

---

He stopped at the chosen limit instead of using interest as proof he could double it.

The worker who came to put the stand aside asked whether the page should be marked where he had reached. He said yes. A plain slip went between the sheets, and the reading was put away without needing his wrist to pinch the paper.

He could return to a thought without holding it in his hand all morning.

The realisation was so ordinary that he almost laughed. It was also the kind of help he had spent years making difficult to accept, as if another person touching the edge of a task meant it no longer belonged to him.

This task still did.

He had not inherited a different syllabus because his mother had been born elsewhere. He did not have to decide that the work was trivial in order to make her history important. There might be days when he could not read at all. This morning there had been a little of both lives in him, and neither had destroyed the other.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini asked Dadi to help sort the clean clothes already in the bag.

The request was small and specific. She wanted the softer cloth nearer the top for the next change, not a new bundle brought from home or an outing arranged to find one. Her shoulder stayed supported while Dadi took out the things which needed moving.

One piece had been folded so that its edge disappeared beneath another. Rukmini tried to explain which she meant by describing the colour, then realised there were two of the same colour in the open bag.

They looked at each other.

"Upar waala," Rukmini said.

"Abhi dono upar hain."

She laughed softly and pointed with the hand she could use without disturbing the support. Dadi lifted the right one. The brief adjustment was done, the rest of the bag closed, and the chair moved back out of the way before the next care.

Nothing had been discovered inside the clothes. No forgotten object arrived to prove the life she was telling her son about. She had simply wanted the next change to be less awkward.

It was good to want that too.

---

After Dadi went to her own rest, Rukmini lay looking at the familiar room.

She had given her son a name from a painful part of her life. He had not become able to mend that life by hearing it, and she had not become only the woman to whom the harm had been done.

He could still ask her how the night had been. She could still be annoyed by a fold of cloth or want the water before another question. There would be no dignity in making herself endlessly available to the telling merely because it mattered.

When the next check came, she said what hurt now and what support was comfortable. The account she was giving Rudra was not a reason to shorten the rest which followed.

She slept.

---

Rudra's request for a later call reached her after his own lunch and rest, not during hers.

She chose eight minutes that evening after the current care, if both reviews and comfort checks allowed. He accepted the limit. The ordinary help needed to set up the phones was agreed at each end; nobody was sent to fetch a family witness or stand by for an explanation of the past.

He spent the waiting time with the next small parts of his actual day.

A supported change of position. Water with help. The pause before the back was comfortable enough for him to stop thinking about it. He did not make a drill out of the pause or set himself a count to conquer it.

The new knowledge had reached a life which was already happening.

He thought of the plain slip in the page. The reading would still be where he had left it. His mother would remain his mother when the accepted conversation ended. A question did not vanish because he let it wait until the person he wanted to ask was ready to answer.

He could carry it into the evening without rehearsing a verdict.

He wanted to understand what had been hidden from him, and why the hiding had felt so complete.

The evening call began after both current checks.

Rukmini had rested and taken the care due before it. Rudra's back was comfortable in the supported position; the hand did not need to hold the phone. Neither had new symptoms to hide in order to earn the eight minutes they had chosen.

For the first part of the conversation, they spoke about the page.

He had read a little and disagreed with a sentence. His mother asked what it said. He tried to explain without reciting the whole thing, became more irritated with his own explanation than with the original argument, and heard her trying not to laugh.

"Ab aap bolo mat ki writer ko bhi poochh lo," he said.

"Nahi bol rahi. Pehle samajh toh aane do mujhe ki likha kya hai."

He began again.

It was good to be told he was being unclear about something which had no connection to a hidden birthright. He could fail to explain a paragraph in the ordinary way and try again without either of them fearing they had broken the new truth between them.

She understood the second version.

She still thought he might need to read the next part before being quite so certain the writer was wrong.

---

"Theek hai," he conceded. "Aage padhunga. Abhi nahi."

"Abhi main hoon."

There was no jealousy in it. Only a mother claiming the few minutes her son had asked to spend with her, while the page could wait where he had left it.

He looked towards the cupboard. The two hearing sheets were still where they belonged, untouched by this different conversation. He had not needed their authority to let her correct his explanation of a paragraph, or to let her account of exile remain hers.

"Aapko meri taraf se koi promise chahiye nahi?" he asked.

"Kis cheez ka?"

"Ki yeh sab sunke main—"

"Jo nahi samjhe, uska vaada mat karo."

The interruption was gentle enough to make the instruction harder to evade.

He had wanted to offer her a good ending before he knew the middle. His mother was not asking him to pretend he knew a road merely to make her feel less alone on it.

That did not mean every road she had chosen for him had been right.

---

He said that last part with more difficulty than he expected.

Not the metaphor, only the thing beneath it: he could hear what had been done to her and still want answers about what had been decided for him.

"Mujhe pata hai chhota tha," he said. "Bahut kuch aapko hi karna hota. Par phir main bada bhi hua."

"Haan," she answered.

"Bas itna bolne se bura lag raha hai. Jaise abhi aapki baat se apni taraf mod raha hoon."

"Ek baat poori zindagi ko chup nahi kar deti."

He let the reply come to rest.

Her pain did not demand that he forget the years in which he had asked badly, guessed wildly or stopped asking because the answer always seemed to be somewhere beyond the age he had reached. His questions did not require him to turn her exile into a story invented for sympathy.

Both could remain true in the same call.

---

He thought of Ram's voice on the earlier call from Varanasi, back when Rudra had still been in Prayagraj.

The father he had always known had named Pataal, named himself Daavansh, named the Asuran inheritance concealed in his son. The kavach had hidden that part too. Rudra had heard him say it; he did not need the newly learned name to make the old answer real.

At the time, the paternal origin had seemed large enough to account for every hidden thing.

It no longer occupied the whole space.

His mother had come from somewhere too. The seal was her act. He knew those things now without needing to imagine a birth he could not remember, or search his body for an image of the person she had been then.

He had a memory of being told about the lock, years of living with it and the actual loss of it. Those were not the same as remembering its making.

---

The nurse asked about the present position. He answered before continuing. Rukmini used the pause for her own comfort check, and both remained willing to speak within the time left.

The question taking shape in him was not how to restore the kavach. It was broken; nothing in this conversation had put it back. He did not reach beneath a dressing or ask his mother to try anything through the phone.

He wanted the purpose in the words of the person who had placed it.

Knowing the reason would not make the infant he had been into somebody who had agreed to it. It would not settle every later choice. But he had been listening to the story as if only one parent could have brought a hidden identity into his life.

Now he knew his mother's beginning was not contained by the kitchen in which she had finally spoken about the seal.

He could ask without supplying an answer for her. He could keep the question separate from a technique, a royal demand or a promise to go anywhere.

There was still time in the accepted call.

"Ma, jo kavach aapne mere janm par lagaaya tha—woh sirf Papa ki pehchaan chhupa raha tha, ya aapki taraf se mujh mein jo hai use bhi?"
