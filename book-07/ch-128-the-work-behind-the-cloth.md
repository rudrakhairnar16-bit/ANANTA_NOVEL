# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-EIGHT — THE WORK BEHIND THE CLOTH

---

> *"The account of the visit first listed everything they had not obtained.*
> *The laughter had to be added afterwards."*
> *— Ira's later register, a correction to the first-court pages*

---

"Haan," Aadhya said. "Ghoomne. Jagah mile toh baithne. Kisi ke bulaaye bina bhi jaane ka mann ho sakta hai. Bas unhe hamaare liye apna din chhodna nahi padega."

Veer considered that on his side of the call.

"Khaali haath waapas aaye toh?"

"Bag toh attendant laayega."

He laughed.

The answer did not require another assignment to improve it. They said goodnight while the offered interval was still an interval, and put their phones aside.

---

In the morning, the knee had settled to the familiar state after the rest. Veer gave the full account, including the pull from the previous evening, at his review. The worker examined what needed examining before confirming the limited travel plan.

He was not asked whether he wanted the day enough to bear a different finding.

Aadhya's own check came separately. The right thumb and index finger still needed watching at ordinary tasks. No new symptom from the earlier trial; no new permission to lift. She chose the outing as it had been described, with its pauses and help, not a longer one she hoped to negotiate after reaching the court.

Vedant and Sitara answered for themselves after their own morning reviews. Their protected arms had not changed status during the night. They could make the inspected walking work with the agreed rest between efforts; neither would carry the gear.

Breakfast and ordinary prescribed care came before departure.

Aadhya watched the blue edge of her scarf being settled clear of the fastening. The person helping her asked where it usually caught. She showed the place and let it be moved. No entrance into another realm was going to improve an awkward fold against her neck.

At quarter to eight, the new vehicle arrived.

---

The driver checked the receiving contact and road location with Sitara before anyone got in.

The two clinical workers gave their names and roles to the people whose care they were taking over. One would remain Earth-side, one accompany the short line. They received the current reports, asked Veer and Aadhya their own questions and accepted this morning's travelling care.

The attendant checked the two folding stools, water and ordinary bags. A fastening on one stool needed settling before it went into the secured space. He dealt with it there rather than promising to make it hold at the far end.

Eight separate belt places were occupied by eight people.

Veer could see his own bag from the seat. It did not require him to hold it in his lap or put a shoulder between it and a moving turn. Aadhya's bottle was placed so that the accompanying worker could help when needed. The driver waited through the position and restraint checks.

At eight, they left Mirzapur.

The ordinary departure report reached the receiving contact; Dhruv's acknowledgment came through after his own morning care. Rudra and Ira were not awakened to witness a car pulling away.

---

On the road, Aadhya began noticing cloth.

A towel over a shop shutter. A faded shirt drying from a nail. The bright synthetic cover of a goods vehicle, tied with a knot which looked more hopeful than effective. None resembled the broad hanging shade above pale paving, and all had the same ordinary burden of needing to stay where somebody put them.

Veer noticed a breakfast stall they could not use.

He mentioned it only once, which he considered restraint.

At the planned stop, the attendant checked the access before the workers helped with the ordinary break. Veer changed the knee's position, accepted the help with the awkward part of clothing and kept the shoulder clear. Aadhya had her bottle opened rather than improving a grip trial in the car park.

The driver took his own pause. The vehicle did not become a waiting room whose clock belonged only to the passengers.

After the current checks, the belts and positions were settled again. Aadhya closed her eyes for part of the remaining drive. She could hear Veer trying to describe the difference between wanting another breakfast and needing one, and the clinical worker refusing to settle a question outside her remit.

He eventually conceded the distinction and ate the portion already carried for him.

The road narrowed towards the familiar approach.

---

They reached the Earth waiting place at quarter to ten.

The driver turned where the outward way remained clear and stopped on the firm space they had agreed. The Earth worker tested the phone connection and sent the practical arrival report. Both receiving contact and Dhruv acknowledged it through the ordinary arrangement.

Outside, the ridge held its scrub and rough stone. It did not look grander because Aadhya knew what lay beyond it now.

The two young travellers stayed beside the vehicle with the Earth clinical worker while Vedant and Sitara prepared for the inspection. The driver remained there too. The attendant took the folded stools and water; the accompanying worker checked the time before joining the mentors.

Four would go first.

Aadhya sat where she could wait without making the return report hurry.

---

At ten minutes to ten, the four went in.

Vedant checked the shallow rise before the tighter bend. Sitara watched the ground from the opposite side where a foot would need to turn on the way home. The attendant carried the gear without making it obscure the footing. The clinical worker stayed close enough to hear their current answers, rather than following so far behind that care became an intention.

Beyond the bend, the phone did not connect.

They continued along the known line, checked the broad resting place and reached the first court. The awning was there; the window was open. A loose end of cloth lay near one edge of the paving, with a person beside it gathering it out of the walking space. The mentors waited until the way was clear, then checked the turning place they would actually use.

They did not inspect the rooms beyond the doorway or promise what might be happening up the terraces.

On the return, Vedant looked back along the approach before taking the narrower section. The angle was still usable from that direction. Sitara gave her own hand and walking-fatigue account at the broad pause; the worker allowed the brief rest before they continued.

The four emerged at twenty-two past ten.

---

Veer had spent part of the wait watching an ant negotiate a crack beside his shoe.

He knew that was not an inspection of the realm. He had no idea whether the ant had come from anywhere more interesting than the scrub. It was making sufficient trouble for itself carrying something nearly its size, and he found himself quietly annoyed when a gust took the thing away.

"Mat uthaana," Aadhya said.

He looked up.

"Ant ka samaan. Usko khud decide karne de."

The ant had already abandoned the first load and found another. Veer conceded that it seemed better at revising its plans than he was.

Then the four people came out of the opening, and he paid attention to the report he actually needed.

The Earth worker received the accompanying findings and asked the mentors directly. Same short line, checked inward and outward; clear first-court turn, ordinary walking effort, no new problem with the protected arms. Phone failure beyond the bend again, ordinary connection here. That was the finding, not an assurance about all of Swarg.

The connected practical inspection report went to the receiving contact and Dhruv. Both acknowledged it.

There was water, a place to sit and a real interval of rest before another choice was asked for. Veer drank with his bag still off his shoulder. Aadhya listened to the returning attendant describe the cloth being gathered clear, then stopped trying to picture the rest of the court from that one sentence.

They would either see it or they would not.

At twenty to eleven, current checks came before the question about crossing. The mentors gave their own answers. Veer described the knee after the road and waiting rest; Aadhya gave the hand's unchanged feeling. All six chose the same short visit under the agreed return limits.

At quarter to eleven, they set out.

---

The attendant did not put a stool into Veer's hand at the first awkward angle.

That made the walk less heroic than some part of Veer still expected. He had nothing to do with the bags. He could place his feet where he had been shown, keep the shoulder free and tell the clinical worker when the broad pause was welcome.

It was welcome.

They waited there, took the short rest and continued together. Aadhya kept her hands clear of the wall. The sound of water reached them around the stone before the light did.

This time she noticed the sound changing: enclosed and low, then sharper, with several little falls inside the one long noise. Perhaps she had heard those before. On the first visit, listening had been crowded by the effort of being somewhere impossible.

They came onto the pale paving at two minutes past eleven.

The court was busy without being full.

A length of rust-coloured cloth hung beside a white one; the patches on the underside showed where light found the thinner places. The shrub still grew from its narrow joint. Above the terraces a roof caught the sun so intensely that Aadhya looked away and found the smaller colours easier: green stem, dark binding cord, a brown hand flattening the corner of a page.

Someone laughed behind the open window.

The man who had received their question was holding a bundle under one arm while trying to leave his other hand free. He finally set the bundle down and used both hands. Whoever was laughing said something Aadhya could not hear, and he answered with an expression which needed no translation.

Then he saw the six visitors near the clear edge of the paving.

Sitara raised a hand without asking for the whole queue's attention. She did not beckon him away from the person at his ledge.

They waited until he had finished with that person.

This morning their own arrival was not the most urgent thing in the court.

---

"Aaj arzi?" the receiver asked when he could turn towards them.

"Nahi," Sitara said. "Aaj sirf thodi der dekhna hai. Pehle aangan tak. Kaam mein rukawat nahi karenge."

He looked at Veer and Aadhya, then at the carried stools.

"Raasta chhodkar baithna. Aur kisi ki cheez rakhni ho toh pehle poochh lena."

He was not giving them ownership of the shade. He was telling them how not to occupy the work in front of his window. Somebody behind him called about a bundle; he answered, excused himself and turned back to it.

There was no disappointment in not being admitted farther.

Aadhya looked towards the other hanging cloth and saw the woman who had moved a bundle for them on their first visit. Today the bundle was smaller. A length of white fabric lay across her lap, its edge weighted with an ordinary smooth stone.

Sitara greeted her. The woman looked up, recognised the little group imperfectly and asked where the chairs were.

"Do saathi aaj rest kar rahe hain," Veer said. "Hum dono aaye."

She nodded without asking him to make their absence interesting.

They asked whether there was room near the edge for two stools. She indicated a strip where they would leave her access to the folded pile.

"Thodi der. Phir yeh kholna hai."

That was enough.

---

The attendant set the stools where the clinical worker could check their footing and the two travellers' positions. Veer lowered himself at the permitted pace; Aadhya settled without putting force through the right hand. Both gave their current answers before the woman resumed the cloth.

Vedant and Sitara stood aside in the available shade, with room to move and no bag to make the protected arms useful. The woman pointed them towards a clear low ledge after asking the person beside it to shift a basket. They thanked both people and sat where the return line stayed open.

The first rest was quiet.

Under the cloth, the light carried the faint colour of its threads. Aadhya could see where a newer patch had been worked into the old edge, the stitch taking a slightly crooked path before becoming neat. She had once thought of mending as a thing which tried to disappear. Here the wrong shade of thread announced that somebody had chosen to keep using what had torn.

The woman pulled the loose edge straight.

"Woh is taraf nahi," she told a younger worker holding the other end. "Pehle seedha karo. Phir naapna."

He said it was straight.

She held up the edge without arguing. The middle sagged. He looked at it, changed his grip and laughed before she did.

Veer could understand that exchange with no knowledge of the cloth's history at all.

---

When the two workers had laid the length flat, Aadhya asked what it was being made into.

The woman tapped the hanging shade above them.

"Isi ka hissa. Udhar se phat gaya tha. Poora phenk dein?"

Aadhya shook her head.

It had not been a request for advice. The woman's smile made that clear without making the question foolish.

She showed the younger worker where the surviving edge could meet the replacement, then went back to her own task. The repair would have to fit something already stretched across this particular space. Nobody was making an emblem of the realm for the two visitors to admire.

Aadhya watched the hands without wanting to make her own prove something beside them.

"Aap help nahi karogi?" the younger worker asked, noticing the way she followed the stitch.

"Haath ko rest hai."

"Achha. Toh galti bata dena."

"Itna aata bhi nahi."

"Phir mat bataana," he said, and they all laughed.

The woman did not convert Aadhya's interest into a shift of work. She asked the younger worker to fetch the binding cord already lying on their pile. Veer stayed seated while he fetched it. No one looked wounded by the absence of Veer's assistance.

For a few minutes, that was the strangest and nicest thing in the court.

---

A child nearby was arranging six small pebbles on a cloth scrap.

Three pale, three dark. She had folded the scrap into a little board with straight divisions and was scolding a boy who kept making the folds spring up. He flattened one corner with a pebble which she immediately took back because it belonged in the game.

"Aur patthar le aao," she said.

The boy pointed at the six already there.

She explained the difference between a playing piece and a weight with increasing patience which was not very patient.

Veer found himself smiling before he noticed Aadhya watching him.

"Respectful interest?" she murmured.

"Public area mein hai."

The child heard enough to glance up. Veer smiled at her and left the smile there, without turning it into a request that she perform her game for the strangers.

She went back to straightening the cloth.

Above them, the older patch moved in the wind. Its stitches held. Under it, the boy returned with a stone which was much too large, and the argument began again.

---

A man in a finely worked wrap came to the window while Aadhya was resting.

She noticed the wrap before she noticed anything else. That embarrassed her. The first morning's bench had taught her to look for a demand even before the person wearing something beautiful opened his mouth.

He asked how long the next bundle would take.

The receiver pointed to the person still waiting with a parcel against his leg. The man in the fine cloth looked at that person, asked whether he had been there first, and took a place behind him when the answer came.

Nothing magnificent happened.

Aadhya was glad of the plainness of it. She had seen somebody use a stranger's body as though it could be moved for convenience. Now she saw somebody wait. Neither sight supplied the names of the powers above the terraces; both belonged to this particular morning.

Veer saw her watching the queue.

"Designation mil gaya?" he asked quietly.

"Nahi. Bas wait karte dekha."

"Useful information."

She smiled and looked back at the cloth.

---

The woman needed the strip beside the folded pile.

She told them before asking the younger worker to open the repaired length there. Sitara thanked her for the time already given and checked the clear few paces towards the low ledge. The clinical worker asked both young travellers about moving now. Their current findings stayed within the short plan.

The attendant moved the stools first, then stayed close while Veer and Aadhya stood at their permitted pace and crossed those few clear paces. The passage back to the opening remained in sight. No new room or flight of steps had been added to an outing by calling it only a little farther.

The woman spread the cloth into the space they had left.

It occupied much more paving than the bundle had suggested. Aadhya could see why a grateful guest remaining ten extra minutes would have been inconvenient. From the stool's earlier position she would have been sitting in the middle of someone else's work while congratulating herself on being unobtrusive.

The younger worker lifted the repaired edge. For a moment it seemed about to fold over itself. Veer's body began the old response before his judgment reached it.

He stopped at the beginning of rising.

The worker had caught the cloth with his own free hand and was perfectly capable of laying it down. The clinical worker looked at Veer; he gave the knee's unchanged feeling and settled back without pretending he had stood for another reason.

"Bach gayi," Aadhya murmured.

"Meri reputation?"

"Kapde ki. Tujhse."

He laughed, softly enough not to interrupt the measuring.

---

The receiver took his own short break.

He finished the person at the ledge, told the next one he would return after eating and moved the unfinished stack clear of the working edge. Nobody mistook the little pause for an explanation of how balance was maintained among worlds. A man needed his meal; the people waiting had their own feelings about waiting.

One person complained to another. Another asked where he could put the parcel down. The younger cloth worker pointed out a dry patch which would not interfere with the measuring, and the parcel owner moved it there himself.

The court rearranged itself around an ordinary need.

Aadhya heard the two children negotiating something more serious to them than the queue. The boy wanted his turn back because the oversized stone had distracted him. The girl said choosing that stone had also been his turn, which he considered a ridiculous addition to the rules.

Veer was listening too.

"Kaun sahi?" Aadhya asked.

"Main abhi jurisdiction establish nahi kar paaya."

She put a careful hand over the laugh before remembering that she could simply laugh.

The boy abandoned the appeal and started again. He was first this time. That seemed to repair the world's injustice sufficiently for the next few moves.

---

At quarter past eleven, the accompanying worker asked for their current answers.

Veer's knee was comfortable in the settled position; shoulder unchanged. Aadhya's hand had been out of the work and needed no experiment to prove the value of the restraint. They drank their own water, the attendant doing the fastenings which required it. There was still time inside the visit they had accepted, and time to leave before the margin grew thin.

Sitara rested on the ledge while Vedant watched the clear return line. When she was ready, he took his own seated pause. Neither had to pretend their walking bodies were inexhaustible because there were fewer questions today.

Near the children, a third pebble clicked into line.

The boy looked down, deeply offended by the arrangement he had helped produce. The girl pointed at the middle square. He insisted that he had meant to move a different piece; she told him that intention was not currently on the cloth.

Veer made the mistake of laughing while still looking interested.

She looked up at once.

"Aapko aata hai?"

"Thoda bahut. Shayad."

The child studied him with the seriousness of someone who had heard an unreliable qualification.

She began gathering the six pebbles into their two little groups.

---

The girl lifted the little board and asked the cloth worker if she could use the dry end of the ledge for a few minutes.

The woman checked what was lying there before agreeing. The younger worker moved a loose length of cord clear. The boy took the enormous stone away with an expression suggesting that nobody here appreciated sound engineering.

The child put the cloth where Veer could see it without bending forward.

"Teen apne seedhe mein," she said, arranging the pieces beside it. "Khadi line ya aadi. Pehle ek-ek rakhna. Phir baari mein ek sarkaana. Bas bagal ke khaali khaane mein. Aise tirchha nahi."

She demonstrated a move, undid it, then looked at him.

He understood the little game they were playing. Three pieces each; after placement, one move into an empty neighbouring square, no diagonal move or winning line. It was close enough to something he knew to make him confident, and different enough that confidence might be expensive.

Only in the smallest possible way.

No shoulder had to take a blow for being wrong about a pebble.

---

The accompanying worker checked whether he wanted to move or remain where he was. Veer said the position was still comfortable. The board was brought within the angle he could watch without holding a strained pose; there would be no reaching across it with the injured shoulder.

"Aap bata dena. Main rakh doongi," the girl said.

"Meri goti bhi?"

"Aap bolna sahi. Main galat nahi rakhoongi."

The boy made a small noise of disagreement. She turned on him at once. He said he had not said anything, which was technically true and did not help.

Aadhya was laughing before the matter could become an argument.

The worker allowed ten minutes within the visit's existing time, less if Veer needed to stop. Aadhya had her own stool and position; she could watch without making either hand responsible for a board or a stranger's expectations. Neither friend had acquired a right to finish a match after their bodies wanted the easier return.

The children accepted this as readily as a limit about somebody needing the ledge back. They would have the pebbles afterwards.

At twenty-five past eleven, there was time for something small.

---

The receiver returned from his break and picked up the waiting work.

Aadhya heard a parcel being counted, the scrape of paper under a palm, a quiet apology when someone had misunderstood a number. The two workers were folding the repaired length so that it could be lifted without dragging. Somewhere beyond the first court there was a burst of laughter which had nothing to do with the visitors.

The realm did not pause to watch Veer decide which colour to choose.

She liked that.

On their first arrival, she had been so busy measuring the distance between beauty and the kicked bucket that almost every detail had needed to become part of an argument. There had been good reason for the anger. The anger did not need the shrub to stop flowering, the cloth to remain torn or this child to lose her afternoon to be true.

Aadhya could sit with both things.

She watched the child count the pebbles once more, suspicious of the boy's engineering contribution to the pile. Six. No extra prize concealed beneath the cloth, no consequence outside the square in which they had agreed to play.

Veer chose the pale pieces.

The boy came back with a flatter stone. He put it outside the board, over the corner the wind had been lifting, and took his hand away with exaggerated care. This time the cloth stayed flat.

Aadhya nodded at the improvement.

"Ab theek."

"Pehle bhi theek tha," he said.

She did not argue. He had found the right thing after choosing two wrong ones, and apparently needed a minute before he could admit that a change had occurred. She knew the feeling well enough to give him the minute.

He sat where he could watch without blocking the worker's pile. Then he discovered that the new angle favoured Veer and shifted a little towards the girl instead.

His loyalties were becoming visible.

Aadhya left him to them. There would be no impartial commission at the dry end of this ledge, and she was beginning to suspect that the game would be better for it.

---

Veer had meant to say that the child should go first because she was younger.

Then he heard how pleased with himself that sentence would sound. She had made the board available, explained the way she and the boy were playing and offered to move his pieces when he named the squares. He could simply accept the offered company without placing a generous adult hand over the whole arrangement.

"Tu pehle," he said instead.

She picked up a dark pebble, but did not place it yet.

Veer was already studying the middle square. He could see how a wrong move would open the line opposite, then the next move, then the place from which he might have to retreat. The little folded field had acquired an extraordinary amount of consequence while still lying on somebody else's ledge.

Aadhya watched his face change.

There was the friend who could turn an argument about breakfast into a defence of principle. There was also the boy who had once believed that if he was not braced for the next impact, somebody beside him would have to take it instead. For a few minutes today the impact was going to be a child telling him he had moved a stone badly.

He could survive that without pretending it did not matter at all.

The girl followed his gaze towards the middle. Then she glanced at Aadhya, at the other five pebbles and back at the visitor who looked as though the little square had insulted his family.

She held her piece above the cloth, waiting for him to meet her eyes before she began.

"Khelna hai ya sirf jeetna?"
