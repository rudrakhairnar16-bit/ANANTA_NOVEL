# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-TWO — THE SEATS THEY CHOSE

---

> *"Nine of us went beyond the last working phone.*
> *That was not the same as nine people able to lift."*
> *— Ira's later register, first Swarg visit*

---

"Ban sakta hai," the clinician said. "Shartein poori hui toh. Sirf baat karne ka iraada tumhaari peeth ko safar se chhutti nahi dega."

Rudra looked at the therapist. She had not brought an exercise sheet shaped like a ticket.

They discussed a short inquiry visit, not a move into another realm. He would need the supported position which the vehicle could actually provide, an observed trial of the ordinary journey's demands, help that did not depend on his injured friends and a return before the next care was due. The inspected line had no compulsory stairs. Its tighter turn still had to accept the foot position his body needed.

Nobody at this bedside could certify an unfamiliar world harmless.

"Wahan se phone nahi laga," Sitara said through the speaker.

The clinician asked her to repeat where contact had worked, how far the chair had gone, and what getting back would require if somebody could no longer walk comfortably. Then she asked Rudra whether he understood that immediate help there would be only the help they took with them.

He said what he understood instead of saying that he was not frightened.

The appointment produced a sequence of tasks, not a departure date. Sitara's call ended before her next work, and the therapist adjusted the plan for the coming days.

Rudra stayed in the chair a little longer, looking at the cupboard shelf he had chosen.

For the first time, leaving it was work someone was willing to help him consider.

---

On day one hundred and seventy-four, they tried the foot-support position on the actual reclining transport chair.

The ankle stayed supported. The knee did not have to fold into the narrower turn; the chair's approach and the support's outward angle changed together. Rudra reported the pressure at the heel before the trial began. A worker changed the padding, and the therapist checked again before anyone rolled him through a measured turn in the ward.

Two uninjured workers handled the movement. His hands stayed where they had been put.

The first stop was deliberate. So was the return.

He was accustomed to noticing a floor's defects as things a body should overcome. From this seat, a raised strip of metal at the doorway belonged equally to the person controlling the wheel. One worker slowed, told him what was coming and took the chair over it without tipping his back into a surprise.

Afterwards they checked the ankle, knee and sore elbow. The grip had not been tested by asking him to save himself.

He had travelled the length of a corridor without making it a training ground.

---

The longer sitting trial was less flattering.

Halfway through the interval, the ache spread along the place beside his spine. Rudra wanted to wait until the therapist returned, to report it after completing the number everyone could see on the clock.

He called the worker instead.

They stopped the trial, changed the supported position and had him reviewed. The next attempt would be shorter and the road plan would need a real pause, with space for the chair to recline while stationary. They did not erase the failed interval because he had already managed the doorway.

That evening he sent Veer a message saying the chair was better at corners than he was at time.

"Toh chair ko class representative bana do," Veer replied.

Rudra laughed with the phone propped beside him, then let himself be annoyed for the rest of the evening.

He could want to go without pretending the setback was secretly a gift.

---

The second funded fortnight of care was coming to its end. The ward gave the next proposed period and charges before it did. Rudra and Ram discussed a bounded extension through the planned reassessments and return care; payment arrived and was acknowledged. There was still no departure to pay for merely because another week had been paid for here.

On day one hundred and seventy-nine, after further ordinary work and a fresh examination, Rudra made the short road trial with the clinical team arranged for it.

This was a new hired run with its own driver and end time. The chair was secured in an accessible vehicle, its occupant restraint checked separately. A qualified worker travelled beside him. At the agreed stopping place they waited, adjusted his position as planned and asked about symptoms before continuing the return.

The journey actually brought him back to the ward. The agreed vehicle-and-worker charge was paid through the booking contact and acknowledged; the driver left. The travelling worker handed the current findings to the receiving nurse and ended that run.

There had been no faint, white-edged sight or new breathlessness. The back ached within the position changes they had used; the joints needed their supports afterwards. The next morning's review included that ache, not just the absence of an alarm.

The clinician considered the finding with him.

A limited visit with a rest stop and suitable help was now something they could plan. Fighting, walking the whole way or lifting somebody else was not hidden inside that sentence.

Rudra asked that the actual finding, including the stop, go to his friends.

---

Aadhya had already asked her own clinician about the walking, the road and the hours between hand care.

She did not arrive at the conversation with permission borrowed from Rudra. The coverings were lighter, the skin reviewed, the altered feeling still present. She could make the proposed ordinary walking interval with rest; carrying, force through the protected hands and treating somebody else were not part of it.

"Main tumhaari first-aid bag nahi hoon," she told Rudra when the six-person audio call began.

"Bag ka zip toh khul jaata hai."

"Phir bag se baat kar lena."

He grinned towards the propped phone. Vedant let the joke finish before giving the actual proposal.

The hundred-and-eighty-fourth day, if the final findings held. A short first visit, on the already inspected line, no more than ninety minutes beyond the last reliable phone point including the walking both ways. Earlier return for anybody's care or request. A clinical worker with them, another on Earth, two suitable attendants, no injured friend assigned a handle.

Those people still had to accept those jobs. The proposal would not become a booking by being repeated confidently enough.

"Devta se usi din baat hogi?" Veer asked.

"Pakka nahi," Vedant said. "Pehla kaam us jagah par apna sawaal pahunchana hai jahan se sahi sunne waale tak jaa sake."

"Matlab queue."

"Ho sakti hai."

"Swarg mein bhi. Achha."

Rudra heard Ira laugh once, quietly, and then ask for her phone to be moved out of the glare.

---

Veer's own trial had been a walk out and back with a seat where his knee needed one, not a march in which the last person to sit lost.

The shoulder remained restricted under load. The ribs had allowed a fuller breath without the old catch during his review; discomfort at the end of a longer effort still needed reporting. He could accompany the proposed visit as a passenger and a walker with pauses, not its emergency carrying plan.

"Tumko mujhe uthaana nahi hai," Rudra said.

"Pata hai."

"Bas bol raha hoon."

"Mujhe bhi wahan dekhna hai. Har jagah tumhaare peeche bhaagne nahi aata main."

There was enough irritation in it for Rudra to hear the affection without being asked to thank him for it.

Veer asked whether an ordinary chair could be available if walking became too much. Sitara said a light spare would need to go with the group, and the return would begin before it became a demonstration of how long he could do without one.

He accepted the condition. He did not volunteer his shoulder to make the spare somebody else's problem.

---

Ira had made her own choice after the eye review.

Limited ordinary right-eye use, the left still covered, glare avoided, a guided line and rest which did not have to be earned by describing something useful. If the first court was too bright despite the shade, she would ask to leave. The others would not send her back alone while they pursued a better view.

"Aur koi reading nahi," she said.

"Humne maangi kab?" Rudra asked.

"Abhi nahi. Baad mein bhi yaad rakhna."

He said he would.

She wanted to go as someone who could hear a reply and ask the next question. The notebook could travel closed. There was no reason for the page to arrive more ready than the eye which might have to look at it.

Aadhya asked whether she wanted their questions read aloud before anything was written down. Ira said yes, but not on this call; her interval was nearly finished.

They stopped discussing her arrangements there. Nobody made her stay until every other person had chosen too.

---

After the call, Aadhya sat looking at the small bag she used for appointments.

The zip pull had become awkward in the exact way Rudra had found amusing. She could hold it if she watched the fingers. She could not trust the missing part of the sensation merely because she knew the shape by heart.

She asked the worker to open it and put the care items where they could be found without emptying everything on a strange floor.

Then she asked for the blue scarf.

"Dhoop ke liye?"

"Haan. Aur blue achha lagta hai."

The worker found it among the folded clothes.

For months, what she wore had been decided first by which bandage could pass through which sleeve. She was not returning the hands to normal by choosing a colour. She was keeping one uninjured part of the decision for herself.

She told Rudra that evening that the scarf would be her contribution to navigation.

"Rasta galat hua toh blue ke peeche mat aa jaana."

"Main toh kursi waale ke peeche aaunga."

"Tum usi mein hoge."

"Phir toh leadership clear hai."

She shook her head, smiling after the line went quiet.

When Sitara asked later whether the limited plan still suited her if the final arrangements were accepted, Aadhya gave her own yes. So did Veer and Ira, through separate replies. Rudra's answer went out after his next position change, not before it.

---

The inspection attendants gave their answers after seeing the actual plan.

One checked the turn against the chair's now-approved support position. The other asked who would decide to come back if the waiting people at the window made the visit run late.

"Ghadi," Sitara said. "Aur jiski tabiyat ya marzi pehle badle. Window nahi."

They accepted a new paid day, not an extension of the inspection job which had ended. A spare folding chair, the main chair's handling and the ordinary bags belonged to their work. Veer's size and Aadhya's hands did not appear in the lifting arrangement.

Two qualified clinical workers considered the care instructions with each person's permission. One would accompany the crossing after inspecting the current line herself. The other would remain on Earth with the vehicle and whoever was waiting there. They were told what the mentors knew and did not know about the destination, the failed phone tests, the length of the return and the absence of a hospital they could promise beyond the opening.

Either could stop the visit. Neither had been hired to fight.

They agreed to the limited work, including a fresh out-and-back inspection before patients entered. The quoted fees covered that time even if the visit ended at the road. The receiving ward accepted the return arrangements through its ordinary contact. That phone number and the road location were checked with the driver assigned to the new booking.

He was not the driver from the inspection car.

---

The accessible minibus had ten ordinary belted places, counting the driver's, and a secured-chair bay. That made room for eleven people without turning a passage between seats into a place for a patient.

Four friends. Two mentors. Two attendants. Two clinical workers. One driver.

Rudra would remain in the approved chair with its own restraints and occupant belt. The spare would travel folded. There was room to keep the planned recline without someone's knees or bag beneath it.

The booking was seven-thirty until four, including the loading time. The driver would wait at the Earth stopping place, with the party back to the road and ready to leave by one at the latest. The first visit's ninety-minute limit would be shorter still. No promise of an audience could spend the reserved time for getting everybody home.

They checked the total against the contributions actually available, after care and the completed road trial. Rudra discussed the remaining amount with Ram; the agreed travel contribution arrived before the deposit was sent. Sitara paid that deposit through the booking contact and received confirmation. The balance, including the clinical workers and attendants, would be due when the accepted return work ended. A separate reserve stayed unspent.

The last particulars went to each friend. Each confirmed the same limited plan, not a later version which quietly included staying overnight.

Ira asked for one addition: when someone read the question aloud, they should not improve it because they thought it sounded too simple.

"Simple hai toh jawaab de denge," Veer said.

"Yeh assumption bhi mat likhna."

---

Rudra called his parents after his afternoon reassessment and their own care on the hundred-and-eighty-third evening.

Ram had asked Rukmini whether she wanted to join. She did. Rudra told both of them the current ordinary findings before describing the trip. The hand remained weak under sustained work. The foot and knee had their supports. The trial had required a stop for the back. No form had been practised to replace any of those things.

Then he named Swarg, the source of the suggestion and the separate passage Vedant and Sitara had actually checked.

It was a first, short inquiry visit. The return line had been walked in both directions; tomorrow's fresh finding could still cancel the crossing. He gave the people travelling, the two people remaining with the Earth vehicle, the care plan, the time of the expected return message and the ordinary receiving arrangement.

He was not giving a speech about becoming invulnerable in a more beautiful place.

"Paani apna le jaa rahe ho?" Rukmini asked.

"Haan. Aur khaana."

"Bottle khud kholne ki zid mat karna."

"Mummy."

"Pichhli baar bhi Mummy hi thi."

He smiled. His fingers had recently failed at a lid he would have liked to dismiss as trivial.

Ram asked whether the final review had included the old faint. Rudra said yes: no recurrence during the observed work, the cause not declared solved, the warning symptoms and stop plan retained. The answer satisfied the question without making his father the person who could grant medical permission over the phone.

They ended before his mother's shoulder became uncomfortable.

The call contained a destination and practical care, not a new audience for the private discussion of the Cycle or a reason to settle every older hurt tonight.

---

Dhruv separately accepted the agreed departure, Earth-arrival and return reports, around his own care. The ward's duty contact was the backup if he could not answer; his willingness did not require him to sit awake beside a phone for an unnamed number of days.

He asked Rudra what he was taking to the window.

"Ek sawaal. Saat Lokas ka santulan sambhaalne waalon ka kaam samajhna hai. Baat kis se kar sakte hain?"

No parent's identity, no private account offered to an unknown listener as the admission price. The four friends had heard that plain question and chosen to begin with it.

Dhruv said it was clear.

Rudra slept after the next care. The bag stayed packed across the room, where wanting to leave could not turn into an experiment with his wrist.

---

The hundred-and-eighty-fourth morning began with the examinations and care already owed, not with somebody shaking Rudra awake because the vehicle was outside.

He described the night's sleep, the back when he turned, the ankle and the grip. The current findings stayed within the limited plan. Veer, Aadhya and Ira gave their own morning accounts to their workers; no one's settled night was used as evidence about somebody else's body.

At half past seven the paid travel work began.

The new clinical workers took the handovers before the attendants touched a chair. Two attendants and the accompanying worker made Rudra's prescribed supported transfer, keeping his wrist out of his body's weight and the ankle clear. The worker asked him about the back before the ramp, not after discovering that a silent patient could still smile.

He answered her. The chair stopped while a fold was corrected.

The vehicle bay accepted it without moving another passenger's legs out into the aisle. Its locks and occupant belt were checked. The spare chair was secured folded, the bags separately stowed. Nothing would have to be caught with an injured hand if the driver braked.

Aadhya came into view wearing blue.

For a second Rudra knew her first as the voice which had filled so many dark phone screens, and then as the person stopping beside him to see whether the angle of his head was comfortable.

"Leadership clear?" she asked.

"Abhi tak."

Veer chose the seat which gave his knee the room agreed. Ira accepted Sitara's ordinary guidance at the doorway, then settled where glare did not fall straight across her uncovered eye. Her notebook stayed in the bag she had asked an attendant to carry.

Ten belts were occupied. Rudra's separate restraints made the eleventh place.

At eight, after Sitara's brief departure report had actually reached Dhruv, the driver pulled away.

---

Varanasi was not outside the window. Mirzapur was: repair shops opening, a cycle loaded beyond its owner's good sense, a woman moving her bucket out of the dust before a truck passed.

Rudra had spent enough weeks looking at a fixed patch of outside to find the moving ugliness beautiful.

He did not say so. Veer was already complaining about a shop which appeared to be selling tea to people who had not yet finished putting up its shutters. They argued about whether that showed excellent service or a dangerous absence of priorities.

The clinical worker asked Rudra about the back before the planned break.

He said the ache was beginning, in the familiar place.

The driver took the agreed stopping space rather than waiting for it to become severe. With the vehicle stationary, the workers used the chair's permitted adjustment, offered water and let the interval be rest. There was no performance of getting out to prove that a pause had happened.

Aadhya drank from the bottle someone opened for her. Ira closed the ordinary eye too and listened to a bicycle bell approaching, passing, going away.

Vedant checked his own forearm's position. The altered feeling in the fingers had not been left at the ward because he was guiding a journey.

When it was time to continue, Rudra's supported travel position and the restraints were checked again. Everyone belted before the vehicle moved.

The second part of the drive belonged to fields and the low ridge approaching beyond them.

No banner marked the turn they took.

---

They reached the Earth stopping place at twenty to ten.

The driver checked the ground for the ramp and parked clear of the road, with the way out available. The clinical worker who would remain outside made the arrival call. Dhruv answered; she gave only the agreed practical report and confirmed the backup contact before ending it.

Rudra stayed supported while the mentors looked at the opening from the road side.

It was smaller than the name inside it.

A shallow shade divided the rock. A thorny branch caught a piece of windblown paper beside it, then released it. He could have passed the turn on an ordinary journey and thought only of whether the road surface would improve.

That was somehow worse, and better, than the entrance he had imagined.

Vedant came back to the vehicle before anyone was unloaded. The exterior line was usable. They would make the fresh inward and outward check now; the four friends would remain with the Earth clinical worker and the driver until it was finished.

The accompanying worker took her own bag. The attendants brought the spare chair and the recorded dimensions of Rudra's wider foot-support position. They would inspect the current turn against those measurements, not discover its fit with his knee inside it.

Sitara asked each of the walking party if they were ready for the accepted inspection interval. Five people gave their own answers, including her.

They went in together.

Rudra watched the last attendant disappear beyond the first bend. The spare chair made a little ordinary rattle, then stopped being audible from the vehicle.

The Earth worker stayed close enough to ask whether he wanted the back position changed while they waited.

"Haan," he said.

The permission to leave was still being made of things like that.

---

At the tighter turn, the attendants checked the available space against the recorded width and angle, not against the spare chair's easier outline.

The accompanying worker watched the shallow slopes from both sides. She asked where she could stop a chair without it standing across everybody else's return. Vedant showed the broad place before the second bend, then walked back through it with her so that the answer belonged to the journey home as well.

They continued to the court. The shade lay where Sitara had described it; people were already waiting at the paper window. A space beside the bench remained clear. It was not theirs to reserve by looking at it.

The call attempt failed again there.

They returned together, checking the turn from the Swarg side before coming out. Both mentors were back at the road with the accompanying worker and the two attendants by quarter past ten. The report was given in person to the Earth worker and the four friends. Today's finding still fitted the short plan. No one called it an assurance about the buildings beyond the court.

Rudra asked the accompanying worker whether she still accepted the crossing after seeing it.

"Haan. Isi had tak," she said. "Aur waapas aana pehle bhi ho sakta hai."

It was the answer they had paid her to be free to give.

---

The attendants brought Rudra down the ramp under control, with the worker checking the ankle's clearance and his account of the back. They did not need him to grip a rail or take a hurried step onto the road.

Aadhya stood in the shade outside, blue drawn loosely over her head. Veer walked the short approach at his own pace. Ira asked Sitara for an elbow to follow, not a hand pulling her towards a view she owed somebody.

The spare chair went folded with an attendant. The accompanying worker kept her care bag. Water, food and the other ordinary items travelled in the places agreed, not in a protected palm which had quietly become useful again.

The Earth worker wrote the leaving time on her sheet.

Ten twenty.

Back at the working-phone side by eleven fifty at the latest. The visit could end sooner. The driver and Earth worker would remain here, with the route to receiving care and the agreed contacts, not vanish after waving them in.

Sitara asked the four friends separately whether the current finding changed their choice.

It had not. Each answered. The five people accompanying them confirmed their own readiness too.

Nine went towards the opening. Two stayed with the vehicle.

Rudra heard its door being drawn partly closed to keep the dust out, a little sound which made the return place more definite than any promise about destiny could have done.

---

The passage took the wheels one turn at a time.

There was no moment in which his body stopped being the body they had brought. The back still felt the change of slope. The wrist stayed supported. At the narrower place, the attendant stopped and spoke to the other man before moving the chair through the angle already checked.

The ordinary scrape of a wheel rose against the stone and died.

Rudra let the pause last. He did not summon force because it might make accepting the next push feel less like being helped.

Vedant stayed visible ahead. The accompanying worker asked whether the pressure at the ankle remained comfortable; Rudra answered before the movement resumed. Behind him, Veer said he wanted the brief pause at the broad place. They stopped there together. Ira rested the right eye while Sitara described the next shallow stretch in the few words needed to walk it.

Then they continued.

The air changed before the opening did. Less enclosed, carrying water and something sweet without the burnt edge of incense.

Rudra saw a strip of pale paving, a moving shadow, the hem of somebody's clothes.

He had imagined arrival as a blow to the senses. Instead it came by portions, each waiting for the wheel to finish its ordinary turn.

The far opening widened.

Beyond it, terraces climbed through green and white and the patient gold of roofs in daylight. Water descended in a long fall between two walls, breaking into brightness before it gathered again below. Cloth moved over the first court in broad slow breaths. Under it, people went about errands which had begun before the visitors set out.

Nobody announced them.

Aadhya drew breath beside the chair. Veer forgot the remark he had been preparing. Ira asked whether the shade was close, and Sitara said yes, the bench was just ahead.

The accompanying worker checked her watch. They had their time, not all the time the beauty appeared willing to offer.

The attendants moved the chair onto the pale paving and stopped before the first court, where the party could settle without obstructing the opening. Rudra looked once towards the water, then towards the shaded bench. The next task was a rest, and after that a plain question at a plain window.

A man in a clean pale wrap stepped out from beside a post.

"Kursi idhar nahi," he said.

The attendant stopped.

There was room. Rudra could see it: a whole empty end of bench, a broad strip beside it, shade lying unused across stone.

The man pointed towards a well-dressed visitor approaching from the court's other side.

"Saaya unke liye chhod do."

Aadhya looked from the empty place to Rudra's supported foot.

"Kyun?"
