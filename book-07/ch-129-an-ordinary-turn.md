# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-NINE — AN ORDINARY TURN

---

> *"The loss required no dressing and no entry in the treatment file.*
> *It was nevertheless remembered with considerable accuracy."*
> *— Ira's later register, the pebble-game account*

---

"Khelna," Veer said. "Jeetna mila toh woh bhi."

The girl appeared to accept the distinction. She placed a dark pebble in the middle square.

He told her to put his first pale one to its left.

Aadhya watched the moves arrive without anybody reaching across a painful shoulder. The child named the square back when Veer's pointing was vague, then put the piece where he actually meant. The boy leaned towards the cloth until she told him his shadow was not one of the playing pieces.

Her second stone went above the middle. Veer blocked the bottom of that column. She placed her third at the upper left; he put his last one at the upper right, closing the top row before she could complete it.

Six pebbles on nine squares.

He felt suitably prepared.

---

The girl moved her middle pebble one square to the right.

Veer studied the empty centre, then asked her to move his left-hand piece down into the bottom corner. The girl slid her top-middle stone down into the square she had just left.

Aadhya could see something changing. She could not tell whether Veer saw the same thing or a more complicated, less useful version.

He asked for his upper-right piece to be moved left.

"Idhar?" the girl checked, touching the empty top-middle square.

"Haan."

She moved it there.

Then she slid her remaining upper-left stone down one square.

Three dark pebbles lay across the middle row.

The boy hit his own knee with both hands, delighted by an outcome to which he had contributed a flat stone and considerable noise. Aadhya looked at Veer's face and began to laugh.

Veer looked at the row, then at the girl, then at the row again.

"Haan," he said. "Yeh toh ho gaya."

He had been beaten by the moves he had watched being made. No hidden rule had appeared after his choice. The little defeat belonged to him so completely that there was nothing to defend except his right to be briefly annoyed.

He exercised that right for perhaps three seconds.

Then he laughed too.

---

"Phir se?" the child asked.

The clinical worker checked the time and Veer's current position. He gave his own answer before looking at the board again. The knee was still in its familiar state, the shoulder comfortable at rest. He wanted to keep the time left for the return, not bargain it away because a loss had happened inside a square of cloth.

"Aaj ek hi," he said. "Warna main haar ko administrative error bolne lagoonga."

The girl did not understand all of that, but she understood one game. She began taking the pebbles off.

Aadhya thanked her for making the place on the ledge available. The boy lifted the weight from the corner as though the whole success had depended on him. This time nobody argued. He had, in fact, made the board stay flat.

At thirty-three past eleven, the six stones were back in the girl's palm.

No prize changed hands. She folded her own cloth and carried it to the space where she and the boy had been playing before the visitors became interesting. The game could continue there without Veer. His absence would not be an unfinished task they had to hold open.

He watched them go with an affection disproportionate to the eight minutes they had shared.

---

"Haarna seekh liya?" Aadhya asked quietly.

"Nahi. Ek baar haara hoon. Certificate mat de."

She smiled at that.

He was glad she had not praised him for allowing a child to win. She had seen the actual game; she knew he had tried. There was room in her laughter for his effort as well as its unremarkable result.

The court carried on around them.

The receiver tied a stack with cord. The younger worker lifted one end of the repaired cloth while the woman checked that the fold did not pull at the fresh join. Under the farther awning someone was still eating. Aadhya could hear a spoon touching the edge of a vessel between the larger sounds of water and people.

Veer had wanted to know what happened in this place when nobody needed him.

For a few minutes he had been allowed to find out.

He did not mistake that small welcome for the answer to every cruelty a beautiful place could contain. He also did not have to make it smaller to prove that he remembered the bucket.

He thanked the woman for the ledge and the earlier shade. She looked up from the fold, nodded towards the clear return space and went back to checking the cloth.

The clinical worker asked whether they were ready for the short last rest before leaving.

Both friends said yes.

---

At the last rest, Aadhya watched the folded length go onto the woman's pile.

Finished did not mean finished forever. The cloth had been made usable for this piece of work, on this day, by people who would eventually have to mend something else. The cloth's beauty included the hand smoothing a stubborn corner, not only the light which would later pass through it.

She wanted to say that, but found no version which would not sound like the opening of Ira's terrible project introduction.

"Achha lag raha hai," she said instead.

The woman looked at the fold.

"Seedha bhi ho gaya aakhir."

The younger worker protested that it had been almost straight before. She let *almost* remain between them, and he began laughing at his own defence.

Aadhya thanked them for the room. She did not ask the woman to turn a kindness into a statement about her whole realm.

The receiver looked up when Sitara told him they were leaving. He asked whether they needed the earlier paper again.

No. Their copy was safely with its owner, Sitara said. Today's visit had not been another hearing. He nodded and went back to the stack. A question could remain answered without keeping everyone who had helped with it permanently at a visitor's disposal.

---

The worker checked Veer's knee before the return walk, and Aadhya's account of the hand. Familiar findings, no new strain; the sitting rest had done what it was there to do. Both mentors gave their own present condition. They would take the broad-place pause again.

The attendant folded each stool after the person using it had risen. He checked beneath them for the small things which could be left behind when gratitude made people forget their bags.

Nothing belonging to the children was among the gear.

Veer looked back once. The girl had already begun another game with the boy, who had apparently decided that he no longer objected to going second. He was studying the middle with an expression Veer recognised more readily now.

Aadhya caught the look.

"Usko warn karna hai?"

"Maine bhi nahi suna hota."

They left the joke with the view.

At quarter to twelve, all six turned towards the opening.

---

The way back did not turn into a trial because the visit had been pleasant.

Vedant checked the short line ahead before the tighter part. Sitara remained where she could hear both young travellers. The attendant carried the two stools and bags; the clinical worker kept the pace within what had been reviewed, not within the speed of their enthusiasm for lunch.

At the broad place, Veer asked for the agreed pause before the knee needed a longer explanation. The worker heard the familiar fatigue, checked the present finding and let the rest happen. Aadhya was glad to sit for the short interval too. The attendant opened the stools where their feet could settle without blocking the remaining clear line.

Nobody used the wall as a test of a protected hand. The stools were folded again before the attendant took the gear.

When they continued, the water's sound withdrew behind the stone. Veer found that he could remember the little click of a pebble more clearly than the largest part of the view. He would have to tell Rudra that and accept the consequences of having admitted an actual defeat.

Aadhya followed the turn into the ordinary light outside.

The driver was at the vehicle. The Earth worker came to meet the returning six while the attendant put the gear down clear of the path.

Her watch read three minutes past twelve.

All six were back before the quarter-past limit.

---

The Earth worker received the accompanying report before asking the travellers their own questions.

Veer's knee fatigue, relieved by the pause but still needing its next care. Aadhya's hand unchanged, ordinary effort in the legs after the walk, no new symptom disguised beneath the word *fine*. The mentors' arms remained protected and their own walking fatigue was given its proper place in the report.

Eight people were reunited at the vehicle.

The phone connected again. The practical return report went to the receiving contact and to Dhruv; both acknowledgments came back through the ordinary arrangement. Nobody had been waiting for a voice to carry around the failed bend.

Veer did not begin the game account while the worker was checking the knee.

There was time for it later, and time now for food, water and an easier position. The driver took his own meal during the accepted wait. The attendant helped with the bags and the fastenings, not an extra errand back through the opening because someone had remembered a thing they wished to see.

Aadhya ate the food she had brought.

For a while the eight people sat or moved within the ordinary space beside the vehicle, attending to the needs which made a return possible. The sun caught a loose thread on the blue scarf. She tucked it clear with the hand she could use safely and left the knot beneath it alone.

The road home was still waiting to be travelled.

---

At half past twelve, the Earth worker checked readiness for the road.

Veer gave the knee's present feeling after food and rest. Aadhya answered for her own hands and walking fatigue. Vedant and Sitara had their current checks too. The easier positions had helped; the need for receiving care had not vanished because nobody had required an emergency response.

The stools and bags were secured again. Eight people took their eight separate places, and the two clinical workers checked the actual positions and belts before the door closed.

At twenty to one, the driver left the ridge.

There were twenty minutes left before the latest departure they had accepted. They used it as margin, not as time owed to one more look inside.

---

The road home carried a different light.

Aadhya could see dust lying in the seams of a shop's folded awning, then a repaired corner pulling against its tie. She had noticed that sort of thing before; now she found herself looking for the person who would eventually climb up to mend it again.

Beside her, Veer was trying not to fall asleep with an expression which would invite comment later.

He failed at the second task first.

"Main dekh rahi hoon," she said.

"Main aankhon ko rest de raha hoon."

"Expression ko bhi de."

He gave up defending it and rested.

At the agreed road pause, the attendant checked the access and the workers helped with the ordinary break. The knee changed position; the shoulder took no hurried load at the door. Aadhya used the help needed for a fastening without treating a successful morning as a reason to stop watching the fingers. Privacy took its own minutes. The driver had his own pause before returning to the wheel.

Supports and belts were checked again.

On the remaining drive the friends were mostly quiet. They had enough to tell each other later without having to rehearse the account above the noise of the road. The two clinical workers kept their practical notes current. The attendant put his own bottle away, then checked that no loose bag had crept into a foot space.

They reached the receiving place at twenty-five past two.

---

The receiving workers had the practical return report.

They heard the actual travelling handovers and asked each of the four returning people directly about the day. Veer did not give the absence of a fall as an answer about his knee. Aadhya described what the uncertain fingers had and had not done. The mentors gave their own arm and walking findings without making the two younger travellers' good return a substitute for theirs.

Care was accepted before the travelling staff ended it.

Veer's next check and settled knee position came first. Aadhya's hands were reviewed in their own turn; rest and the existing plan remained. Vedant's forearm and Sitara's covered hand were no more available for lifting than they had been in the morning. Ordinary fatigue was recorded, not hidden under the phrase *a nice day*.

Only after those handovers did the attendant take the stools and borrowed equipment to the hiring worker who had come to receive them.

The inventory was checked together: folds, fastenings, bags and carried supplies. The fastening adjusted before departure had held. The equipment was accepted back without an unmentioned defect folded into the seat.

---

After her own review, Sitara settled the new booking through the agreed contact.

The acknowledged deposit was deducted once. The driver checked and acknowledged the vehicle amount against this return journey. The attendant and both clinical workers each received the full accepted day fee and confirmed receipt. The work had ended inside the reserved block; nobody's fee was shortened because the return had gone well.

The unspent reserve was reconciled against the contributors' record. That number was not a fresh promise to pay for another trip, nor a settlement of the old damaged vehicle's separate outstanding account.

The attendant took his own bag and left after the equipment handover. The clinical workers confirmed that their reports had been received with the accepted care, collected their things and departed. With his own payment confirmed, the driver drove the vehicle away.

By twenty past three, the hired workers had actually gone.

The two mentors were in their own rest. Veer and Aadhya were in receiving care, with their ordinary belongings and no new duty to keep the day entertaining for somebody else.

This outing was finished.

---

Aadhya sent the practical completion message when her interval allowed it: returned, checked, paid work settled, resting. She asked Rudra and Ira separately whether they wanted to hear about the court after their own care and her rest.

There was no attached photograph to open and no request for Ira to make a page ready.

Rudra answered first. Later would suit him, if the back allowed the short call. Ira replied after her quiet interval. Audio, briefly; she would decide the end by the time she had actually offered.

Veer wanted to join after his own rest too.

Aadhya put the phone aside before trying to turn the loss into a perfect story. It had been funny without rehearsal. There was every chance it could remain so after sleep.

---

At half past five, after rest and fresh agreement, the four friends spoke by audio.

Rudra's nurse had checked the supported position and the short interval he wanted. Ira was listening with the ordinary eye resting, the left covering undisturbed. Veer had the knee settled. Aadhya's phone was placed where the hand did not have to hold it.

They began with the current bodies and the actual return, not a demand for the best thing anyone had seen.

Then Veer said he had lost.

Rudra went quiet for a moment.

"Kis se?"

"Ek bachchi se. Patthar waala khel tha."

"Poora bata. Isme brevity ki zaroorat nahi."

"Time agreed hai. Uske andar hi meri beizzati hogi."

Aadhya laughed and took the beginning of the account: six pebbles, a folded cloth, an unnecessarily large weight and a child who had offered to move Veer's pieces so his shoulder would not have to reach.

Ira asked whether the child had moved them to the wrong places.

"Nahi," Veer said. "Wahi problem hai."

---

They told the game without making Ira assemble a picture she had not asked to hold.

Three in a straight row. A move named clearly and repeated before it happened. An obvious loss which Veer had needed several looks to acknowledge. The other boy taking credit for keeping the board flat, with some justification.

Rudra laughed so hard at the last part that he felt the familiar pull in the back. He stopped, told the nurse what had happened and waited through her check before continuing. No alarm followed; the ache still belonged in the account of his own evening.

"Hansne ki bhi permission limited hai," he told them.

"Maine warning di thi," Veer said. "Brevity rakhni chahiye thi."

The joke survived becoming quieter.

Ira asked what they had brought away from the game.

"Kuchh nahi," Aadhya said. "Unki gotiyaan, unka kapda. Wahi reh gaya."

"Achha."

It was not distrust of an ordinary souvenir. The game had been real without something needing to be collected from it, and nobody had to open a painful eye to make its proof adequate.

For a minute the only important inquiry was whether Veer had wanted a rematch badly enough to sulk. Aadhya testified that the sulk had lasted approximately three seconds. Veer disputed the precision, not the existence of the sulk.

Rudra thought that was a respectable position under the circumstances.

---

Then Aadhya gave the rest of the court.

The awning repaired with a different shade of thread. A younger worker insisting that a crooked edge was almost straight. The woman needing her space back and the visitors actually moving when it was time. A man in beautiful clothes waiting behind someone who had arrived before him. The receiver taking a meal, the people with parcels rearranging themselves around the wait.

She told these as things she had seen that morning, not a code by which all Swarg could be read.

"Kisi ka rank pata chala?" Rudra asked.

"Nahi. Poochhne ki zaroorat bhi nahi padi. Do kapde sambhaal rahe the. Do bachche khel rahe the. Us waqt utna hi mila."

The woman had not been a kindness-machine left there for their comfort. She had her own pile and an end to the time she could give. Aadhya liked that the ending had been ordinary, not an offence they had to forgive her for.

Ira asked whether they had been expected at the window.

No appointment. No hearing this time. The receiver had finished with someone else first; Sitara had said they were only visiting. They had asked the cloth worker for the small strip, then moved near the offered ledge when the work needed that strip back.

"Toh unhone apna din nahi chhoda," Ira said.

"Humein thodi jagah di," Veer answered.

He sounded pleased by the difference.

---

Rudra listened without finding an unasked question which would make him responsible for the next departure.

He had wanted to go. He still wished he could have heard the child explaining the squares, or seen Veer recognise the middle row too late. That wish did not make the story a report of something stolen from him.

He could be glad his friends had enjoyed a day which his own body could not yet manage.

The admission did not feel noble. It felt like making room in a crowded bag without throwing out something he needed.

"Agli baar mere saamne mat haarna," he told Veer. "Main chup nahi rahoonga."

"Agli baar abhi book nahi hui."

"Mujhe bhi pata hai. Hypothetical beizzati allowed hai."

Aadhya said she would not adjudicate it. Ira said that was wise, and there was another brief, careful laugh before the practical question about how much of the interval remained.

They had not solved the failing balance between breakfast and the road home. They had found people whose morning had contained cloth, food, a place in a queue and a game worth winning.

That was what the travellers could honestly bring to this call.

---

Ira had reached the end of the interval she had offered.

She said so while the conversation was still pleasant. There was no need to wait until the eye hurt enough to make leaving convincing. She had heard the account; she did not need another description, a picture or a notebook entry tonight.

"Mujhe weight waala ladka pasand aaya," she said.

"Meri performance?" Veer asked.

"Usne improve kiya."

The verdict was delivered so gently that he had to laugh again.

Ira ended the call for her own rest. Veer was due for his next knee care and said goodbye too. Neither departure cancelled the shared few minutes. There would be another ordinary day in which they could disagree about the game without keeping this one open indefinitely.

Aadhya had reached her own time as well.

Rudra thanked her for the account and asked whether he could leave one short question later, if it stayed with him after care. Not ask her to remain awake for it.

"Message chhod dena," she said. "Apne waqt par sun loongi."

They ended the call.

---

The next position change came before the question.

Rudra gave the back's present feeling, including the pull during the laugh. The nurse checked it, kept the movement within the current plan and helped him settle with the elbow protected. The wrist did not take his weight. The ankle and knee needed their ordinary support after he was comfortable everywhere else.

He was tired.

Not dramatically, not in the way which might make exhaustion a more interesting proof of courage. He had listened, laughed, missed a place, been glad for his friends and needed help turning in a bed. Those things belonged to the same evening.

When the nurse asked what he wanted within reach, he chose the phone stand and the college page. The two Swarg sheets could stay in the cupboard. They had done the work for which they had crossed; a returned question did not need to supervise everything he thought afterwards.

The worker put the page at the comfortable angle, then read the short part he indicated. He corrected one word from the earlier dictation and asked to stop there. The little correction was sufficient work for now.

Afterwards he let the room become quiet.

---

Elsewhere in receiving care, Aadhya had the scarf folded into her bag.

The person helping her shook it lightly first. Nothing extraordinary fell from it. A thread had worked loose at the edge; it would need ordinary attention when there was time. She thought of the woman showing a sag in the fabric instead of arguing that it had always been straight.

The memory made her smile.

She had not photographed the child or asked the cloth worker to stand beside the finished fold. She could remember a face without making the person remain available inside an image. If a detail blurred later, it would blur; the loss of that detail would not turn the morning into a thing which had not happened.

Her own hand still needed care. She watched the fingers where watching helped, accepted the rest of the task and left the blue scarf to be put away by someone who could do it without testing an uncertain grip.

The day had not made her less interested in Swarg.

It had made her less interested in describing it as though its people were only evidence for or against the view.

---

Rudra could hear a scooter being started beyond the shabby wall outside.

He remembered wishing to tell somebody how ordinary that wall looked after his first crossing. Now an ordinary story had come back from the other side: a child wanting a proper opponent, a man wanting his meal, a woman wanting her work space returned before gratitude occupied the whole morning.

The beauty had not been exposed as a trick.

Neither had the cruelty.

He could still remember the kicked bucket without needing to put the man who kicked it inside every beautiful piece of cloth. He could remember the people who made room without appointing them witnesses for the defence of everyone who had refused it.

There was another person in that first court, though, whom the recollections kept leaving at the edge.

Not the well-dressed visitor. Not the man by the post.

The one holding the bucket.

Rudra knew the strap had pressed into the cloth at his shoulder. He had seen him set the weight down, heard him ask where it should go and watched him pick it up again. Aadhya had told him the spill had not been an accident because they had all been there to see the kick.

That was an account of something done to the man.

It was not yet the man's account of his day.

---

He did not know what else the worker had been carrying besides water: another task delayed, somebody waiting, a temper kept quiet for a reason the visitors could not see. He would not make those possibilities into facts because a story felt incomplete without them.

The unknown part did not entitle him to summon the man or spend his friends' next recovery day going to find him. Today's staff had gone. Today's money was settled. Aadhya had offered to hear a short message when her own time allowed, not accepted another errand.

Rudra asked the worker beside his bed for help with the phone, within the brief interval she could give. He recorded the question, listened once, then confirmed that it could go to Aadhya's existing contact. It asked only what she could know from having been there, not for an answer from every person in Swarg.

The message sent after that check was short.

"Jiska paani gira tha—us din uska apna kehna kisne suna?"
