# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-THREE — A PLACE IN THE SHADE

---

> *"We brought home a time to return, not a remedy.*
> *Also, a less innocent description of the view."*
> *— Ira's later register, first day across*

---

"Unhe yahin baithna hai," the man said.

Aadhya looked again. The approaching visitor had not reached the bench. He wore a pale embroidered wrap and was talking to somebody beside him, one hand describing an ordinary circle in the air.

"Poora saaya?" she asked. "Kursi side mein reh sakti hai."

"Udhar le jaao."

He indicated the open paving beyond the post, where the sunlight lay almost white.

There was no explanation about a reserved place. He had answered with a preference made large enough to move somebody else's body.

The accompanying worker stepped nearer the chair.

"Dhoop mein wait nahi karenge," she said to the attendants. "Jagah nahi mili toh lautenge."

She did not need to threaten the man with a power she had not brought. The limit concerned the people she had.

A woman beneath the opposite edge of hanging cloth lifted a bundle off a low stool.

"Idhar thodi jagah hai. Raasta khula chhod dena."

Sitara looked along the few steps of paving between them. It lay beside the inspected court, level and clear, with room to turn back without going around the bench. She asked the woman whether they could rest there for a short while. The woman agreed, until her work needed the space again.

The attendants moved the chair into that shade. The spare opened for Ira; Veer accepted the stool after the woman offered it. Nobody was left standing in the glare to make a point about the first man's manners.

Rudra answered the worker's questions before looking at the court again.

The back held the familiar ache. The ankle's position remained comfortable. There was no dizziness or new whiteness at the edges of his sight. She checked the supports and his present condition, then let the first rest actually be rest.

Ira closed her uncovered eye.

The woman went back to sorting her cloth. She had given them a place, not agreed to become the tenth person responsible for their journey.

---

Under the hanging shade, the beauty grew details.

Aadhya could see tiny repairs in the cloth overhead, thread a little darker where one tear had been mended. On the wall beyond it, a flowering shrub had pushed a thin green stem between two pieces of dressed stone. The fall of water she had heard from the opening ran down a narrow face cut by light, breaking into clear beads over the last ledge.

Nothing in her hands reached towards it.

She watched the leaves move because leaves moving were worth watching.

"Blue kaam aaya," Rudra said quietly.

She looked down. An edge of the scarf had fallen into a patch of sun and made its reflection gentler on the pale paving near Ira's chair.

"Invoice bhejoongi."

Ira smiled without opening the eye.

For a little while the three sounds nearest Aadhya were water, cloth, and Veer trying to sit without making the stool creak. The realm did not become less strange because ordinary things occupied it. It became strange in a more difficult way, close enough for her to want an afternoon there instead of a magnificent view from which she could hurry home.

Then the man beside the post struck the edge of a water bucket with his foot.

---

It happened on the far side of the bench, away from their return line.

A worker had set the bucket down and begun easing the carrying strap off his shoulder. The kick sent water over the edge into the planting bed. The worker caught the handle before the bucket overturned completely.

"Yahan mat rakha karo," the pale-wrapped man said.

There had been room beside the post. Aadhya could see that too.

The worker stood holding what remained, the strap pressed into a dark line across the cloth on his shoulder. He asked whether the water should be taken behind the bench instead.

"Pehle poora bhar ke lao."

The well-dressed visitor had arrived by then. He looked at the wet earth and asked why the bucket had been kicked.

The man beside the post began explaining where it ought to have been put.

"Hatane ko keh dete," the visitor said. Then, seeing the party under the other cloth, he asked why the chair was over there when this side had room.

Nobody offered him a version in which the chair had preferred the sun.

The worker with the bucket waited through the exchange, still holding its weight. Aadhya wanted to say something sharp enough to take that weight from his arm. There was no such sentence.

"Rakh lo," she called across the paving. "Yeh apne aap nahi gira tha."

He looked at her, then at the ground, and put it down.

---

Veer watched the well-dressed visitor lower himself onto one end of the bench, leaving the other empty.

The man by the post said something about keeping the place clear. The visitor shook his head and gestured towards the bucket standing on the paving. Whatever authority either possessed, Veer could not name it from the gesture. He knew only who had kicked, who had objected and who still had water to carry.

The worker rested the strap across the bucket instead of putting it back on his shoulder at once.

He rubbed the flattened cloth at his shoulder and watched the water settle. After a minute he picked the bucket up again and went towards the fall, while the two men continued talking.

"Kya hua?" Ira asked softly.

Veer gave her the actions he had seen. Her eye stayed closed. He did not add a rank to either man's clothes.

"Woh Devta tha?" Rudra asked.

"Mujhe kaise pata hoga?" Veer said. "Kapde dekhke designation thodi milta hai."

It was easier to laugh at the sentence than at what had made it necessary. Rudra did neither. He looked towards the empty shade again, then asked the accompanying worker whether the agreed rest was enough for the next short move.

She checked with each of them before answering.

The court still held the route they had come to use. It also held an ordinary ugly act nobody could improve by calling the whole view false.

---

The woman sorting cloth needed her stool nearer the bundle again.

Veer got up at his permitted pace and thanked her before an attendant put it back where she wanted it. Ira remained in the spare chair. The woman pointed to the strip under the window's own awning when Sitara asked about waiting there; there was shade over the queue too.

They did not make her promise it would stay empty.

Rudra asked whether she wanted something for the use of the space.

"Raasta chhod diya. Kaafi hai," she said, tugging a length of cloth free from beneath another.

He thanked her. She nodded without making their departure a ceremony. The bundle had work left inside it which did not concern them.

At the edge of the awning, Vedant checked the few intervening paces. Clear paving, a turning place, the way back still visible. The two attendants moved the chairs across, with the accompanying worker close enough to hear Rudra's answers. Veer walked beside them. Aadhya kept her hands out of the moving wheels.

The spare chair kept Ira from having to spend her ordinary sight on judging each bright joint in the stone. She told Sitara she wanted to listen at the window, and that the eye was comfortable closed. Sitara stayed beside her rather than inventing a view she ought to open it for.

Their worker checked the watch again.

Quarter to eleven.

There was time to ask. Not time to become whatever the waiting room demanded merely because they had crossed a realm to reach it.

---

The person ahead of them wanted a correction made to the number of cloth lengths received.

Rudra could not read the paper over the person's shoulder and did not try. He could hear the complaint about three lengths being entered as two. A man inside the window compared one ordinary piece of paper with another and called somebody from the room behind him to check the bundle count.

The wrong number was corrected only after the second person answered.

Rudra watched the first person leave with the amended paper folded under an arm. The view did not turn supernatural in order to make its small work important.

When their turn came, the man at the window looked first at the chairs, then at the people waiting with them.

"Baith kar bolo," he said, moving a stack away from the lower part of the ledge. He found another stool for Veer when Sitara asked. The opening was low enough that Rudra did not have to stretch his back to be seen.

"Kis baat ki arzi?"

"Sawaal poochhna hai," Rudra said.

"Sawaal kis kaam ka?"

"Saat Lokas ke santulan ka."

The man stopped sorting his stack. He did not bow to an identity Rudra had not given.

"Yahaan uska jawaab mere paas nahi hai."

"Kahan poochhein, woh jaan-na hai."

The man considered the distinction. Then he asked where they had come from. Rudra told him Prithvi, which was his own journey to describe. He did not offer a parent's identity or a guessed relationship between the man at the post and anyone who maintained the realms.

The man reached for a clean sheet and placed it where his own ink-stained hand would not mark the middle.

"Theek sawaal bolkar bataao. Jiske kaam ka hai, us tak bhejne ka tareeqa dekhte hain."

Aadhya asked whether he could read back what he wrote. Ira wanted to hear it, and Rudra's grip was not available to make a neat copy merely because the counter had room.

"Haan," the man said.

---

"Jo Devta saaton ke santulan ka kaam karte hain," Rudra said, "unka kaam samajhne ke liye hum kis se baat kar sakte hain?"

The man wrote slowly enough that the words could be checked as they arrived. He read the whole question back without substituting an answer he thought visitors ought to want.

Ira heard it once. She asked him to keep *samajhne* where it was. They were asking to understand the work, not declaring that they had arrived to take it over.

He left the word.

"Koi khaas ghatna hui hai tumhaare yahaan?"

"Haan," Rudra said. "Kuch humne khud dekha hai. Kuch baat doosre logon se mili hai. Dono ko milaakar apni dekhi hui nahi bolna chaahte."

The man looked up from the page.

Rudra could hear the water behind him and a cough from the queue. He wanted, abruptly, to empty everything onto the ledge: the ruined street, the bank, his father's fear finally given a name. The scale of what he had survived ought to have bought something less patient than another person's need to understand the question.

It did not give him permission to tell a stranger someone else's private account.

He kept the question where he had put it.

"Jiske kaam se yeh juda hai, uske yahaan kaam samajhne waale se baat karwa sakte ho? Pehle utna."

The man said he could try to arrange that. Not a promise to bring a Devta to the counter, and not his own claim to know what maintained every Loka. A short hearing with someone familiar with that work was within what he could ask for.

"Ruko. Waqt poochh leta hoon."

He went through the open doorway behind the counter with the written question in his hand.

---

The accompanying worker asked Rudra about the back while they waited.

It was tiring in the supported position. Not a new sharp pain, not a reason to prove he could outlast the seat. She checked and changed the allowed angle a little, then asked whether he would rather end the inquiry here for today.

He considered the question while she was still beside him.

"Do minute aur dekh lete hain. Phir lautenge."

The two minutes were offered, not smuggled into the plan as the beginning of another hour.

Aadhya watched the doorway. Veer rested on the stool without pretending it was incidental. Ira listened to the room while Sitara stayed beside her: papers, a low argument about a bundle, somebody laughing farther back. Nothing she needed an uncovered second eye to hear.

The man returned before their two minutes ended.

He had arranged a short interval on the morning after next, at this same window, for their question to be heard by a person familiar with the balance-maintainers' work. No stairs, no procession into an unseen building required just to ask it. He could not promise the answer to every part, or an audience with a Devta after that.

He asked for a name to put beside the time.

Rudra gave his own first name after asking whether the others wanted that as the contact for their shared question. They agreed. There was no request for a family tree.

"Parso aa paayein, woh care ke baad hi tay hoga," Rudra said.

"Theek hai. Na aa sake toh baad mein phir waqt poochh lena. Main apna kaam karta rahoonga."

The man did not offer to hold a morning open forever.

---

He made a plain copy of their question and the offered time, read both aloud, then added what they should bring if they came: a short, clear account of the things which had led them to ask. What each had witnessed should be distinguished from what somebody else had told them. No unnecessary private names or papers on the ledge today.

"Suna hua bekaar nahi hota," he said. "Bas jisne dekha, uski jagah tum mat ban jaana."

Rudra looked at the man's ink-stained fingers.

The advice did not make Ram's account less real. It gave that account a place beside their own experiences, without asking either to impersonate the other. His father had named an actual enemy and an actual failing order. Rudra had seen what had happened at the bank. Those statements could begin the inquiry together; they were not the same evidence.

He asked for the copy to be handed to Sitara, who could carry it without making the weak grip hold a page flat against the wind.

Ira asked the man to repeat the preparation note once, for hearing, not to grant her a new job writing through eye pain. He did. Her notebook stayed closed.

The accompanying worker said it was quarter past eleven.

They had five minutes to begin the return with the margin intact.

Rudra thanked the man. The stool was put back, the chairs turned where there was space. He looked once more at the clean copy while Sitara folded it into the dry pocket she had chosen.

A time. A place. Their actual question accepted for a hearing.

It was more than an imagined welcome and less than a repaired universe. He could carry that much honestly.

---

They left the awning at twenty past eleven.

The court had not finished being itself. Cloth was carried past the window. A person beside the bench began arguing about a place in the queue. The wet earth where the bucket had spilled was already losing its shine.

Rudra looked for the worker once. The place where he had set the bucket down was empty. Rudra knew what had happened in that part of the court, not where it would take the man's afternoon.

The woman sorting cloth lifted her chin when they thanked her again from the clear paving. Her stool was back, her space no longer occupied by their chairs. They left her to the work she had kept.

At the opening, Vedant looked along the first stretch before the wheels entered it. Sitara and the accompanying worker remained with the others. There was no movement blocking the return. They went back by the bends through which they had come, keeping the chairs on the checked line.

The back of Rudra's neck cooled when the rock took the daylight away.

He had expected leaving Swarg to feel like being shut out. Instead he could feel the exact place where the chair pressed against his tired body, and wanted the gentler position they could use beside the vehicle.

Wanting to return did not cancel wanting to come again.

---

At the broad pause, Veer asked to stop.

The knee had begun its familiar complaint. He stayed within the position allowed and told the worker what it was like while everyone rested. He did not pretend to be checking a loose shoelace. Rudra was glad of the pause too; he said so, rather than making Veer the owner of the whole group's delay.

Ira stayed in the spare chair, the ordinary right eye resting. The left covering had not moved. When the worker asked whether anything had changed, she said the shade was comfortable and she wanted to keep it that way until Earth.

They let the rest take the time set aside for it.

At the tighter turn the spare chair was braked in the checked broad space while the second attendant helped the first with Rudra's angle. No hand went from an injured friend's lap to a wheel because it would be quicker. Once Rudra's chair was through and held, the second man returned to move Ira's. Sitara stayed beside her until they could continue together.

Rudra heard the little changes in sound which had accompanied them coming in, in the opposite order now: wheel against stone, voice becoming clearer around a turn, the dry rustle of scrub outside.

He could smell warm dust before he saw the road.

The opening widened into the ordinary ridge and the waiting minibus.

The Earth worker came towards them, counted the returning party with her eyes and asked for the present findings. Nine had come back. Behind her, the driver had the ramp ready and the way to the road clear.

Her watch said eleven thirty-eight.

---

The accompanying worker gave the actual return handover before setting her bag down.

Rudra's familiar back ache and joint positions. Veer's knee fatigue and requested pause. Ira's closed ordinary eye, no new symptom, rest still needed. Aadhya's hands protected throughout, no new force or injury reported. The mentors gave their own accounts too; walking had not removed Vedant's altered fingers or made Sitara's covered hand available for lifting.

The Earth worker checked what needed checking rather than treating the words *we're back* as a complete examination.

The chair's allowed position changed while stationary. Rudra drank with help. Ira kept the glare off her right eye. Veer accepted the spare for the Earth-side rest after Ira chose the vehicle seat she could recline in; the two workers supervised the ordinary moves without asking Aadhya to help lift.

The return report went to Dhruv. He acknowledged it after his own care. The receiving ward got the practical update through its chosen contact and confirmed that the returning patients would be received as arranged.

Their first visit had ended. The trip had not.

They ate the food they had brought in the shade available at the vehicle, with the help each task actually required. There was no rush to use the extra minutes by going back inside for one more look.

Rudra asked Sitara whether the paper was still dry. She checked the pocket and said yes. They left it folded while they finished the food.

---

At twelve twenty the driver was ready to leave, within the waiting time he had accepted.

The spare chair was folded and secured again. The attendants controlled the ramp movement, the clinical worker checked Rudra's position and the separate vehicle restraints. All ten other occupants took their belted places. The driver counted the occupied places once more before closing the door.

The driver pulled onto the road.

At the planned stop, one attendant checked the clear access to the washroom before the workers helped with the ordinary needs of the break. Rudra had to ask for help with clothing as well as the chair. The privacy mattered as much as the assistance; nobody made the moment a joke or a lesson for somebody else.

After the rest, position and restraints were checked again before they continued.

The road brought back the repair sheds, the unfinished wall, the shops now fully open. The minibus reached the receiving place at two.

The workers waiting there had actually received the return call. They checked the handovers, heard each person's own answers and accepted care before the travelling staff ended theirs. Rudra's supported transfer went onto the prepared bed with his wrist clear. His phone, clothes and ordinary belongings came with him.

Only then did the attendants take the transport chairs back to be checked.

---

The hiring worker accepted the chairs and the returned equipment after checking them with the attendants. Nothing damaged was hidden under a folded seat.

Sitara settled the agreed balance through the booking contact after her own care had been handed over. The deposit already paid was deducted once. The driver checked the vehicle charge and acknowledged it; the attendants and the two clinical workers received their accepted day fees and confirmed them. The remaining reserve was counted back into the travel account.

The day fees covered the accepted block. They had finished the return well before four; there was no overtime to settle.

The minibus left the receiving place. The attendants took their bags; one answered a call of his own while putting on the sandals he had changed out of for work. The clinical workers confirmed that their written notes had been received with the accepted care, collected their bags and departed too.

Sitara told Rudra the day was settled when his next interval allowed the brief message. She did not book another day on the strength of a time written in Swarg.

In the ward, the people now caring for him had begun their own work. The back and the ankle were examined after the journey, the supports changed where the day had pressed them differently, and the familiar fatigue was recorded. He stayed for the rest he needed. There was no new power test hidden inside asking how the return had gone.

He slept with the comb beside the cupboard.

---

When he woke, the light outside had moved towards the same shabby wall he had been looking at before the journey.

The wall had acquired no grandeur during his absence. A flake of paint lifted near its upper corner. Somewhere below it, a scooter refused to start.

He found he wanted to tell somebody how ordinary that looked after Swarg, then how wrong ordinary was as a way of dismissing it.

First he dictated the arrival message his parents were expecting: back in receiving care, the current checks done, resting before any further conversation. The worker read it to him before sending it to the agreed contact. Rukmini's acknowledgment came later, with no demand for a long call.

Then he heard the separate replies from the friends after their own care.

Veer said the knee had been checked and he was resting. He would decide about another visit after seeing what tomorrow's body said, not after imagining tomorrow's queue.

Aadhya sent a short description of what she wanted remembered: the empty shade, the bucket kicked with a foot, the visitor objecting, the woman making room without being asked to stop her whole day. The falling water belonged in the description too. Leaving it out would give the first man too much of the place.

Ira said she was resting her eye and would not make an entry tonight. The question had been read aloud; she knew what it said. They did not need a newly written page to prove that she had been there.

Rudra answered that the copy was safe. He asked for no further reply.

---

Sitara had handed the folded sheet to the receiving worker at Rudra's request before leaving for her own rest. Now he asked to have it placed where he could read without holding it.

The worker unfolded it on the stand and checked the angle with him.

Their question was still plain. Beside it lay the offered morning interval, at the same window, and the preparation note. The person who would hear them had not been given a face or a promise of omniscience. They had gained an actual hearing to consider, not instructions to be well by then.

Rudra read the note again.

What they had seen. What they had been told.

He knew how to give an account of the bank without pretending the form had cured him. He could describe a ruined place without giving his father an invented childhood in it. He could say what the man at the post had done without awarding him a divine title.

The failing balance was different. He believed the source who had told him. What he did not yet have was the account beneath that account: how Ram had come to know it, what was an observation, what had been learned from someone else, which questions his father himself still carried.

Wanting to ask did not retract what Ram had already given.

Rudra asked for the sheet to be put away and for help sending a short request to his father. Could he hear about the visit and one further question when his own care allowed? Ram replied after his next appointment. He could speak now, briefly, alone.

Rudra's nurse checked his position before the call.

---

He gave the current care first, then the visit.

The pale terraces. The paper window. The question actually received, the possible return after fresh choices and reviews. He had not used the private account about Kaal or a family identity to make the man listen. Ram heard what Rudra himself had done, not a claim to have discovered the laws of the seven Lokas in one court.

His father asked whether he needed to stop. Rudra said he could manage the short remaining interval.

"Ek sawaal reh gaya," he said.

"Bolo."

The sheet was safe in the cupboard. The new morning written on it belonged to somebody else's offered time, not to Rudra's already-promised body. Outside, the scooter finally caught and went away.

He could remember the window man's fingers flattened against a clean page, leaving a space for the exact question instead of its grander replacement. He wanted to take that care back to the source without making a cross-examination of the fact that his father had answered him at all.

The route had brought him to a place where someone might listen. Preparing to be heard meant understanding which part of the truth he could carry himself.

"Papa, 2020 mein aapko kis tarah pata chala tha ki saaton ka santulan bigad raha hai?"
