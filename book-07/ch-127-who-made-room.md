# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-SEVEN — WHO MADE ROOM

---

> *"We had mistaken being needed for having a place.*
> *The distinction first appeared on a day with no summons."*
> *— Ira's later register, notes on the first court*

---

"Us khidki par, woh aadmi," Sitara said. "Baari usne sambhaali. Kiske kaam ka sawaal hai, usne poochha. Sunna hai ya nahi, aaj us aurat ne tay kiya. Saare Swarg mein kaun tay karta hai, woh mujhe nahi maaloom."

Rudra listened.

"Bench ke paas waale aadmi ko humne koi poora adhikaar nahi diya tha," she added. "Usne jagah rok li. Doosron ne jagah banaayi bhi."

The nurse was waiting.

"Theek hai," he said. "Ab rakhta hoon."

Sitara said goodbye and ended the call.

The nurse moved the stand clear. He gave her the back's present ache before she and the worker began the position change. The elbow needed protecting even from an impatient attempt to help; he stopped that attempt, let the support take the arm and waited while the sheet was eased beneath him.

He had wanted the name of whatever sat above the whole court.

What he had received was an account of several people making separate choices. One had kept room to himself. Another had moved a bundle. The receiver had asked somebody else whether she could hear a question. None of those acts had arrived without a person behind it.

For tonight, he left the question at that size.

---

The following morning did not bring a mission.

It brought a breakfast which cooled while the easier position was being found, a review of the elbow and the usual discomfort of needing help before being properly awake. Rudra was less gracious about the last part than he had been on the phone. He apologised to the worker for the tone, not for requiring the help.

Afterwards, he asked for the college page again.

The worker read until he could tell her which paragraph he had meant. He listened to its ordinary unfinished argument with an attention the pale court had not made obsolete. There were things he had wanted to learn before seven Lokas became a number people said around his bed.

He still wanted them.

Over the next days the page gained a second page, then a small correction he dictated badly and asked to have read back. On an afternoon when the back was sharper after care, he did not work at all. The unfinished line remained unfinished without turning into evidence against him.

---

On the second evening, Rukmini could take a short call after her own care in Varanasi.

He gave her the practical state first. No new journey for him; the returning mentors were resting; his own treatment continued. She had received the earlier completion message. He did not tell her the private hearing account merely because she was his mother and the phone was open.

"Khaana khaaya?" she asked.

"Is sawaal ka bhi ek department hona chahiye. Sab wahi poochhte hain."

"Department se jawaab do phir."

He told her what he had managed and where help had been needed. She told him the small ordinary things she had eaten, including the part she had not wanted. Her supported shoulder made an attempt to reach for something off to one side a bad idea; he heard her ask the person nearby to pass it instead.

The line settled into an easy quiet.

In Varanasi, Rukmini watched a little skin form on the tea she had left too long. The cup was near enough. She did not pick it up just to occupy the silence. Her son was breathing on the other end of the call, answering from the place where he was actually being cared for, and there was no further fact she had to prise from him before allowing the evening to pass.

"Thak gaye?" she asked.

"Thoda."

"Kal bhi page wahin hoga."

She knew about the college work because he had just told her. That small knowledge pleased her more than an improved answer to the breakfast question would have done.

They ended when they had agreed.

She asked for her tea to be warmed, then waited while the person helping her finished the task already in hand. Nothing across the distance warned her of another world. The room held a cooling cup, an ordinary favour and her own next rest.

---

By the fourth day, Veer had decided he did not want the whole of Swarg to remain an awkward bench.

He said it during a short call with Aadhya, after both had finished their morning care. She had been describing the shrub behind the cloth, the green shoot making its own narrow place between the stones.

"Poora realm yaad hai tujhe?" he asked.

"Nahi. Wahi thoda sa."

"Mujhe stool ki awaaz yaad hai. Har baar laga ab toota."

"Toota nahi."

"Haan. Meri izzat ko doubt tha. Lakdi ko nahi."

Aadhya laughed, and asked whether the knee was easier today.

He answered that question honestly before saying what he had actually called to say.

---

"Phir dekhna hai," Veer said. "Kisi ko haraane nahi. Woh kapde kahaan jaa rahe the, log fursat mein kya karte hain. Wahi sab."

Aadhya did not answer immediately.

"Mujhe bhi. Par hum logon ko museum banaakar toh nahi dekhenge."

"Main ticket nahi kaat raha."

"Tu sawaal bahut official poochhta hai."

"Kya boloon? Namaste, aapki daily life bataaiye?"

They both laughed at how terrible that would sound.

After the laughter, the possibility remained. A short visit, if it could be made without turning the people in the court into an exhibition or their own bodies into the cost of a good afternoon. They would ask about their actual ability first. Neither called the other one's yes a booking.

---

The intervening days had small setbacks in them.

Veer's knee disliked a longer walk on the fifth morning. He reported it, returned to the earlier interval and kept the following review. The shoulder could manage ordinary unweighted movement better than it could bear anything useful on the road; he still did not carry his own water bag for the pleasure of looking recovered.

Aadhya's hand examination found the same missing confidence in the right thumb and index finger. Looking at a fastening helped her manage it; looking away still made the grip uncertain. She could use the permitted light tasks, then stop. There would be no experiment involving a hot vessel simply because the cup was small.

Their walks lengthened by approved pieces, not by the distance between a bed and a wished-for realm.

When Veer told Rudra about the idea, Rudra asked what they meant to ask at the window.

"Abhi kuchh nahi."

The pause afterwards was awkward enough for Veer to hear Rudra reconsidering the question.

"Bas jaana hai?"

"Haan. Tujhe bura laga?"

"Mujhe jaana hai. Woh alag hai."

Veer let the distinction stand. He did not make his own visit depend on Rudra promising not to miss it. Rudra, in return, did not find a necessary message which would make the two travellers his delegates again.

They talked instead about the old college bag. Veer suggested that it had probably achieved a higher attendance than either of them.

"Usko degree mil jaayegi," Rudra said. "Humein bag uthana padega."

"Mera shoulder dekh. Uski bhi application lagegi."

It was an ordinary complaint about a future neither of them had abandoned.

---

Before the ward's agreed care period ran out, Rudra discussed the proposed next bounded period with Ram and the receiving team. The charges and review dates were checked; the agreed payment arrived and the ward acknowledged it. The travel reserve was not included in that care amount. The old damaged vehicle's unresolved account did not disappear into either one.

He knew what had been paid for here, and what had not been decided.

During the following sitting trial, the back began pulling before the useful road interval could be reached. The therapist ended the attempt. His elbow was less swollen than it had been weeks before, but the hand still could not be trusted to rescue his balance, and the old faint remained unexplained. There was no recommendation for another long outing now.

Rudra lay back in the easier position and gave the returning ache its honest name.

"Theek ho jaayega," Veer began when he heard.

Then he stopped.

"Matlab—main kal phone karoonga. Theek na bhi hua toh."

"Yeh better hai," Rudra said.

Neither had to pretend the first sentence was a crime. The second was simply more useful.

---

Ira answered the proposed visit after her own eye review.

No travel for her. The left covering stayed where it was; ordinary right-eye use remained short and glare-sensitive. She had gained no longer interval by listening to an account of seven Lokas. Rest still had an inconveniently specific meaning.

"Tasveerein mat laana meri taraf se," she told Aadhya. "Tumhe chahiye toh alag. Mere liye dekhne ka kaam mat banao."

"Kya laaoon phir?"

"Waapas aa jaana. Baaki baad mein sun loongi. Agar mann hua."

Aadhya heard the small smile in it.

There was no list to complete, no person whose clothes they had to decode, no symbol Ira needed described at length. The notebook would not accompany them as an absent friend's authority. Ira asked after Aadhya's hand, and they spent the remaining minute arguing about whether Veer's imagined museum introduction could possibly be made worse.

It could.

"Namaste," Ira said, perfectly serious. "Humein aapki rozmarra ki samasyaon par ek project banana hai."

Aadhya laughed so suddenly that she had to tell the worker near her that nothing had hurt.

The visit was still uncertain. The laughter was not.

For the first time since the hearing, wanting another glimpse of the court felt like something they were allowed to want before it became a task they could justify to someone else.

---

Sitara heard the request after her hand care, on a call she had chosen to take.

She wanted to know where they meant to go. The first court, Aadhya said. No farther than the places already reached, and no promise of meeting the woman from the hearing. They could watch the ordinary work if there was room, ask before occupying anybody's shade and leave when their time or someone else's space ran out.

"Aur agar kuchh khaas na dikhe?" Sitara asked.

Veer considered that.

"Toh bhi gaye honge."

It was a better answer than the list of useful things he could have invented.

Sitara would discuss her own current ability before choosing. Vedant gave the same answer separately. Neither mentor treated the earlier successful inspection as a duty to repeat it on the first date two students could manage.

By the tenth morning after the hearing, their own reviews had allowed the familiar short walking work with protected arms, a rest between inspection and visit, and the same right to end before crossing again. Vedant's fingers still gave their altered answers. Sitara's covered hand would carry no stool. Dhruv chose to remain with his leg care; he could accept practical reports at agreed times, with the receiving contact as backup.

They chose the hundred-and-ninety-ninth day as a possibility, three mornings away.

First came the actual trials.

---

Veer walked the reviewed interval with the therapist, turned where he had been told to turn and sat before the knee required an argument.

The effort was ordinary enough to annoy him. He could remember holding a line under an attack; now the important achievement was lowering himself to a chair without disguising the last few paces. The therapist asked what he felt, and he described it without offering the battle as contrary evidence.

After the rest, he stood again under supervision. The shoulder was not used as a lever. He made the short return, then let the current findings decide the next part.

Aadhya's trial was separate.

She could walk without asking the uncertain fingers to do a walking stick's work. She also had to manage the ordinary things which would happen at the end of that walk: bottle, food, fastening, rest. The worker opened what needed opening. Aadhya used the permitted light grip while watching it, asked for help when it became unreliable and left a lifted bag to the person who had agreed to lift it.

The therapist asked whether she understood the limited purpose of the permission.

"Ghoomne ki," she said. "Kaam pe lagne ki nahi."

It was easier to say than it had once been.

---

Their road trial used a separately quoted ninety-minute out-and-back booking, including the stop, with clinical accompaniment. The same receiving team had accepted their return. The suitable vehicle had four occupied separate belt places and positions they could actually use. Its driver accepted this trial's fee and stopping places; nobody obtained an extra day's work by describing it as preparation.

Veer and Aadhya chose to go after the fresh checks.

The road outside Mirzapur was being itself. A motorcycle had stopped beside a puncture shop. Somebody balanced an empty blue crate on a bicycle and shouted at a friend who had apparently suggested the wrong repair. Aadhya watched the crate wobble and felt an absurd relief that no cosmic explanation was likely to improve the scene.

At the agreed stop, the travelling worker asked them separately about the position and discomfort. Veer's knee needed its change; his shoulder had not been made to bear the door. Aadhya's hands remained protected, the bag opened by the person carrying it. They dealt with the washroom and water before getting back in.

On the way home Veer stopped trying to make the trial sound shorter by never looking at the clock.

He knew how long he had been seated. That knowledge was useful, not shameful.

They reached the receiving place inside the accepted trial time. The worker gave the actual findings; the receiving staff checked both travellers and accepted care. The driver acknowledged the full quoted payment through the contact and left. The travelling worker's own fee was paid and acknowledged after handover, and she departed too.

There was no trial car waiting outside overnight to become their realm transport.

---

The following review included the evening afterwards.

Veer had needed the settled knee position and had kept it. Aadhya's altered sensation had not changed; there had been no new strain concealed beneath a successful return. Both had eaten, rested and reported the parts which were tiresome rather than alarming.

The proposed outing stayed conditional on the final morning review: ordinary belted road travel with the planned stops, the short familiar walk with qualified accompaniment, actual seated rests and no lifting. It did not include fighting, catching a falling load or spending an extra hour standing because something interesting had begun.

Veer wanted that last part said plainly to him.

"Main khud bhool sakta hoon," he told the worker. "Aap bol dena."

"Bolenge," she said. "Tum bhi bataana."

The exchange did not make him a child. It made the people coming with him less likely to have to guess.

Afterwards, he called Aadhya for their agreed short update. She had reached her own answer, not received his as a group permission.

Yes, if the morning findings held.

He gave the same answer for himself.

---

On the hundred-and-ninety-eighth day, Sitara went through the new booking with them.

Eight occupied belt places, including the driver. Veer, Aadhya, Vedant, Sitara, one uninjured attendant, two qualified clinical workers and the person driving. Two light folding rest stools and the ordinary bags, secured away from those places. No patient travelling across somebody's knees because an excursion was less important than an inquiry.

One clinical worker would remain on Earth with the driver. The other would accompany the short route, first for the mentors' out-and-back inspection and then, only after return checks and rest, for the separate visit. The two young travellers would wait with qualified care during that inspection. The attendant would carry the stools and water, not rely on a student's shoulder or a mentor's hand.

Veer counted the places himself.

"Main eighth nahi, passenger hoon," he said. "Matlab bag nahi uthaaoonga."

"List mein bag ka belt nahi hai," Aadhya told him. "Tera hai."

He accepted the rebuke with an injured dignity which was not one of his clinical findings.

---

The booking contact gave the actual new quote.

It covered the ordinary return vehicle, waiting, planned stops and the accepted workers' full day fees. The block began at quarter to eight and ended at half past four. A roadside end to the visit would still leave those fees payable. No one was agreeing to earn them only if two passengers got a magnificent afternoon.

The four proposed travellers checked what each could actually contribute after care and the separate road trial. Veer's and Aadhya's authorised amounts were received in the travel account; Vedant's and Sitara's agreed parts arrived too. The total was checked against the new quote and the return reserve before Sitara sent the deposit. The booking contact acknowledged it against this job's balance.

Rudra and Ira were not charged for declining to come.

The old reserve had an owner and a record; it did not become available to every journey because it was sitting near another number. Sitara reconciled the amounts openly, and left the unrelated damaged-ambulance account where it still belonged, unresolved.

Then the people being hired accepted their actual work.

The driver knew the Earth approach, the waiting place, the latest departure and the planned road pause in each direction. He was not accepting a guide's job beyond the ridge. Both clinical workers were told where phone contact had failed on the previous visits, who would remain connected and the limits of the checked line. The attendant accepted the unfamiliar short crossing and the gear he would carry. They could ask questions before saying yes; each did say yes to the finite job described.

Sitara sent the practical arrangement to the receiving contact and to Dhruv. Both acknowledged it. The receiving team accepted the planned return reviews and care. Dhruv gave the report intervals he could manage around his own treatment; the ward contact would receive them if his phone was unavailable.

No one at the window had been asked to hold a place for this outing.

---

The timetable left room for discovering that they should not go in.

After the Earth arrival, the two mentors would make the fresh inward and outward check with the attendant and accompanying worker. Their findings would come back to the people waiting beside the vehicle. Rest belonged between that work and any second crossing; it would not be spent persuading someone to ignore a changed knee or a different pain.

The intended crossing was quarter to eleven, with all six back on Earth by quarter past twelve. The first court only. They would begin the return before the margin became the last possible minute. Road departure no later than one, within the driver's accepted wait and the receiving plan.

Veer repeated the important parts to Aadhya in the wrong order, then corrected himself.

"Pehle report. Phir rest. Phir poochhenge kaun jaana chahta hai."

She nodded. She had heard the same plan and could give her own answer when that time came.

There would be water and their own food. No need to turn a stranger's offer into a meal the clinical plan had not considered. No expectation of buying anything, being shown a private room or meeting the person who had answered the balance question. A visit could succeed without gaining access to someone else's work.

---

After the practical call, Veer asked for the small bag to be opened.

His ordinary shirt had been folded with an attention he would not have given it at home. He had meant to ask whether it looked respectable enough for the court, then heard the question in his head and lost interest in its first version.

It needed to be comfortable. The fastening needed to be manageable without provoking the shoulder. It did not need to impersonate a pale embroidered wrap.

The worker helped him settle what would be worn, what would stay in the bag and what she would fasten in the morning.

Veer looked at the sleeves.

For a second he imagined the man at the bench finding something inadequate in them. Then he pictured the woman who had shifted her bundle without inspecting anyone's clothes first.

Neither image told him what the next day would contain.

He asked for the shirt to be put where it could be reached easily, and left the imagined inspection unfinished.

---

Aadhya chose the blue scarf again.

It had not become lucky. Its edge had softened the glare once, and she liked the colour. Those were sufficient reasons to ask for it, instead of searching the bag for something that might look as though it belonged beneath a different sky.

The worker helped with the fastening she could not trust out of sight. Aadhya checked the little case of ordinary care things, then left the bag open for the person who would carry it tomorrow. Nothing needed to be packed secretly under the clothes. There was no page she had to slip onto a stranger's ledge.

She was looking forward to the water.

That was the part which made her feel foolish, briefly. After everything that water had stood beside, wanting to hear it again seemed a small reason for so much planning.

Then she remembered how often she had wanted to see the Ganga from the college side of an unfinished afternoon, without requiring the river to solve anything before she could be glad it was there.

She let the wish remain ordinary.

---

Rudra sent a short message after his own care, asking if the booking had been confirmed. Aadhya answered with the practical plan, not a request that he pretend to be delighted.

He heard it, then asked for one thing.

"Apni taraf se dekhna. Meri jagah bharne ke liye mat jaana."

She could do that. She said so and did not attach a promise to remember every detail perfectly.

He wanted to know whether Veer had selected clothes for diplomacy.

"Belt waali diplomacy," she said. "Bag uska representative hai."

The joke went back to Veer later with Aadhya's permission, along with the affectionate complaint that it was growing through retelling. Rudra had asked for no further update that night. He was keeping the next position change and the small college-work interval he had chosen, then resting.

Ira's reply to the departure message came separately. She would be asleep or in care at pickup time; no need to wake her to say that a car had arrived. If the friends returned and wanted to talk later, they could ask then.

Aadhya put the phone down.

Nobody was waiting with a blank page which she had to fill to make the visit worthwhile.

---

Veer's last walking interval that evening ended beside the chair prepared for him.

He had felt the familiar knee pull and said it before anyone asked. The worker settled the support; the shoulder stayed out of the movement. They checked what needed checking, and the planned morning review remained exactly where it had been, capable of changing the day.

He was pleased by how little drama there was in it.

Earlier, he might have hidden that small discomfort until a departure could no longer be stopped without inconveniencing everyone. Then the inconvenience would have become one more reason to keep going. He could see the old argument now, not as bravery but as a trap he knew how to build with very ordinary materials.

The driver had accepted an ending at the road. His friends had accepted a different day's care. He did not need to make a knee ache into a secret on behalf of people who had not asked for one.

The worker asked where he wanted the bag left.

"Jahan subah aapko aasaan ho," he said.

She gave him two actual choices, and he chose the one which would not block the floor beside the chair.

It was possible to become so pleased with being considerate that he forgot to answer a simple question. He grinned at himself after she left.

---

Aadhya could manage a final short call before her evening rest. Veer asked first, heard yes and kept the phone where the shoulder did not have to hold it.

They discussed food for a minute with the seriousness of people who had once crossed cities while forgetting an ordinary meal. Everything necessary was already arranged. That did not stop Veer asking whether the packed portion described as sufficient had been measured by somebody his size.

"Tujhe court nahi, kitchen dekhna hai," Aadhya said.

"Private area mein nahi jaaoonga. Bahar se respectful interest."

She laughed softly.

Then they fell quiet without either having used up the offered time.

---

The care worker had drawn the curtain beside Veer's bed. Beyond it someone was trying to fold a newspaper one-handed and producing a noise much larger than the task. The familiar room would still contain that kind of work when he returned.

Tomorrow's court would have its own unfinished errands. Cloth being counted, a bundle put down somewhere inconvenient, people wanting the window before the day grew hotter. He knew a few of those things from the visit he had actually made; the rest of its life had continued without him for two weeks.

They had not been called back. No stranger owed them a welcome, and no enemy stood waiting to make their presence necessary. Perhaps there would be nothing to do but ask for a little room, sit in it while it was freely offered and give it back.

Veer found that prospect harder to picture than another assignment.

Aadhya was still on the line, within the short time she had given. He asked the question without dressing it up as a problem about transport or the people of another realm.

"Hum wahan bina kisi kaam ke jaa sakte hain?"
