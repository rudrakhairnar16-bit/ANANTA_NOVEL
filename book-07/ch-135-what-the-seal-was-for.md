# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-FIVE — WHAT THE SEAL WAS FOR

---

> *"Protection was the reason we were given.*
> *Being included was the question that remained."*
> *— Ira's later register, no receipt for consent*

---

"Dono," Rukmini said. "Tumhaare Papa ki taraf se jo pehchaan tummein hai, aur meri taraf se jo hai. Kavach dono ko chhupa raha tha."

Rudra looked at the supported phone.

"Sirf unka nahi," he said.

"Sirf unka nahi."

She had placed it at his birth to conceal the identity which could make him a target. There were beings who would hunt what they recognised in him; her purpose had been to keep her child from being found through that identity.

She was speaking about the kavach she had placed, not describing a second seal beneath it or something which had survived its breaking.

"Main tumhe zinda rakhna chahti thi," she said. "Chhupaana uske liye tha."

The answer did not arrive with a picture from the day he was born. No voice he had forgotten, no recovered glimpse from inside an infant's body. He was nineteen, in a supported position in Mirzapur, hearing his mother explain an act he could not remember.

He had asked what the seal had been for.

She had answered.

---

He took time before speaking again.

Pataal had not become less real because his mother's birthplace now stood beside it. Nor had one inheritance cancelled the other into something new which the phone could teach him to use. He knew more about what had been concealed; he did not know a new way to fight.

"Aapne pehle bola tha," he said, "ki dikhne se jaan ko khatra tha. Tab mujhe yeh nahi pata tha ki aapki taraf ka bhi chhup raha hai."

"Ab bataa rahi hoon."

"Haan."

The words were not enough to make every year of not knowing easy. They were enough to make the answer clear.

He did not ask her to repeat an old method or try anything on the injured body she could not see during this audio call. Nothing had changed beneath the dressing because the purpose had been named.

The broken kavach was still broken.

---

For an instant, his mind moved towards the sister and her ally.

He caught the movement before it became an accusation spoken as fact. His mother had told him who had stood together in the history of her exile. That was not an answer to every danger which had ever reached her child.

He could ask more later without deciding the missing names for her now.

"Main sab ek hi kahaani banaane lagta hoon," he said.

"Ek saath bahut kuch sun rahe ho."

She did not tell him that meant his guesses were true.

The people who had falsely accused her, the beings she had wanted to hide him from, the enemies he had actually faced: he could remember each account without forcing all three into the same doorway merely because the doorway would be easier to guard.

He had no guard to post. There was a nurse beside the bed, a supported phone and his mother's tired voice at the other end.

He answered the present comfort question before either of them continued.

---

"Main bachha tha," he said.

"Haan."

He could hear the care she took not to answer a different question.

"Main kuch samajhkar haan nahi bol sakta tha."

"Nahi," she said. "Tumse aisi haan mili thi, yeh nahi keh rahi."

He closed his eyes for a moment.

He had not been demanding that an infant be made responsible for its own protection. He needed the reason not to turn backwards into an agreement he had somehow given, or into a debt which could be collected from the person he was now.

His mother let the distinction remain.

It did not erase that she had acted to keep him alive. It did not decide that every later act of control had therefore been necessary.

---

The eight-minute call reached its limit.

Rukmini said she needed to stop. Rudra agreed, said goodbye and let the connection end with a real answer already spoken. The phone was put away after the finite help; the next care began without another question keeping her on the line.

He gave the nurse his actual symptoms and accepted the protected change of position. The back, wrist and elbow needed the same attention as before. The ankle and knee were settled with help. Knowledge had not made the weak grip trustworthy, and the old faint had not acquired a family explanation.

In Varanasi, Rukmini took her own care and rest after the ended call.

Rudra lay quiet for a while.

Both, he thought.

It was a word about his parents, not an instruction to become twice as useful to them.

He let the evening's ordinary needs come back: water, supper, the next rest. There would be time to ask what protection should mean between people who could now speak to one another.

The next morning, a fold in the sheet took more attention than the largest answer of the previous night.

It pressed where the supported ankle could not simply move away from it. Rudra tried to ignore the small discomfort through the first part of the check, then told the nurse exactly where it was. She stopped to attend to it, protected the position while the fold was freed and asked again before continuing.

The difference was immediate and ordinary.

He was embarrassed by the strength of his relief.

"Itni si baat thi," he said.

"Bataa diya toh theek hua."

She did not make the answer into a lesson about his character. There was still the wrist to check, the back to settle and the actual night's report to hear. He gave the account without turning the mother's explanation into a reason that every sensation now meant something new.

The fold was gone. The ankle was still injured.

He could be grateful for one without pretending the other had disappeared.

---

After breakfast, he rested with the stand out of his way.

He remembered being told that the seal was a lock. Not the making of it, not an infant's impossible recollection of a mother's fear, but the later conversation in which she had admitted the act and spoken of the danger of being visible.

He had carried that answer through other answers since then.

The loss of the concealment. His father's origin. The mother's birthplace and the name she had been born with. Each had changed what he understood, without moving the old conversation to a different house or making him a witness to his own birth.

Now he knew the kavach had concealed both parents' inheritances.

He did not need to go looking for an old object before the statement could mean what it meant. The person who had placed the seal had answered him herself.

There was a great deal he still did not know.

He could feel the temptation to make that fact into a refusal of the thing he did know: if she had not told everything, perhaps he ought to reject the whole answer until it came complete. But he had asked a particular question. The answer to it was not less true because it did not contain every other one.

Nor was he obliged to stop asking.

---

What troubled him now was not whether his mother had wanted him alive.

He believed that.

He had believed her love through years in which love had not been enough to make the house gentle. The difficulty was not discovering a good intention inside somebody he had thought had none. It was understanding why a good intention had so often been treated as the end of the discussion.

Danger could make a decision urgent. It could not explain every morning afterwards, every time the child became old enough to understand another part and the explanation stayed out of reach.

He did not remember being one. He remembered being older.

Those memories were his. His mother's exile did not make them petty. The birth-kavach did not turn every later fear into an instruction which he had failed to obey well enough.

He wanted to say that without making her regret giving him the answer.

The wish was understandable. It was also a trap: if the cost of getting one truth was to make no difficult use of it, he would still be standing outside the same door, only better informed about its wood.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini spent part of the morning with her eyes closed while the room carried on around her.

The phone was away. The clean clothes stayed in the order she had asked for yesterday. Dadi took her own time to sit, then left when the nurse came for the next part of care. Neither needed a report of the private call to know that the shoulder still wanted attention.

Rukmini gave the present answers and asked for the support to be checked.

She had told her son why she had placed the seal. The desire which had made her do it remained real. So did the years which had followed an act he had been too young to remember.

He had not promised to be made peaceful by the explanation.

She lay still through the adjustment, waited until it was comfortable and let the next rest come without trying to reach him ahead of his questions.

---

After lunch and actual rest, Rudra asked for another private conversation.

He said he wanted to speak about the years after the kavach had been placed, not ask for a demonstration of it. Rukmini chose a ten-minute afternoon interval following her own care and rest, if both present checks allowed. He accepted.

The ordinary phone help was agreed for that interval. Nobody was asked to retrieve an old object, find a birth witness or put Ram on the line to settle her account for her.

While they waited, Rudra asked for the water he needed and rested again.

He did not rehearse a way to make his question painless. He tried instead to make it say only what he meant.

He could believe that she had protected him then and still want to understand how protection had been used afterwards.

The afternoon connection began after both of them had rested and answered the current checks.

Ten minutes, supported phones, the next care kept outside the chosen interval. Rukmini was willing to listen. Rudra did not need a better version of his body to ask a difficult question with it.

"Ma, darr ka naam sunte hi lagta hai ab mujhe sab samajh lena chahiye," he said. "Jaise uske baad jo hua, uspe sawaal karna chhota ho."

"Kavach ke baare mein bol rahe ho?"

"Uske baad ka. Ghar ka. Training ka bhi."

He waited while she heard the whole of what he had said.

"Tumhe chot pahunchi thi," she answered.

"Haan."

The reply was almost a breath, but not a measure he could improve or a thing he needed to hold longer to make it count.

"Tumhe khone ka darr tha," she said. "Usse har galat baat sahi nahi ban jaati."

He looked at the curtain and let the sentence finish before he decided what it had given him.

---

It was not the same as getting back the mornings.

He could hear his mother's protective purpose without being asked to make a good childhood out of what had hurt. The person who placed the seal had wanted him alive. The woman in the house had still had choices about how a child was treated, what he was told and how long later could be made to last.

He did not have to accuse her of never loving him in order to say that.

"Aapke saath jo hua, usse mere saath jo hua theek nahi ho jaata," he said.

"Nahi," she answered quietly. "Mera dard tumhaare saath galat hone ki chhoot nahi hai."

He went still.

He had been afraid the words would sound like a demand that she defend her love. He was speaking about the harm, not asking whether she had ever meant to keep him safe. She had heard the difference without making him offer a kinder version first.

"Wahi bolna tha," he said.

---

"Main tumhaare Papa ki taraf se sab jawaab nahi de sakti," she continued. "Apni taraf ka bhi unse nahi dilwaaoongi."

He had not asked her to confess his father's thoughts. She had not offered Ram as the only person responsible for everything in a house they had both made.

His parents remained two people. Their fears could be related without becoming identical. Their decisions could meet without either person disappearing into the other.

He could ask his father another day, with his father's own willingness and present care. This call did not bring Ram silently to the edge of the phone or give him the private maternal account by implication.

For now, his mother was here.

"Mujhe abhi theek nahi lag raha sab," he said.

"Abhi theek kehne ko nahi bol rahi."

"Aapne bataaya. Uske liye bhi—"

"Sun liya tumne. Badle mein maaf bolna zaroori nahi."

The relief was not happiness. It was the absence of a demand he had been preparing to meet before he knew whether the words would be true.

---

He let himself be quiet without offering a verdict.

There were things an explanation could do. It could give an act its intended purpose, let him understand why an infant had been hidden, stop him blaming himself for a danger which had not begun with his choices. There were things it could not do. It could not make every later silence inevitable, turn a child's endurance into agreement or ask the grown son to thank the pain for keeping him alive.

His mother had not asked those things of him now.

He needed time to discover what that changed.

"Aaj itna hi," he said when the reminder came.

"Theek."

They said goodbye. The call ended within the accepted ten minutes; the phones were put away and the brief help finished. Both took their next care and actual rest. Rudra reported the familiar discomfort as the position was changed, accepted the protected movement and did not make a difficult conversation another reason to keep the body waiting.

Rukmini rested in Varanasi with her own support checked.

No promise of forgiveness had been made at either end.

---

Afterwards, Rudra listened to the room without deciding what the conversation must mean for every day still ahead.

One answer was not a new childhood.

He did not want to turn this afternoon into a promise his mother had not made, or use a sentence she had spoken carefully as proof that the next difficult question would be easy. He could value what she had said without asking it to repair the whole house at once.

There would be days when he was angrier than he was now. Being heard this afternoon did not make those future feelings a betrayal. There might be ordinary mornings too, when he could speak to her about a page or a meal without finishing the larger argument first.

He could leave room for both. It was a beginning small enough to belong to the people they actually were, rather than the people a good ending would require them to become before supper.

For the rest of the afternoon, he did not ask for another call. He ate when supper came, rested after the help and let the answer remain unfinished work between people, not a test one of them had passed and the other was now obliged to grade.

Day two hundred and nine began with a question about the next supported change of position.

Rudra wanted to know what would be moved first so he would not prepare for the wrong movement and pull against the help. The nurse explained the immediate step, waited for his present answer and continued with the supports protected.

He reported the feeling as it happened. They paused, adjusted and went on when the position was comfortable enough.

It was not independence. Somebody else still needed to do work he could not safely do alone. It was not a victory over the back, wrist or elbow, and the ankle and knee still had their own inconvenient requirements afterwards.

But he had not been merely the object being moved.

His question had not insulted the person helping him. The answer had made the help easier to receive.

When it was finished, he thanked her and rested instead of trying the movement again as though understanding it had made it his to practise unsupervised.

---

Ira's morning had a voice in it instead of a page she needed to look at.

An attendant offered to read the first short paragraph of her ordinary course material while she rested the eye. Ira accepted that paragraph only. The woman had a little time, not the rest of the morning, and the offer did not need to grow into a new duty.

The left covering remained in place. Ira closed the ordinary right eye and listened.

Halfway through, she disagreed with the sentence being read. She let the woman finish before asking for the last little part once more, within the same short help. It was as unconvincing the second time.

"Bas," she said, when the offered paragraph was done. "Aage baad mein."

The woman put the material aside and returned to her other work. Ira did not keep her to explain why the sentence irritated her, nor did she turn the small offer into a debt which must be repaid by finishing a whole task.

For a while she considered the argument with both eyes resting.

A line in a course page could be wrong without being a hidden message. She could dislike it without needing to discover a suppressed life underneath. The plain annoyance was hers, and she enjoyed having enough of herself free to spend on it.

She asked for quiet when the room grew noisy. The next care and rest remained part of her actual day.

---

Veer let a radio question finish before answering that morning.

For a little while he was alone with the programme and his own supported shoulder, so nobody heard the achievement as an achievement. The answer was still wrong.

He considered objecting to the radio.

Then the programme moved on, as it had every right to do, and he let himself be entertained without needing to win. When the worker came for his current knee care, he gave his real answers before the next question could claim his attention. Afterwards he rested the ribs and shoulder as his own day required.

There would be no report to the others unless he chose to tell them one.

Aadhya, in her own room, chose not to add a social call to the morning after hand care. Her right thumb and index finger had not become more certain because the previous call with Rudra had ended kindly. The plain repaired scarf remained in the bag. It did not need to be brought out again to demonstrate that she had found meaning in it.

They were all having mornings which Rudra did not supervise.

---

His own had a short reading interval after breakfast and rest.

He reached the next paragraph and discovered that it answered part of his objection. Not all of it. Enough to make him feel unfair to the writer, which was a useful irritation to have before he became too pleased with his earlier certainty.

He asked for the plain slip to be moved when the page was put away at the limit. It was. The task could wait without the unfinished part being recited to somebody on the phone.

After lunch, he slept for a while.

The body did not care that he was approaching a question which mattered to him. It needed the ordinary next things in their ordinary order, and he was less inclined to treat that fact as opposition.

---

His new request to Varanasi came after he woke and took the care due then.

Rukmini chose eight minutes later that afternoon, following her own rest and present check. Rudra accepted after his own review. The finite help needed for the phones was agreed; no one was asked to remain beyond the setup or become an audience for what the mother and son might say.

He had not learned how to make every future danger wait until everybody was ready.

That was not the thing he wanted to claim.

He wanted to know how being protected could include him now, rather than being something he understood only years after it had been done. An infant could not have joined the old decision. The nineteen-year-old listening to its reason could speak.

He let the last of the waiting time stay quiet.

The question was not an accusation against the existence of help. He had needed help all morning. He wanted a place inside it.

The call connected with both of them rested, checked and willing for the eight minutes.

Rukmini's shoulder was supported. Rudra had given his present answers before the helper stepped back; the wrist could remain where it was safe. At neither end did the phone need to be held up by the person who wanted to speak.

"Writer ko thoda galat samjha tha," he said.

His mother was quiet for a moment, then laughed softly.

"Aage padh liya?"

"Thoda. Poora sahi bhi nahi tha woh."

"Tum bhi nahi the."

"Haan. Dono ko jagah de raha hoon."

The word both was ordinary again. It could belong to an argument on a course page, not only to the identity which had been hidden in him.

He was glad of the smaller use.

---

She asked whether the reading had hurt the back.

He told her about the checked angle, the short interval, the plain slip moved when it ended. Enough of his own morning to answer, not a recitation of everyone's care or an invitation for her to decide what the next review ought to permit.

She said it was good he had stopped at the limit.

He did not hear the sentence as a command to become a better patient for her sake. She was allowed to be glad he had not added avoidable pain to a day which already held enough. He was allowed to want her gladness without making it the only reason to be honest about the body.

"Aapne rest kiya tha?" he asked.

"Kiya. Isliye ab baat kar rahi hoon."

He liked the answer's plain order.

She had not spent the waiting hours staying ready for whatever he might need to ask. She had cared for herself, and now they had this time.

---

He told her that he believed the purpose she had given him two evenings earlier.

He wanted to say that plainly. Yesterday's harder conversation had not made her answer into an excuse invented after the act, and accepting the answer had not made yesterday's questions disloyal. He did not want either of them to hear one statement as the retraction of the other.

"Tumne suna tha," she said.

"Ab uska matlab bhi thoda samajh raha hoon."

She let him take the time. He was not arriving with a verdict on her whole life, and she did not ask him to bring one before the call could continue.

"Aapne mujhe zinda rakhne ke liye kiya tha," he said. "Yeh samajh aaya."

"Haan."

He did not add that he therefore understood everything.

The gap after his sentence was not ungrateful. It was the distance between a reason and the rest of a life.

---

Rukmini waited while he found the next words.

He did not want another answer about how the seal had been made. He did not want somebody to fetch an object or tell him the event as though he had been old enough to witness it. The purpose had been given in his mother's own voice, and he could receive that without pretending to be the child who had chosen the act.

He also did not want to be told he was now too old to need protection.

He needed it in very ordinary ways. A supported phone. An honest explanation before a movement. Somebody capable nearby when the hand could not be trusted to do what he wanted. There would be dangers he could not meet alone even after these injuries had had their actual time and care.

Wanting to be included was not the same as wanting nobody to help.

He wished he had understood that difference sooner. He could start with it now.

---

"Jo poochhna hai bolo," Rukmini said.

The invitation did not make the next question easy. It made him less inclined to hide it inside a question about something else.

He could ask about a title, a date, a part of her history she had not yet told. Those questions would remain. They did not need to stand in for the thing happening between the two people on this particular call.

His mother's wish to see her birthplace was still hers. The sister's false framing was still wrong. The named past alliance did not become a present map, and the broken kavach did not return because they now understood more of its purpose.

Nothing required him to finish the whole history before he could ask where he stood in his own life.

---

The nurse's quiet check interrupted them for a moment.

Rudra answered. Rukmini gave her own present answer at the other end, took the small pause she needed and returned willing to continue. There was time left in the accepted interval; he did not need to hold her past its end to get the words out.

He was not asking her to stop being his mother.

He was asking her to hear the person her child had become, without pretending that being heard would settle every old hurt. The next step could be difficult and still be a step they actually chose, rather than a good ending somebody declared for them.

He looked at his resting hand, then back towards the phone.

"Ma, ab meri hifaazat ka koi faisla hoga, toh usmein meri marzi kahaan hogi?"
