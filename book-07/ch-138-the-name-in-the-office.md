# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-EIGHT — THE NAME IN THE OFFICE

---

> *"The office named the person in power.*
> *It did not name every person who had paid for that power."*
> *— Ira's later register, the limits of a public answer*

---

"Daksh," the receiver said.

Sitara heard the name over the small scrape of a bundle being shifted behind her.

There was no ceremony around it. The man had been asked who ruled Swarg, and had answered from the place where he was working. He did not lower his voice or look behind the teachers for a person whose family might explain their interest.

"Yeh abhi ki jaankaari hai?" Vedant asked.

"Haan. Is daftar ki vartamaan jaankaari mein Swarg ke shaasak Daksh hain."

The receiver tapped the ledge once with an inked finger, waiting to see whether their short question had another part.

Vedant did not ask him to turn the answer into a history of the reign. They had not come for a private interview with the ruler, and had not seen him. What they had now was a current answer given by this public office, here, this morning.

It answered the question they had brought.

It was less than seeing all of Swarg for themselves.

---

Sitara asked whether they could pass on the public answer orally, saying where and when it had been given.

"Sarvajanik baat hai," the receiver said. "Haan."

He did not offer a private record or ask for a patient's name in exchange. The question had been received without either. There was no reason to supply one now, as though plain information became a more valuable gift if somebody paid for it with a family.

"Dhanyavaad," she said.

The man nodded towards the next person waiting.

That was the end of the inquiry.

Behind them, the owner of the folded sheet was trying to persuade its crease to lie in a different direction. A woman carrying cloth asked for a little room. The court resumed around the teachers without needing their permission to become ordinary again.

Sitara let the attendant know that they were finished. He kept the chair clear of the moving bundle while the care worker heard her present answer, then Vedant's.

The name had not changed either answer about the protected limbs.

---

They remained seated for the small pause after the exchange.

The care worker counted the actual walking already used, including the brief morning transfers and the movement to the ledge. Twenty-seven minutes each, not only the twenty-four spent in the three passage crossings. The remaining road and receiving transfers would need room too.

They had enough for the inspected return if they did that work now.

Not enough for an excursion behind the answer.

Vedant looked at the stool beneath him. Its uneven seat had made one small part of the morning possible. He waited for the uninjured attendant to take it back after helping him rise; the forearm did not acquire a lifting task because he wanted to leave the place tidy.

The receiver saw the stool returned to its place and acknowledged it.

Nothing from this office was leaving with them except a spoken answer they had permission to carry.

---

At four minutes to eleven, they left the ledge.

The one-minute movement brought them to the familiar bench. Both teachers sat again while the empty chair was prepared for the passage. Sitara's hand stayed clear of its folds and catches. The care worker checked their actual comfort, and they took the five seated minutes rather than treating the approaching return as a reason to shorten rest.

A boy farther across the court was holding a ball against his hip while someone finished speaking to him.

Sitara did not make him the boy from another visit. She did not know him. He was a person waiting for his own conversation to end, not a sign sent to confirm that their answer belonged inside a larger pattern.

When he ran away, the ball moved with him.

The court kept its people.

They kept their return.

---

At two minutes past eleven, the four entered the inspected line together.

The attendant managed the folded chair. The care worker stayed available to both teachers. Vedant turned without pressing the forearm against the rock; Sitara did not offer the covered hand at the uneven place.

The passage was no more obliged to become easy because they had obtained what they wanted.

They used the line they had checked from both sides. At the bend, the voices stayed within the reach actually tested. No screen was made into a promised connection through the stone.

After eight minutes, Earth light widened around the opening.

All four came out.

Sitara saw the car before she saw the driver's face. It was where his accepted waiting placed it, with ordinary dust on its side and the road continuing past. A vehicle, not a rescue which had been required to find a different realm on the strength of a missed call.

It was ten past eleven.

The passage return was done. With the short bench movement included, each walking account stood at thirty-six minutes, leaving four for the remaining small transfers. The care worker heard their direct answers before anybody moved towards the car.

The name they had come for was still only a name in a report.

The people who had carried it were actually back.

The minute back to the car was counted too.

Then the teachers sat. The care worker checked the present sensation, the fatigue and the supports, heard each answer separately and let the rest take its time. A practical return message connected from the tested Earth-side reach. The receiving contact acknowledged all four back from the passage; the phone was put away.

They had returned before the driver needed the missed-return action.

Sitara drank, then ate the food she had brought with the help she needed. Vedant did the same. Neither spent the interval rehearsing a speech for a student who had not asked to hear it from a moving car.

The driver took his own meal and break. He was not sent through the rock while the travellers rested, or given a smaller errand because the question had ended earlier than the latest road departure.

At a quarter to twelve, the road journey began.

---

The two uninjured workers had secured the folded chair and bags. The teachers were in the same separate belted places, their present seated comfort checked before the car moved. Five people remained five; no person from the court had become an unannounced passenger.

The return road had more traffic.

At a crossing, the car waited behind a bicycle carrying a long, awkward parcel. The man riding it kept one foot down until he had room to turn. Dust hung briefly in the warmth beyond the glass.

Vedant closed his eyes without sleeping for a little while.

A brief answer could have a long day around it. He had known that when he chose the work, but knowing did not make his forearm less tired or the car seat into his own resting place. He told the care worker what needed checking instead of saving a better account for arrival.

The support was attended to. He rested through the next stretch.

---

At ten past one, they reached the Mirzapur receiving place.

The accepted ward help was there. The travelling care worker gave the actual account of the day: walking used, rests taken, present symptoms and the protected limbs which had not been put to loading. Both teachers answered the receiving questions for themselves.

The short movements from the vehicle to their supports took two further walking minutes each. Thirty-nine across the outing, inside the forty allowed, with no bout exceeding either person's smaller current limit.

The unused minute was left unused.

At twenty past one, receiving care accepted the handover. The travelling worker's clinical responsibility for this job ended there, not at the vehicle door. He stayed for the agreed equipment and payment closure, not as an extra ward worker quietly booked until somebody remembered to release him.

Both teachers received their next care. Their own supports, not the hired chair, took the resting weight.

---

The equipment attendant and the travelling worker unloaded the ordinary bags and chair without either injured person taking a share.

The belongings and remaining supplies went to their proper places. The chair's hiring worker arrived for the arranged return check. He and the attendant opened it, tried the brakes, checked the seat, folds, foot supports and the fitting whose angle had been corrected before departure.

It had stayed secure.

The hiring worker accepted the returned chair after the check. There was no new damage to leave for another conversation. The temporary vehicle supports and all hired pieces were accounted for; nothing was being kept overnight beneath the excuse that the trip had been helpful.

Sitara watched the attendant fold the accepted chair one last time.

She had used it. She did not have to turn that use into ownership, or turn the worker who handled it into a permanent part of her care.

---

The payment was completed before anyone left.

The vehicle balance was fourteen hundred rupees. The booking contact and driver acknowledged it against the thousand already received: twenty-four hundred in all, the deposit deducted once. The vehicle bill was paid, not partly paid with an old reserve expected to appear later.

The equipment attendant received nine hundred. He counted and acknowledged the full fee.

The qualified travelling care worker received his nine hundred and acknowledged it too.

The chair's three hundred was paid to its hiring worker, who confirmed the charge settled along with the equipment return.

Thirty-five hundred at this handover, after the earlier thousand. Four thousand five hundred for the accepted job.

No further cost had been agreed or incurred from the contingency.

---

The unused five hundred was divided back at ten minutes to two.

Two hundred and fifty returned to Vedant; two hundred and fifty to Sitara. Each acknowledged the return. Their fresh five-thousand fund stood closed at zero, with the separate contributions and payments reconciled. It had not acquired the damaged ambulance's unresolved final liability, property or repair account, and it owed nothing to the closed crews of earlier journeys.

The chair's worker left with the accepted equipment.

Then the driver and car departed, their payment and work finished.

At two, the equipment attendant and the travelling care worker said goodbye.

"Kaam poora hua," Sitara said when the attendant thanked them for keeping the agreed stop time.

The words were not a refusal of gratitude. They were the shape which allowed it to end without becoming another unpaid request.

Then both workers left as well. All the people hired for this trip were gone, inside the block they had accepted. Ordinary ward care continued under its own arrangement.

She took the rest already due.

The report could wait until the people who had carried it were ready to speak.

Late that afternoon, after their care and sleep, each teacher chose to give the public report.

They offered a six-minute audio conversation. Rudra accepted after his own rest and current check. The ward workers separately agreed the small setup tasks: supported phones, a usable angle, nothing requiring an injured hand to keep the connection alive. The three voices could meet without either teacher walking to the other's room.

The hired trip workers were no part of it.

When the connection began, Sitara said she was comfortable enough for the chosen interval. Vedant gave his own answer. Rudra did the same; the familiar back discomfort had been attended to before the phone was placed.

He did not ask them to begin with the important part as though everything preceding an answer had been somebody else's wasted time.

"Lautkar rest ho gaya?" he asked.

"Haan," Vedant said. "Ab chhoti baat kar sakte hain."

---

They told him what they had actually done.

A fresh inspection in both directions, the later outward crossing, the public queue and the same receiver. The teachers had asked the current ruler question, without a patient's identity or family story. The receiver had answered that, in the office's present information, Swarg's ruler was Daksh.

Rudra looked at the phone.

He had heard the name before. That was not the new part.

For the first time, he was receiving it through people who had asked a present public question and come back to tell him where the reply had been given. His mother's account of an ally in the past had not been silently stretched to cover every day since.

"Aapne unhe dekha nahi," he said.

"Nahi," Sitara answered. "Daftar ka jawaab suna. Aaj subah ka."

She confirmed the receiver had allowed that public answer to be carried orally with its place and time. No private record had been obtained. The ordinary watch placed the inquiry at ten forty-eight; it did not turn their morning at one window into knowledge of every part of the realm.

---

Rudra thanked them.

Then he asked the permission his mother had told him to ask.

"Yahi sarvajanik jawaab, aap dono se suna hai aur kahaan suna hai yeh bataakar, apni ma ko keh sakta hoon?"

"Meri taraf se haan," Vedant said. "Isi seema mein."

Sitara gave her own yes. Their spoken report could reach his mother with its source intact. They were not choosing a general audience or giving him a transcript to circulate.

He repeated the limit back in his own words.

He did not tell either teacher why the name mattered privately. They had chosen a narrow public task and its limited telling; gratitude did not entitle him to add the rest of his family to their responsibilities afterwards.

Nor did he need to pretend the news meant nothing.

"Mujhe yeh jaana zaroori lag raha tha," he said. "Shukriya."

The sentence was true without the parts he had not been given permission to disclose.

---

Vedant's voice sounded tired now that the main answer was spoken.

"Thak gaye?" Rudra asked.

"Haan. Isiliye chhe minute."

There was no reproach in it. His teacher had not offered a heroic account which required the student to deny the effort. He had chosen a time which admitted it.

Sitara said the receiving care had accepted them, the hired equipment was returned and the new job's payments and departures were finished. The practical arrangement was closed; neither was waiting for Rudra to make a further decision before being allowed the next rest.

Rudra had been preparing to apologise for the tiredness.

He let the apology become something more useful.

"Phir main waqt nahi badhaaoonga," he said.

They exchanged goodbyes at the six minutes. The connection ended. Each phone was put away, the brief ward setup work finished and all three took their next care and rest in their own places.

The report had not created an evening audience which the teachers must continue to serve.

---

Later, Rudra lay with the name and the source beside each other in his thoughts.

It was tempting to strip away the small words: the office said, this morning, the people who had asked. To keep only the certainty-shaped piece and make it sound larger when he gave it to his mother.

He did not want to meet her with a more impressive journey than the one anybody had actually made.

The two hearing sheets remained in the cupboard. No one was asked to add this new name to them, and no friend received a message which turned the public inquiry into an explanation of the private reason behind it.

He ate supper with the help still needed. The wrist did not hold the bowl; the elbow, ankle and knee did not become easier because a question had an answer. He reported the back's familiar pull when the position was changed.

Afterwards the room was quiet enough for him to notice that he was tired too.

He would ask his mother when she wanted to hear the report, not use its arrival to claim the rest of her night.

On the two hundred and fourteenth morning, Rudra asked when Rukmini wanted to hear the new public reply.

She chose ten minutes that afternoon, after her own care and rest. He accepted after his review and actual rest. Both wanted the conversation private. The small phone tasks were separately agreed and completed in supported positions; Ram was not an assumed listener at her end, and Rudra's friends were not assembled at his.

The present checks allowed the chosen interval.

"Vedant sir aur Sitara ma'am ne kal subah usi daftar mein poochha," he told her when they connected. "Wahaan ki vartamaan jaankaari mein Swarg ka shaasak Daksh hai. Unhone usse dekha nahi. Khidki par jo jawaab mila, woh mujhe diya hai."

He said both teachers had agreed that he could pass this limited report to her with its source. The receiver had allowed the public answer to be carried too.

Rukmini was quiet for a little while.

He did not decide what the silence meant before she spoke.

---

"Kal subah ka jawaab," she said.

"Haan. Main wahaan nahi tha. Un dono se suna hai."

"Theek bataaya."

He waited, without asking her to be grateful for the news or injured by it in a way he would recognise.

There had been a part of him which wanted the answer to be different simply because she wanted home. A kinder name, a vacant place, something which would make the distance between her wish and a possible return look shorter.

The teachers had not gone there to obtain a kinder fact.

His mother had not asked him to make one for her.

At the other end, something small touched a hard surface. Perhaps a cup being set down beyond the phone. He could not see it, and did not turn the sound into a gesture he could describe afterwards.

"Main sun rahi hoon," she said when he had been quiet too long.

"Pata hai. Bas soch raha tha."

---

"Uska naam daftar mein bolne se aapke saath jo hua, woh sahi nahi ho gaya," Rudra said.

"Nahi. Raaj karne se jhooth sach nahi hota."

Her voice had lost neither its tiredness nor its firmness.

She had told him that her own sister had falsely framed her against becoming queen, that she had been forced out, and that Daksh had stood with that sister. Now a present public source named him as ruler. The answer did not supply every step by which power had been taken, or a date for each step.

It did not need to do that in order for him to refuse to call the displacement just.

He could understand the wrong in the usurpation without pretending a receiver had witnessed it for him. The past account and the present report could remain distinct. Keeping them distinct did not require putting his mother's truth in doubt every time a working office used the ruler's name.

The office had named power.

It had not absolved it.

---

"Aapko bataana tha," he said. "Ab iske baad aapko kya karna chahiye, woh tai karke nahi baitha hoon."

"Achha."

The word was dry enough that he almost smiled.

He had expected the grown-up part of this exchange to be the political statement. Instead, it might have been that small word: his mother being glad she did not have to fight a decision he had already made for her while presenting it as help.

"Ghar dekhna chaaha tha maine," she said. "Us baat ka bojh tumhaare shareer par nahi rakh rahi."

"Jaanta hoon. Seekh raha hoon," he corrected himself.

The correction did not make her laugh. It made room for the fact that understanding was not one clean moment after which the old habits would never return.

Her wish remained hers. His anger remained something he could acknowledge without converting it into a journey. There was no date of departure in either room.

---

They spoke for a little while about the difference between giving information and asking somebody to become ready because it had arrived.

He had wanted the answer. She had chosen to hear it. Neither choice required a promise that tonight she would decide the whole future of a place from which she had been driven.

"Aaj bas itna sun sakti hoon," she said.

"Itna hi laaya hoon."

It was not quite true of all the thoughts waiting in him. It was true of what he was offering her in this interval. The distinction spared her the work of refusing questions he had not yet asked.

He did not give her news she had no source for, or ask her to confirm an army, a ceremony or a room they had never seen. He did not offer the old household pain as the price of agreeing that she had suffered a wrong.

One truth had not purchased the disappearance of the others.

---

At the ten-minute end, she said goodbye.

Rudra returned it. The connection closed, both phones were put away and the finite setup work was finished. Rukmini took her next care; the shoulder support was checked before she rested in Varanasi.

In Mirzapur, Rudra gave the nurse his present answers. The assisted change of position protected the wrist and elbow, and he reported the pull rather than letting it become an unspoken tribute to the seriousness of the call. The right ankle and left knee were settled before he slept.

Nobody had been asked to carry a crown across the rock.

A small public answer had reached the person who had chosen to hear it.

That evening, the name Sampoorna came back to him while a worker was straightening a crease beneath the resting support.

Not a voice. Not a recovered memory of a place he had never seen as a child. A name his mother had chosen to tell him, belonging to the same person whose Earth name he had spoken all his life.

"Ab theek?" the worker asked.

Rudra attended to the actual question. The crease no longer pressed where it had been pressing. He said so, and the worker finished the small adjustment before going on with the rest of the care.

There was nothing grand in the exchange.

He was glad the ordinary room could still require him to answer ordinary things. It prevented the woman in Varanasi from becoming only an exile while the woman attending to his comfort became only a pair of hands beneath an important thought.

Both had work and limits outside the story he was making in his head.

---

He thought about Rukmini, too.

The name had not become a false label because she had another one. The kitchen in Varanasi had existed. The meals, the pauses at the doorway, the fear he had not always understood: those belonged to a lived Earth life, not a disguise which could now be peeled off to reveal the only real person underneath.

Nor was the life before it less real because he had learned about it late.

He had been trying to hold both without making one consume the other. He was beginning to see a third habit he needed to examine: whenever he learned a part of her past, he immediately looked for what it explained about his own danger.

That question mattered.

It was not the only question she deserved.

He could want to know where he came from without treating his mother as a hidden instruction waiting to be extracted.

---

The night was an ordinary night in care.

He slept, woke when he needed the help, gave the present answers and settled again. No face formed in the dark to supply a name before he had asked for one. The old faint stayed something the reviews had not explained; his hand did not wake more reliable because he had spent an afternoon thinking carefully.

The two hundred and fifteenth morning brought breakfast and the next check.

Later, within his short reading time, he reached the end of a page he had meant to abandon. It was not an achievement anybody needed to measure. He simply wanted the next part now, and had enough of the allowed interval to read it before the stand was moved away.

He asked to stop when the time was done.

The reading stopped at a different place. That was allowed to be the morning's small success.

---

After rest, he requested another private conversation with his mother.

Rukmini chose eight minutes that afternoon, after her own care, review and sleep. Rudra accepted. His current check and rest came before the connection; the ordinary ward help agreed the finite setup at each end, protected the injured arms from holding the phones and left the devices supported.

Both gave their present comfort answers.

They were still in their separate cities. There was no visitor listening for a family name, no notebook open for somebody else's later use. The new public report had already been delivered in the interval chosen for it. Today's question was his own request to her, not another errand assigned to the teachers.

"Aaj koi nayi khabar nahi," he said when they connected.

"Achha. Tum?"

"Main hoon. Thoda aapke baare mein poochhna tha."

"Poochho."

---

He nearly began by explaining why the information might be useful.

The familiar language was ready: what might have been hidden, what an enemy might recognise, what he would need in some later dangerous place. He did not need to make those concerns false in order to leave them outside the first sentence.

He knew about his father's side of the family. He had learned that his mother had placed the birth-kavach to hide both parental inheritances, and that its protective purpose did not answer every question about the way his childhood had been lived.

Now he wanted to ask about the inheritance itself.

Not another transformation. Not the name of a thing which could be called upon to repair the hand.

A Kul was a lineage. People before him, a belonging his mother had carried while being the person he already knew. The question could begin there, without a demand that the answer make him more useful in a fight.

---

More than half the accepted eight minutes remained.

Rukmini had not withdrawn her invitation to ask. Rudra heard her breathing at the other end, an ordinary presence on an ordinary connection. He did not search the sound for a clue she had not chosen to give.

He let the question be smaller than the uses to which he might later be tempted to put its answer.

The phone stayed on its support. His wrist stayed where the nurse had settled it. Nobody had to be made stronger, or brought across a realm, for him to ask his mother about herself.

"Ma, aapke Kul ka naam kya hai?"
