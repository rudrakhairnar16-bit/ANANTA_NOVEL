# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-NINE — THE LINEAGE SHE CARRIED

---

> *"The word had been in the city for years.*
> *What changed was who said: mine."*
> *— Ira's later register, a name heard at home*

---

"Prakriti," Rukmini said. "Mera Kul Prakriti hai."

Rudra knew the word.

He had heard it in the city, spoken as the name of a human lineage, carried through ordinary claims and difficult arguments. It had not been waiting outside his hearing until this afternoon. What he had not known was that his mother could answer a question about herself with it.

The phone remained still on its support.

"Aapki taraf se," he said.

"Haan. Meri taraf se."

He let the answer arrive without trying to find a new sensation beneath the dressings. There was no answering warmth which could prove her statement, no movement in the old marks, no memory from before he had words. A woman in Varanasi had told her son the lineage into which she had been born.

That was how he knew.

---

He said the name once, quietly.

His mother did not correct the way he said it or ask him to speak it more proudly. She waited while a familiar word found a place in the part of his life which had not contained it before.

He had been thinking of her birthplace as distance. The natural passage, the shaded court, the sound of water under paving: things he had seen much later than she had lived there. Now there was a nearer fact, not because the realm had moved, but because he could speak about his own family without ending at his father's side.

"Main aapko usmein soch nahi paaya tha," he admitted.

"Pata nahi tha."

The answer was gentle without making the years of not knowing disappear.

"Haan. Ab pata hai."

It was not everything. He did not need everything to make this one answer count.

---

For a moment he wanted to ask what the name entitled him to do.

The thought arrived almost before he noticed it. A habit trained into the shape of a useful question: what could be done with this, how quickly, against which danger. He could have wrapped it in concern for her and made it sound less like a demand.

Instead, he told her the truth.

"Naam sunte hi main uska kaam dhoondhne laga."

"Abhi mere Kul ka naam suna hai," she said. "Kaam baad mein bhi poochh sakte ho."

He smiled a little, then became serious again.

She had not offered a lesson in a technique. Kul was not the name of a form which the right knowledge would let him enter. He was still the same injured nineteen-year-old, with both inheritances and no new permission to use the body as a demonstration.

"Pehle aapki baat sun loon," he said.

"Sun rahe ho."

---

There was room left in the eight minutes, but not room for every question the answer could make.

He did not use the room badly simply because it existed. His mother had told him the name he asked for; she was not obliged to give him a complete family tree before the connection could be allowed to end.

"Yeh baat abhi humaare beech rahe," she said.

"Rahegi."

The private account had gained a word. It had not gained an audience. He was not being asked to deny his own existence; he was agreeing not to make this conversation somebody else's news without another conversation about what could be told.

He could know what he had been told without sending it to a friend for safekeeping.

The distinction mattered more because he wanted company, not less. Wanting another person near was real. It did not make every detail in his keeping his to spend.

---

At the eight-minute limit, Rukmini said goodbye.

Rudra returned it, and the call ended. The supported phones were put away after their finite setup tasks. Neither room was held inside the other while the next care began.

His nurse heard the actual answers before helping change the position. The wrist and elbow were kept from taking weight. He told her when the back pulled, waited while the support was attended to and answered again before the right ankle and left knee were settled.

The old faint had no new explanation. A family name was not a diagnosis.

In Varanasi, his mother received her own next care and rested. He knew she had ended because she needed that part of her day, not because the name had frightened her into taking it back.

It had been given.

It could remain given through a closed connection.

---

Afterwards, he lay listening to a trolley wheel which complained at the same point in every turn.

The room contained no new object from Swarg. No sheet had been signed, no figure had appeared to approve the telling. His hand was as unreliable as it had been before the call.

And still something had changed.

His mother's side no longer ended in the words he did not know. It had a name she had chosen to say to him.

For a while he did not ask it to do anything else.

After the next rest, Rudra discovered that he wanted to tell somebody he was glad.

The wish embarrassed him more than asking for company with his anger had. Anger could arrive with a reason for taking up space. Gladness felt, absurdly, as though it ought to be able to look after itself.

He had learned something about his own family. The details were private. Neither fact required him to pretend the afternoon had been empty.

He asked whether Veer wanted four minutes of company after his own care. No explanation to solve, no work to do, just talk.

Veer chose a later interval after rest. Rudra accepted it, ate first and let his own next check finish before the supported phone was placed. At the connection, both gave the present comfort answers. Veer's shoulder, ribs and knee had not acquired a holiday because his friend had good news he could not describe.

"Aaj thoda achha lag raha hai," Rudra said.

"Achha."

He had prepared a defence of why he could say only that much.

Veer had not asked for one.

---

For a moment, neither spoke.

Beyond Veer's phone, there was a small clatter followed by a worker saying something which the microphone did not carry clearly. Veer answered the practical question at his end before returning to the conversation.

"Haan," he said. "Main hoon."

Rudra had been about to apologise for the silence.

Instead he told Veer about the trolley wheel. It made its complaining sound at exactly the same point in the turn, as though the whole purpose of the trolley's journey was to return to its one grievance.

Veer said that sounded like a man he had once argued with at the akhada, without identifying him or making the old contest into a new report.

"Usko jeetna tha," Rudra said.

"Wheel ko bhi."

Rudra smiled carefully enough not to ask the back for a louder version.

The joke did not need a political fact beneath it. It was allowed to be a wheel and two friends being unfair to it for a minute.

---

"Radio?" Rudra asked.

"Band hai."

"Kyun?"

"Abhi tu bol raha hai. Do jagah se bakwaas sunne ki capacity nahi hai."

"Bahut samman mil raha hai aaj."

"Good news ka discount hai."

Rudra let the warmth of it stay small.

He was not taking his mother's name into the joke, or placing a clue where a friend would have to pretend not to see it. He had said that he felt glad. Veer had chosen to hear him and had kept enough of himself in the conversation to be ordinary about it.

That was different from receiving a report.

Rudra found that he did not want to turn it into one. The afternoon's answer belonged in its chosen private place; these four minutes could belong to their friendship without being made less real by what was not being said.

---

Near the end, Veer said he would leave the radio off a little longer.

It was his own choice about his own room, not an invitation for Rudra to stay connected until the next programme. Rudra did not fill the space on his behalf.

They said goodbye at the four minutes. The call ended; the phones were put away, the brief setup help finished and both took the care and rest which followed their chosen conversation.

No friend had received a lineage name or been charged with its keeping.

Rudra lay still afterwards, surprised by how little effort it took not to send another message. The company had not made him lonelier by ending. It had given the gladness somewhere to be heard without requiring the private cause to leave his mother's hands.

He did not need a whole audience to make the answer true.

---

Later, a worker asked whether he wanted the light lowered.

He chose not yet. There was nothing he needed to read; he simply wanted a little while in the room as it was. The request was accepted, and the worker went on with the evening's tasks.

Rudra watched the curtain's edge move when someone passed beyond it.

For years, not knowing had become so entangled with not being ready that he had begun to think the two must be the same thing. Perhaps the adults had measured a capacity he could not see. Perhaps there would be a day when the right amount of pain or practice finally made the hidden things his to hear.

Now an answer had been spoken while his hand still needed help with supper.

He did not have to pretend he understood every reason for the old silences. He could notice this: he had not become less entitled to an honest answer by continuing to need care.

That seemed worth keeping.

When the next check came, he gave the actual answers, accepted the support correction and asked for the light to be lowered then. There was no late reading task which somebody else had to remain for.

He slept without sending the name anywhere.

In Varanasi, that evening had its own small requests.

Rukmini wanted the edge of the support checked before the meal. The worker heard her, adjusted it and waited for her answer instead of assuming that silence meant the position had become comfortable. She said where it was still wrong. The second adjustment was better.

Then the meal could begin.

She did not have to spend the rest of the evening inside the few minutes in which she had spoken a lineage name. There was water to accept, an ordinary mouthful which needed more time than the worker had first allowed, the next stretch of rest. She asked for that time and was given it.

The room was not Swarg.

It did not have to pretend to be Swarg for her to live in it.

---

After the meal, she listened to the footsteps outside.

Somebody stopped to ask where a thing had been left. The answer was impatient but useful; the footsteps went on. A door closed too hard, then opened again with the brief apology of a person who had not intended the noise.

She knew this world through more than the work of hiding in it.

Rukmini had been her name through real days. It had been spoken because somebody needed her, because food was getting cold, because an ordinary decision could not wait for a person who was standing at the stove. None of that became counterfeit when she said Sampoorna to her son.

Nor had Sampoorna ended because the people who drove her out did not want her to have a future there.

The two names did not stand on opposite sides of the bed asking her to choose which life to disown.

She was the person who had lived them.

---

Prakriti was not a new sound to her.

Yet saying it to Rudra had been different from knowing it in the privacy of her own mind. The word had entered the space between them, where it could be heard, answered and remembered by somebody who had been left outside it.

She had worried about how much he would make it carry.

He had told her the beginning of that impulse. A name, then its use. She had been glad he had said it instead of offering a more dutiful version of himself. It gave her a person she could answer, not a silent intention she would discover only after it had become a plan.

She could not ask for that honesty and then require every honest sentence to be the one she most wanted to hear.

The thought was uncomfortable enough to be useful.

---

She still wanted him safe.

That wish had not become smaller because she had admitted that keeping somebody safe could not always mean keeping him uninformed. It remained a fierce thing, able to dress itself in practical arguments before she had noticed what it was asking her to take away.

She knew the old temptation: wait until he was stronger, wait until the next danger had passed, wait until there was a way to tell the story without anything in it hurting.

There would never be a past which had not hurt her.

There was, now, a son who could ask about it.

He was not responsible for making the telling painless. Nor had he become the person whose body must settle its account. She had given him a word about his birth, not a bill to be paid back through the injured hand.

She let the next ordinary breath come without trying to turn the thought into a grand promise.

---

A worker returned to check whether she was ready for the next care.

"Haan," Rukmini said. "Pehle bataa dena kis taraf karna hai."

The explanation was given before the supported movement. She answered the present questions, asked for the pause she needed and waited until the shoulder was settled again. Afterwards the worker checked once more and finished that part of the task.

It was a modest exchange. It still mattered that she could ask what was about to happen to her.

She had not always made enough room for the same question from her child.

Remembering her own fear did not remove that truth. Being wronged elsewhere had not made every silence at home an act which could no longer be questioned. She did not need to deny what had been done to her in order to acknowledge what he still had a right to ask.

The rest would have to be lived, not announced into being tonight.

---

When the room grew quieter, she chose no further call.

There was no message waiting to tell Ram or a teacher what Rudra had heard. The conversation had remained where she and her son had chosen to keep it. She did not need another person's approval before allowing her own answer to stand.

She wanted to see home again. She also wanted to sleep.

Neither wish had to defeat the other to be real.

The worker put what she needed within the usable reach already checked and lowered the light as requested. Rukmini gave the final comfort answer and let the day's speaking end.

She had not travelled anywhere.

She had nevertheless stopped leaving one part of herself outside the conversation.

The two hundred and sixteenth morning brought the name into an older, less comforting memory.

Rudra remembered the relief driver's account of the food which had come back undelivered. A hospital meal, two groups claiming the way, two different slips offered as if a person had to choose the right belonging before a pot could reach hungry people.

He had heard the driver's account. He had not stood at both obstructions and seen everything for himself.

Prakriti had been among the names used there.

It had not made everybody carrying that lineage the woman who tore a delivery slip. Vedant had insisted on that distinction at the time. Rudra found he needed it equally now, when the word had entered his own family instead of remaining a label he could examine from a distance.

His mother's belonging was not an excuse for another person's act.

Another person's act did not make her account of herself untrue.

---

He did not dislike the answer because it refused to be simple.

There was a peculiar relief in that too. He had not acquired a lineage made entirely of the people he would most like to resemble. He had learned something real, which existed before his need to make it useful or innocent.

His father had taught him how quickly a family name could become a target. The men claiming roads had shown him how quickly it could become an excuse to choose targets. Neither lesson meant that the only honest response was to have no people.

He could refuse a demand without pretending the bond did not exist.

He could question an act without placing a whole birth behind it as the answer.

The thought did not give him a cleaner past. It gave him a harder, more usable way to remain inside the one he had.

---

The review interrupted before the thought could grow into a speech.

He gave the account of the night, the grip which still could not be trusted and the pull during the ordinary assisted change of position. He did not turn the family conversation into evidence that any movement had become safe.

The nurse heard the actual description and attended to the support. The current plan for care, short reading and rest remained the plan; there was no new journey hidden in his question about the afternoon.

Breakfast came afterwards.

He ate with help, let the ordinary work take as long as it needed and slept when the next rest arrived. The old faint had not gained a name simply because his mother's lineage had. Nobody was asked to try a technique on him to see whether the answer could be made visible.

When he woke, the question he wanted to ask was still about her.

---

She had told him where she was born, the name she had before Earth, the injustice which had forced her away. She had told him she wanted to see home again. None of those answers required him to imagine that she had left it in one piece and walked into an easier life.

He realised he had been thinking about what was taken from her almost entirely in things he could picture.

A place. A future she had not been allowed to choose. A false accusation shaped by somebody who wanted her out of it.

But what happened to the part a person continued to call their own after being told they no longer had a place among the people who used its name?

He did not know what she had called that relation privately. Knowing her birth did not entitle him to supply the rest of her feelings.

He wanted to ask without putting a preferred answer into her mouth.

---

He requested another conversation, and Rukmini chose ten minutes that afternoon.

She would take her own review, care and rest first. He accepted after his own next rest. They agreed that it would remain private, and the ward help at each end accepted only the brief supported setup, not an obligation to listen or make a record.

The two hearing sheets were not brought out. They belonged to a different inquiry and could give no permission for this one. No old trip worker was recalled to arrange a connection between two ordinary phones.

When they were ready, his mother gave her current comfort answer. Rudra answered from his own supported position, the wrist and elbow settled and the screen where it could remain without his grip.

The call connected.

---

"Aaj bhi us naam ke baare mein soch raha hoon," he said.

"Kis tarah?"

"Pehle bas socha ki aapke baare mein mujhe nayi baat pata chali. Phir laga, aapke liye toh nayi nahi thi."

"Nahi thi."

"Mujhe pata chala, usse pehle bhi aapka tha."

He heard her take a little time before saying yes.

He was not offering a discovery she had failed to make about herself. He was admitting how much of her life existed outside his learning of it. The name had not started on his afternoon, and her relation to it could not be measured only by the relief he had felt when she spoke.

There was time left to ask about that relation.

For once, he did not begin by asking what part of it he might need in a fight.

"Kal tumhaari aawaaz thodi badli thi," Rukmini said.

"Buri tarah?"

"Alag."

He considered that. She had heard the voice which actually reached her, not the later conversation or the night he had spent with it.

"Achha laga tha," he said. "Sab theek ho gaya, waise nahi. Par apne baare mein ek jagah khaali thi."

She let the answer keep both parts.

It would have been easier to offer her the undamaged version. To say the name had made him whole, that now he understood why everything had happened, that the joy could be accepted only if it arrived without an old question attached.

He did not feel that way.

He felt glad, and still capable of being hurt by what had been kept from him. The two feelings were not waiting for a verdict which would leave only one alive.

---

"Pata chalne se pehle main kam nahi tha," he said, slowly. "Par pata chalne ka haq bhi tha. Dono samajh raha hoon."

"Haan," she answered. "Tum kam nahi the."

"Aur aap bhi sirf woh nahi ho jo mujhe ab pata chal raha hai."

There was a little silence.

He had spent so long looking at the people around him for the missing explanation that he had almost forgotten they continued to exist when they were not explaining him. His mother did not begin at a birth-kavach. His father did not begin at a training hour. Neither truth made the birth or the training unimportant.

It meant the people could be asked questions which were not only about what they had done to him or for him.

He could not make those other questions disappear. He could stop making them the price of every new piece of her life.

---

Rukmini asked him not to make a good afternoon into proof that difficult conversations would no longer be needed.

"Woh bhi karenge," she said.

He answered that he knew.

She was not offering all of Ram's answers in advance, or asking him to accept her word as the final account of a different person's choices. She was leaving her own part of the work open rather than declaring it finished because the son sounded less angry today.

Rudra found that the honesty steadied him more than reassurance would have.

He did not have to protect the new answer from all the questions which had existed before it. A true thing could stand beside an unfinished thing without being destroyed by it.

The supported phone made a small sound when a notification arrived. He did not reach for it. The call remained the task he had chosen, and the wrist remained where it had been settled.

Nothing needed to be proved through movement.

---

"Naam kisi kagaz par likhwaane ko nahi bola," he said.

"Zaroorat lagi thi?"

"Nahi. Bas ek pal socha tha, likha ho toh jaise zyada pakka hoga. Phir laga, aapse suna hai. Woh apni jagah pakka hai."

He was not claiming that a private statement answered every possible historical question. He was saying he did not need to turn his mother into a doubtful witness to her own lineage until a more impressive object could speak for her.

The two old hearing sheets could remain what they were. His memory of this conversation could remain a memory of hearing her, not a copied institutional finding with the office's authority borrowed for it.

"Abhi likhna nahi chahta," he added. "Ya kisi aur se likhwaana."

"Theek."

The choice ended there, without giving an absent friend the work of keeping a new family record.

---

Outside his room, someone was asking for the way to the receiving desk.

Rudra heard the answer and the steps going away. The ordinary word receiving stayed with him for a moment. People arrived somewhere with a need, and a person there had to say whether the need could be taken into their care.

He had been received on roads and at beds. He had been told to wait, to turn, to stop. His mother's story contained the different word: sent away.

He knew he could not measure that loss by comparing it to a hospital doorway. He was thinking about the relation between a place and the people who could tell somebody that there was no longer room.

She had not ceased to have been born there.

But that was a fact he knew now, not a description of how it had felt to remain herself afterwards. He had no right to make a simple sentence out of her whole private answer before hearing it.

---

Several minutes remained in the ten they had accepted.

He could ask one real question and hear her, rather than arrange another list for a future hour. His mother was comfortable enough to continue; he gave his own present answer when the familiar pull was checked at his end. The phone did not need to move.

He thought of the name as she had said it yesterday. Not an office's permission, not a reward he had earned by surviving long enough. Her account of where she belonged.

There was something he had not yet asked about the person who had kept that account through the years before he could hear it.

"Ma, Swarg se nikaale jaane ke baad bhi aap apne Kul ko apna keh paayi thi?"
