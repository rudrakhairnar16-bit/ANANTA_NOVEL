# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-ONE — THE NAME SHE HAD BEFORE

---

> *"The first name was not replaced by the second.*
> *That was the difficulty with the truth: it left more than one real life standing."*
> *— Ira's later register, a family account shared by permission*

---

"Swarg mein," Rukmini said. "Svar Loka. Main wahin paida hui thi. Wahin badi hui."

Rudra heard the small sound of her swallowing before the next sentence.

"Wahan mera naam Sampoorna tha."

He did not reach for the phone.

The voice had come from the same supported place in Varanasi, with the same slight roughness at the end of the evening. Nothing in the room announced that a different woman had taken his mother's place. The nurse stood where she had stood a moment before, within reach of care, not waiting to bow.

"Sampoorna," he repeated.

"Haan. Yahaan Rukmini."

He looked at the ordinary edge of the stand.

He had sat in Swarg and wanted to describe its water to her. He had thought of calling home from the other side of an experience she had not shared.

She had been a child there.

"Aapne mujhe bataaya nahi tha," he said.

"Nahi."

It would have been easier if she had mistaken the sentence for a question. Then he could have been angry with an explanation instead of sitting with the admission he had actually received.

---

The nurse reminded them that the accepted interval was ending.

The reminder did not become unreasonable because the last minute had changed what he knew about his mother. Rukmini heard it too. At her end, help was ready for the next care; she did not dismiss it to keep him on the line.

"Main aur poochhna chahta hoon," Rudra said.

"Main baat karoongi. Abhi dono rukte hain."

He wanted the promise to mean a call beginning again as soon as the screen went dark. It did not. They would ask for another interval after actual rest and the next reviews, and she would choose it with him.

"Theek hai," he managed.

"Good night, beta."

He said it back. The call ended at the agreed limit.

There was no final hidden sentence murmured while a nurse thought it was over.

---

His next answer was to the person beside his bed.

The back felt as it had before. He had not felt new dizziness or lost his breath; the silence had been shock, not an attempt to endure a new symptom without telling anyone. She checked him directly, heard what he could say about the sudden news without requiring its private content, and helped with the planned position.

The elbow needed protecting through the movement. The wrist could not become a handle simply because his thoughts had gone somewhere else. He gave the small practical answers and waited for the ankle and knee to be comfortable before asking whether the phone could be put aside.

He did not want another call tonight.

The wish surprised him. It sat beside the much louder wish to ask everything, neither cancelling the other. There was a difference between wanting his mother and wanting to be carried straight out of the feeling her answer had made.

For a while he let the room remain a room.

---

Sampoorna.

The name did not fit easily into the places where he usually remembered her.

It did not arrive carrying the smell of dal or the impatient instruction to eat before something became cold. It had not been called through the doorway when he left for college. He could not yet hear it in anybody's voice except hers.

That was not proof those other memories belonged to a false person.

He tried it beside Rukmini rather than over her.

A woman could have been called by one name before her son existed and another throughout his life. He had accepted that much about his father, though acceptance had come slowly and had not made every withheld year easy to forgive. His mother was not obliged to be less complicated so the house in Varanasi could remain recognisable.

He was still angry she had not told him.

The anger did not need to grow into an accusation that she had never been his mother.

---

At the far end of the finished call, Rukmini asked for help before trying to alter the shoulder's position.

Dadi came when called. The care proceeded in the ordinary order: the present pain described, the support checked, the movement made without a hurried reach after the phone. Rukmini did not say she could manage alone because she had managed a difficult sentence.

When she was settled, she looked towards the darkened screen.

Her son knew the place and the name now. He did not know the whole story. Neither did she know exactly what he would do with the little she had given him, or whether tomorrow's first words would be angry ones.

For tonight she did not send a second message instructing him how to feel.

Dadi put the phone out of the way when asked. The water stayed near. Elsewhere in Varanasi, ordinary traffic was thinning into the night, and in Mirzapur her son lay awake for a while before sleep finally came.

Day two hundred and one began with questions whose answers had not changed overnight.

The weak grip still needed help. Rudra had slept after lying awake, not discovered a way to rest without sleep. During the review he described both things, and the team kept the supports and present restrictions rather than reading last night's news as a reason to test a little more.

When he asked about another call, he asked after care.

The request reached Rukmini after hers. She chose the afternoon, following rest, for a private ten-minute interval if their current checks still allowed it. He accepted. The morning remained available for food, a little ordinary reading and the long blank part of recovery which no new name made exciting.

He was impatient through some of it.

Patience was not a permanent improvement won during yesterday's call. Sometimes it was simply not sending the next message while he wanted to.

---

They connected at the newly agreed time, after both ends confirmed readiness.

Rudra's phone was supported. Rukmini had help available without an audience sitting in to make her answer respectable. Ram was having his own rest; this was not his account to deliver in her place.

"Aapko kya bulaaoon?" Rudra asked.

"Ma bologe toh sun loongi."

He nearly smiled, then stopped trying to make the question sound lighter than it was.

"Naam ki baat kar raha hoon."

"Yahaan main Rukmini hoon. Us naam se jo jeeya hai, woh bhi mera hai. Sampoorna bhi main hi thi."

The answer gave him no trick with which to separate the truthful woman from the one who had withheld a truth. Both had been present when she reminded him about the shirt, and when she listened to him talk about the shade.

"Main Swarg hokar aa gaya," he said. "Aapne mujhe tab bhi nahi bataaya."

"Haan."

"Aap pehle bataa sakti thi."

She took time with that sentence. He heard a small sound at her end and waited while she answered the worker checking her position.

"Keh sakti thi," she said when she was comfortable. "Main talti rahi. Darr tha. Par chup rehne ka faisla bhi mera tha."

He had expected to need a long argument to reach that much. Without the argument, the anger had nowhere useful to rush. It remained where it had begun, in the simple knowledge that his mother could have told him and had not.

---

He did not ask her to undo it by telling everything faster.

For years he had thought a withheld answer must be a single thing kept behind one door. When the door opened, the whole room would be visible and he could decide what to feel about it. His father's telling had already proved otherwise. A place led to people; a name led to years; love complicated a harm without erasing it.

Now the same difficulty had arrived in a voice which had once asked whether he wanted more rice.

"Mujhe gussa hai," he told her.

"Suna maine."

"Phir bhi baat karni hai."

"Mujhe bhi."

It was an agreement small enough for both of them to keep in this interval.

---

Rukmini asked what he had heard in the name.

He could not answer neatly. Newness, mostly. A name which had existed without a younger version of himself somewhere nearby needing to be fed or found. He told her that much, with an awkward laugh at his own selfish picture of time.

She did not laugh at the picture.

"Bachche aise hi sochte hain," she said. "Tum ab poochh rahe ho."

He looked towards the edge of the bed, where the blanket had been folded back clear of the support.

"Aur aap ab bol rahi ho."

"Haan."

For once neither tried to award the other the greater bravery. They were late to the conversation in different ways. His late question had not caused her long silence. Her present answer did not make his wanting it into a kindness for which he ought to be thanked.

He said the name again, less tentatively.

Sampoorna did not change the light through the window. It did not move beneath his skin or explain the exact pressure in his damaged back. It was not a word he could use to get out of the body listening to it.

It gave his mother a childhood outside the reach of his own memories.

He had not known how strange a gift that could be until he tried to picture her receiving an instruction instead of giving one, waiting for somebody else to finish a task, being bored while adults spoke above her head.

He stopped before the imagined child acquired a face he would later mistake for something she had described.

There was a living person on the phone who could tell him what she wanted him to know. He did not have to invent her because he was afraid another silence would arrive.

"Wahan ki yaad aati hai toh kya yaad aata hai?" he asked.

Rukmini was quiet long enough for him to hear somebody moving beyond her room. She answered after the sound passed.

"Har baar ek jaisa nahi. Kabhi jagah. Kabhi bas bachpan. Subah uthne ko jee na karna. Kisi ka kaam khatam hone ka intezaar karte hue lagna ki mera hi kaam sabse zaroori hai."

A small smile entered her voice.

"Main hamesha tumhari ma jitni samajhdaar nahi thi."

"Ab ho?"

"Itna bhi khush mat ho."

He smiled before he remembered to be careful with the laugh. There was room for the joke without making their earlier anger disappear.

She had not given him a palace, an address or a procession. He had not asked for one. What he could begin to picture was not the private design of a house he had never seen, but the impatience of a child who had once occupied time without knowing how much of it she would later miss.

That impatience was familiar enough to hurt.

---

He asked whether she thought of Varanasi as home too.

"Haan," she said. "Yahaan ka ghar bhi."

The word *bhi* mattered more than he had expected.

Two places did not have to stand at opposite ends of a scale, each made lighter by every honest thing she admitted about the other. He had spent so much of his father's telling trying to keep the city where he had grown up from being declared merely a hiding place. His mother had lived years there too. Her first home did not cancel the real one in which she had become his mother.

He could keep wanting to know about the first.

"Main tumhare bachpan ki baat se bach nahi rahi," she said, after checking that he was still comfortable. "Jo tumne mujhse ghar ke baare mein kaha tha, woh mujhe yaad hai."

He looked at his supported hand.

She did not have to recite their old conversation. He remembered the child in the courtyard and her voice admitting that not every morning had been stopped. He did not want that child escorted out of the story now that a more extraordinary childhood had entered it.

"Dono baat karoonga," he said.

"Kar sakte ho."

It was not a promise that she would always answer well. It was a refusal, today, to make one truth the price of another's silence.

---

Before the interval ended, Rudra asked about telling his friends.

Not forwarding a recording; he had made none. Not a roomful of listeners admitted while his mother thought she was speaking only to him. He wanted to be able to say why he was quiet without choosing between a lie and giving away the whole of an account she had scarcely begun.

"Aadhya, Veer aur Ira ko naam aur janm ki jagah bataa doon? Bas utna."

"Haan," she said. "Itna bataa sakte ho. Mere liye koi faisla karne ko mat kehna."

"Nahi kahoonga."

His own feelings were his to describe. Her history did not have to become a meeting agenda to give those feelings somewhere to go.

The nurse gave the final reminder. They stopped instead of seeing whether ten minutes could stretch into eleven if the words became important enough. Both said goodbye. The connection ended, the phone was taken off the stand and Rudra answered the current check before the next position change.

At the other end, Rukmini accepted her own care and rest. She had not volunteered her shoulder for a test because her voice had been steady.

---

Later, he asked for a short message to go to each friend.

He would like to talk after rest, if they wanted, about something his mother had told him and allowed him to share. Nothing for them to solve. No new visit required, no notebook, and no reply needed before their care.

The messages actually went. Rudra did not spend the next hour watching for acknowledgment. He ate, listened to a little of the college reading with ordinary help and stopped when the allotted part was done. There was no shortage of new things in his head; that did not make his hands better at turning a page.

The replies came at different times.

Aadhya would like to hear him after her next rest. Veer chose the same later interval if his knee care was finished. Ira offered a shorter part of it, by audio and with the eye resting, and said she would leave when she had said she would.

A six-minute call was agreed, with Ira's portion shorter. The brief phone help was accepted as part of the actual ward work; no mentor was summoned to hold an open-ended conference between patients.

They were his friends, not a committee required to find the correct use for his mother's name.

For a while, before the evening call, that distinction was enough to make waiting bearable.

When the evening interval came, each of them answered from their own place.

Veer's knee care was finished and he had rested afterwards. Aadhya's phone was supported clear of the hand she still needed to watch. Ira listened with the ordinary eye resting, the left covering left alone; she confirmed that she could hear without increasing the volume. Rudra gave the nurse his current answers before she finished the short setup.

No chairs were travelling tonight.

"Ma ne khud bataaya," he said once they were connected. "Woh Swarg mein paida hui thi. Wahan badi hui. Wahan ka naam Sampoorna tha."

He said she had allowed him to tell the three of them those facts. The rest was still a private conversation beginning between them, not a story which had been completed and withheld from the group.

Nobody answered for a moment.

Then Veer said, "Tu theek se sun paaya tha?"

"Haan. Doosre call par bhi baat hui."

Veer accepted the answer. He had asked whether his friend had actually heard, not offered to decide whether somebody else's mother was telling the truth.

---

"Kaisa lag raha hai?" Aadhya asked.

Rudra had prepared a clever answer while waiting for the call. It vanished when he tried to use it.

"Gussa bhi hai," he said. "Aur ab aur jaan-na hai. Dono ek saath."

"Abhi humse kya chahiye?"

"Pata nahi. Sun lo."

"Sun rahe hain."

It was almost irritating that the words helped. He had not been given advice against which to sharpen himself. No new theory about his body had arrived to make the room briefly exciting. He could tell them that the name felt strange in his mouth, and that his mother had still sounded like the woman asking about dinner.

Ira said she had heard the two names.

"Ek bolne ke liye doosra mitaana nahi padta," she said. "Tumhe aaj hi har baat samajhni bhi nahi hai."

There was nothing in her voice asking to be appointed the keeper of this account. She had not opened the notebook. She did not offer to inspect his mother through something he could neither hear nor verify.

For these few minutes she was his friend on an ordinary audio call.

He had needed that more than he had known how to request it.

---

Veer asked whether Rukmini had heard about the game before or after telling him.

"Pehle," Rudra said.

"Theek hai. Toh meri news purani ho gayi."

"History mein date se jagah milti hai. Teri haar rahegi."

Aadhya let out one short laugh. Rudra smiled rather than joining loudly; when the nurse looked over, he gave the present back feeling without pretending the question had interrupted the good part.

The joke did not make his mother's answer less important. It kept that answer from swallowing every other voice in the room.

Ira reached her offered limit and said goodbye. They actually ended her part of the connection. She wanted the phone put away, not a later photograph or a written summary of what the others said after she left.

Veer asked no further question about Swarg. Aadhya said she was glad he had heard the name from his mother herself. Neither friend knew the life Rukmini had only begun to name.

They could let the unknown remain large without rushing to occupy it.

---

At the six-minute limit, Rudra thanked them.

Aadhya asked whether he wanted another message tonight. He said no, not because she had asked something wrong, but because he wanted to stop speaking for a while. She accepted it. Veer said good night before his next care. The call ended; the supported phones were put aside with the brief help already agreed.

Rudra's nurse checked his comfort and the position of the elbow before leaving him to rest. The day had contained two chosen conversations. It had not secretly contained another journey, a cleared exercise or a new reason to postpone care until somebody else's curiosity was satisfied.

He could hear work continuing beyond the curtain.

He had once imagined that learning a parent's secret would make every ordinary person around him look foolish for caring about ordinary things. Instead he was grateful for the person who noticed that the corner of the sheet had caught where his weak hand could not free it safely.

She put it right when he asked.

He said thank you and meant the actual small thing, not everything which had ever kept him alive.

---

Before sleep, he thought of asking his father about the name.

Ram might have things of his own to say. That did not make him the person who must confirm whether Rukmini's past was allowed to belong to her. Rudra had heard her directly. For the next part of her account, he wanted to keep asking her.

The wish did not require a call now.

He let it wait through the night, beside the anger which had become less sharp without being finished. He had learned where she had begun. He still did not know how a childhood there had become the life he knew here.

The next morning, he needed help with a cup.

It was an unremarkable difficulty, except that some stubborn part of him had expected an extraordinary truth to make an ordinary action easier. The grip did not share the expectation. He asked before trying to keep the cup steady with uncertain fingers, accepted the help and described what the hand could manage when the clinician asked.

Day two hundred and two remained a day of protected movement and current review. The elbow was not cleared for a load. The back still determined how long a position was useful. The ankle and knee had not been waiting for a birthplace to explain them.

He ate breakfast with the help he needed.

Afterwards, he tried the college paragraph again. Somebody read the short part he chose, at the comfortable angle and pace. He lost the sense halfway through, admitted it, and asked for that sentence once more rather than nodding towards work he had not heard.

On the second reading he understood it.

The small success belonged to the page. It did not prove that he had understood his mother, or that the anger had become a better kind of son. He stopped after the agreed portion and rested, pleased for a moment by something which had not asked him to resolve his whole life first.

---

A new request went to Varanasi after care.

Rukmini chose a short call later in the morning, after her own rest. Eight minutes again, if both present checks were satisfactory. Rudra accepted the time; the ward workers accepted the brief setup without taking on an indefinite private lesson. The space between the request and the call was actually used for rest, not another stream of questions sent in advance.

When they connected, he could hear the ordinary room around her.

A faint movement of cloth. Somebody outside asking for something to be brought nearer. The pause while his mother confirmed that her own position was comfortable before the helper gave them privacy. No sound could tell him what her childhood had looked like, but these sounds could tell him she was here.

He was glad of here.

"Neend aayi?" she asked.

"Aayi. Pehle thodi der nahi aayi. Phir so gaya."

She answered the same question for herself. There had been rest, not a painless night; her current care had been done. He did not turn the admission into an instruction for her to stop speaking at once. They had both been asked, and both had chosen this interval.

He told her he had spoken to the three friends as she had allowed.

"Naam aur jagah," he said. "Aur apna jo lag raha tha, woh."

"Theek hai."

She did not ask him to make them send back the correct response. There was no family verdict being counted at the far end of the phone.

---

"Aapko kaisa laga, bolkar?" he asked.

Rukmini took time to answer.

"Darr abhi bhi hai," she said. "Tumhe bataaya hai. Isliye yeh nahi ki ab kuch mehsoos hi nahi hoga."

"Halka nahi laga?"

"Sirf halka nahi."

He could understand the distinction, though he had wanted an easier answer for her. His father's first telling had not ended his father's fear. A difficult truth could be right to tell without making the person who told it immediately grateful for the relief.

"Mujhe achha laga ki aapne khud bola," he said.

"Aur gussa bhi hai."

"Haan."

"Pata hai."

There was almost affection in how accurately she could hear the unfinished part.

He no longer needed to test whether she would permit it by saying it more harshly. She had heard him. What she would do with that knowledge over time was larger than either of these calls.

---

For a moment, he thought of the journeys they had already made.

Each one had been defined in advance by a line back: a pause, a person waiting, a time after which the whole party must leave rather than spend what belonged to somebody else's rest. His body had made those limits impossible to treat as abstract. He could remember wanting home in the middle of wanting to stay.

He had been able to want both because a return had existed in the plan.

He did not know what returning meant in his mother's history. He had begun supplying a journey between the two names as readily as he had once supplied a childhood which contained nothing except waiting to become his mother.

Nothing she had told him yet described that journey.

Swarg was a birthplace. Varanasi was a real home. Between those facts was not an empty space he had permission to decorate with whatever made the whole account easiest to love.

He waited while she answered a brief comfort check. She returned without being hurried, and he let his next breath stay an ordinary one.

---

There was still time in this new call.

He had learned a place and a name, heard her admit the silence and kept the older question about their house alive. None of those answers needed to be withdrawn to make the next question possible.

He wanted to know about a choice before assuming there had been one.

The familiar word for leaving came first because it was the one he had. He could still picture somebody packing, deciding what mattered, looking back from a doorway they might one day use again. He knew those were pictures, not her account.

His mother was waiting for him to speak.

He did not ask for directions to her childhood or whether the path they had used would carry her there. He asked about the part no inspected road could answer.

"Ma, aapne woh ghar kyun chhoda?"
