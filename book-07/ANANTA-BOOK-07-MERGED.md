# ANANTA — BOOK 07 — MERGED

# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-ONE — WHERE THE QUESTIONS HAD TO GO

---

> *"The first direction was one word.*
> *Learning to take it required considerably more."*
> *— Ira's later register, opening of the ascent account*

---

"Swarg," Vedant said. "Svar Loka. Wahan jo Devta santulan ka kaam sambhaalte hain, sawaal un tak le jaana hoga."

For a moment Rudra forgot the little fan making its uneven circle above him.

Not because the answer was a promise. Because it was a place.

"Aapke paas poora jawaab nahi hai?"

"Nahi. Main kis se poochhna hai, usmein madad kar sakta hoon. Unke har jawaab ki guarantee nahi."

The elbow lay thick and uncomfortable in its support. Rudra looked at it while the name acquired distance: another realm, living people, somebody who might have done the work his father had only been able to describe from outside it.

"Wahan tak—"

"Ek puraana kudrati raasta hai. Pataal waala nahi. Uski abhi ki haalat dekhni hogi, aana aur lautna dono. Aaj tum kahin nahi jaa rahe. Main bhi phone rakh raha hoon."

A small sound came from Vedant's end, a person waiting without wanting to interrupt. His teacher answered that he was finished.

"Yeh naam doston ko bataa sakta hoon?"

"Haan. Meri taraf se jagah ka sujhaav hai. Safar tay hua, aisa mat kehna."

Rudra said he understood. They exchanged good nights although it was not quite night, then let the two rooms separate.

The answer had taken less time than the question had taken to learn how to ask.

---

The nurse wanted to know whether the ankle's throbbing had changed.

It had eased in the corrected position. The deeper breath still caught at the back. He gave both answers, and watched her compare the new note with the last one instead of looking towards the phone for the next task.

Later, someone brought a comb with a tooth missing.

Rudra had not asked for a comb. He had asked whether his hair could be made less objectionable before the next examination, a remark he had intended as a joke until the worker asked which side he preferred the parting.

"Koi bhi."

She waited.

He chose a side.

There was a tug where dried sweat had held a strand together. She loosened it with water and worked from the end, while he sat supported at the angle already allowed. It was such a small service that his throat tightened over it. He had become used to hands approaching his head because something had to be checked.

"Itna serious mat ho," she said. "Baal hi hain."

He smiled without lifting his head away from the support.

By evening the phone was charging again. The comb lay beside it, clean, the missing tooth turned towards the wall.

He did not tell himself that a new world had been booked for tomorrow.

---

The night contained a pain check, an awkward waking for prescribed care and a dream about missing an examination for a subject he could not remember choosing.

In the dream everybody else had found the room.

He woke irritated rather than enlightened. The nurse helped change the sheet beneath his back. Nothing spoke inside him to explain the realm his teacher had named; nothing in the faint colour beneath the old marks repaired the hand which could not help pull the cloth straight.

Morning came through the window one dull pane at a time.

After the review and breakfast, Rudra asked for three short messages to be sent, each to a friend who could hear it when ready. He said what Vedant had actually answered. Swarg, Svar Loka. The Devtas responsible for that work were the people to seek. An old separate passage might make reaching the place possible, after inspection. No route had yet been checked, and none of them had been asked to agree to go.

He listened to the recording before it was sent. Then he asked the nurse to remove the opening in which he had said *hum jaayenge*.

The second version said *wahan poochhna hoga*.

She played that one back too.

---

Veer's reply arrived after lunch.

"Achha. Destination mil gaya. Ab conductor se mat lad lena."

Rudra laughed more carefully than he wanted to. His back had not agreed to become part of the joke.

Aadhya's answer was shorter: she had heard the source and the difference between a suggestion and a plan. Ira replied after her own rest, asking that the inspection finding be given to her when it existed, not in the hopeful tense.

None asked him to stand up for them.

He watched a schoolboy on the opposite roof try to retrieve a kite with a length of pipe. The boy could almost reach it. For several minutes almost appeared to be a sufficient reason to keep leaning farther, until an older person came out and took the pipe away.

The kite stayed caught.

Rudra found, to his surprise, that he was relieved.

When Sitara answered his later message, she had checked Dhruv's next free interval with him. He was willing to hear Ram's account the following morning, after his leg care, for a short call. Vedant's direction could wait to be discussed until that account had actually reached the second person Ram had permitted him to ask.

Rudra accepted the interval.

The phone went dark before the light on the roof did.

---

Dhruv listened on the hundred-and-forty-fourth morning with his leg resting where the worker had put it.

Rudra first gave him the part of the bank he had not seen. The stretcher's fall. The elbow and back. The decision to end Unbound: Pataal before the crew transferred him. He did not make the form sound like the reason the injuries should be smaller.

"Abhi bhi pakad kamzor hai?" Dhruv asked.

"Haan."

The answer did not disappoint him into changing the subject. He asked whether Rudra wanted to stop before beginning Ram's account. Rudra said he could manage the agreed few minutes, and asked again whether Dhruv wished to hear it.

He did.

The name sounded less impossible on another deliberate telling. Kaal. Ram's discovery during the fight with Adhira six years before Rudra's birth, the same hand behind Aegis Meridian, the basic balance among seven Lokas already failing. Kaal wanted it destroyed. Rudra kept the timing and the speaker attached. His father did not know whether stopping that hand would repair the failure.

"Aur us aadmi ko Papa nahi pehchaan paaye," he added. "Bank waale ko."

"Us khaali jagah mein naam bharne ki jaldi mat karna," Dhruv said.

Rudra had almost expected a second revelation. Instead he heard the scrape of an ordinary chair being brought nearer to Dhruv, and his teacher thanking the person who had moved it.

"Vedant ne Swarg kaha," Rudra said. "Jahan santulan sambhaalne waalon se poochh sakein."

"Jagah theek sujhaayi. Wahan pahunchna aur sahi sunne waale tak pahunchna alag kaam honge."

Dhruv did not claim a repair he had performed or an audience he could command. Asked about the old passage, he said Vedant and Sitara could discuss the approach they knew. His own leg was not going on an inspection because he had been included in a call.

"Main yahan se poochhne mein madad karoonga. Chalne ka kaam kisi aur ki haan se hoga."

"Aapko bura nahi lagega?"

"Meri taang ko achha lagega. Uska bhi vote hai."

Rudra smiled. The call ended at the time they had agreed.

---

Sitara heard Vedant's route suggestion from him that afternoon, after he had chosen the interval and she had finished her hand care. Rudra was not required to relay a description of a bend he had never seen.

There was an older natural way, separate from the damaged approach to Pataal. Familiarity with its existence was not a report on its present floor. Neither offered to prove otherwise that evening.

Vedant asked what walking she was currently allowed. She asked the same about his arm and balance. Their hands could not become supports for an empty chair merely because there was no patient in it yet.

They agreed to seek their own reviews and return with findings, rather than choose a date which their bodies would then be expected to obey.

Rudra heard that much through the reply they permitted Sitara to send. There was no picture of a gate, no new map for Ira to examine, no demand that the four friends begin packing.

He asked the nurse whether the hair on the other side was sticking up again.

"Haan."

"Theek hai. Kal doosri side karenge."

She left the comb beside the folded cloth.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini watched a sparrow try to carry a piece of string longer than its body.

The loose end caught against the balcony rail. The bird pulled, stopped, hopped nearer and took the string farther down. It rose only a little before the end caught again.

Rukmini's fingers had tightened on her sleeve. She let them open.

Her shoulder rested against its support. The phone contained the last update she had actually received, not the account of this morning's separate calls. She did not reach into its silence for an omen. When the person helping with her care asked whether the curtain should be moved, she shook her head.

The sparrow abandoned the string.

A little later it returned for a shorter piece.

She stayed watching until her own next appointment required her to turn away.

---

The first week did not become a week of waiting beside a packed bag.

On day one hundred and forty-seven, Rudra spent the permitted sitting interval looking at a college form somebody had sent to his phone. The deadline had not learned where he had been hurt. A box wanted a signature, another wanted an attachment, and a third offered too little space for an explanation which included a damaged ambulance.

He asked for the ordinary medical certificate instead.

The worker enlarged the part he needed to check. He dictated a request for an extension without putting a realm into it. Then the phone was set aside and the next meal came.

The following morning, the treating team discussed moving him into the rehabilitation ward. The urgent observation had stayed settled; the need for assisted transfers, joint protection and follow-up had not ended. They showed him the proposed care schedule and the charges for the first two weeks before anybody treated the new bed as occupied.

Rudra spoke to Ram about that actual amount, after asking whether he could discuss money now. They checked what was available without assigning the old vehicle's unsettled damage account to it. Ram agreed to the care payment. Rudra heard the transfer acknowledgment later that day.

The ward accepted the care plan and the payment before his bed was moved.

---

The move was along a corridor, not across a city.

A receiving nurse checked the written instructions with the worker who had brought him. Supports, changed grip, pain with breathing, the old exertional faint for which a complete explanation was still missing. His phone and the borrowed charger travelled in a bag he saw opened at the new bed. The people who had wheeled him there left only after the new team accepted him.

The room had a dented cupboard and a window which opened farther.

Rudra chose the cupboard shelf he could see from the bed. They put his clothes there. Choosing did not make him able to reach them, but he discovered that knowing where they were mattered more than he had expected.

On the hundred-and-fiftieth day, the therapist raised the head of the bed and waited while his body caught up with the change. The first transfer to the supported chair used her instructions and two workers, not his recovering wrist under the mattress.

He sat long enough to notice how loudly somebody was peeling an orange outside.

Then the back began pulling. He reported it, and they returned him before the orange was finished.

The next attempt was shorter by design. It went better.

---

There were changes which did not announce themselves as victories.

The scrape on his palm closed. The elbow's outline slowly became less unfamiliar, though sustained grip still weakened and the last part of the movement remained painful. Breathing quietly ceased to occupy so much of his attention. A cough could still find the sore place with cruel accuracy.

The ankle required its support. The knee could not yet be trusted with the turn his mind completed before his foot began it. During the third week's supervised work, a permitted rise pulled sharply enough that the therapist stopped the next repetition. The clinician reviewed him that afternoon. The day's work changed; the question of travel did not buy an exemption.

He did not call the colour back to carry him through the rest.

By the following week, he could spend longer out of bed and make the short assisted transfer without either hand taking his body's weight. The workers stayed in their places. Afterward they examined the leg and asked about the back rather than measuring success by how pleased he sounded.

One afternoon he managed to feed himself part of the meal with the adapted position they had shown him. He gave up the spoon before his grip gave it up for him.

The remainder tasted no different when somebody helped.

---

Veer sent a complaint about the food at his own place, and then, because Rudra asked, an honest account of the shoulder.

The ribs were easier. The knee accepted more ordinary walking with pauses. A loaded reach still pulled through the shoulder; his review had not turned him into available carrying strength. He was tired of people assuming that being broad meant being repaired.

"Patla ho ja," Rudra suggested.

"Tumhaari advice sunke khud hi kamzor pad jaaoonga."

Aadhya's hands had a slower story. Some coverings became smaller after examination. The right thumb and index still required her eyes to check what feeling could not reliably tell her. She had learned how much she could do with a page or a spoon and how differently those tasks behaved from each other.

Ira heard their messages after her eye intervals. The ordinary right eye tolerated a little more daylight now; bright glare still shortened it. The left remained covered. She told Rudra she wanted a description of Swarg, if they reached it, which included where someone could sit without staring at a white wall.

"Bas sundar mat bol dena."

"Main architecture ka viva nahi de raha."

"Phir toh pass ho sakte ho."

They let the joke occupy the call instead of converting it into a pledge.

---

The next care payment was discussed before the first fortnight ran out. Ram confirmed the bounded second period, and the ward acknowledged receiving it. The longer account remained something to review, not permission to keep charging an invisible future.

For the inspection, the friends checked the small amounts they could spare. Rudra asked Ram separately about the shortfall. An agreed contribution arrived and was acknowledged; the sum set aside covered the quoted inspection and kept a separate return reserve untouched. It did not settle the ambulance account, buy four passages or hire anyone for an indefinite stay.

Vedant and Sitara had their own examinations before choosing the hundred-and-seventieth day.

His finger sensation was still altered, but ordinary walking and turning without loading that forearm had been observed and allowed. Her covered hand was kept out of lifting; the walking interval they proposed fitted her current review. Neither was offering power as insurance against a floor which failed inspection.

They hired two uninjured attendants for the empty-chair check and ordinary equipment, explaining the unfamiliar destination, the lack of assured phone contact beyond the approach and the right to stop. No patient would be sent ahead to discover whether the route worked.

One attendant asked whether leaving at the first uncomfortable finding would reduce his fee.

"Nahi," Sitara said. "Ussi kaam ke paise hain."

Both accepted the limited job. The hired vehicle had five belted places including the driver and room for the folded chair. Its booking ran from eight until two, with no more than two hours waiting at the road. The driver would stay on Earth and leave with them on the agreed return, not guard an entrance forever.

Dhruv kept the departure and return details beside his own care times.

Nobody packed Rudra's clothes.

---

They left at eight on the hundred-and-seventieth morning.

The attendants folded the chair themselves and secured it in the luggage space. Vedant and Sitara took the rear seats, neither wedged against equipment which would have to be caught on a bend. The driver checked all four passengers before moving.

The road left Mirzapur behind in pieces: the last crowded shopfront, a row of repair sheds, fields beginning where somebody's unfinished wall had stopped. A tractor delayed them at a narrow crossing. Sitara watched the driver wait for it instead of manufacturing time by passing badly.

They reached the agreed stopping place before half past nine.

A low ridge rose beyond the road. The stone opened along a natural break, broad at the foot, narrowing where the faces leaned towards one another. Scrub moved in the light outside it. This was not the damaged mouth at which the ambulance had been struck.

Sitara called Dhruv from beside the vehicle. He heard the actual arrival and the latest return time. The driver had the ordinary contact too; a missed return meant making that call, not following strangers into rock.

The attendants waited at the opening while Vedant and Sitara made the first walking inspection.

They took their time over the shallow rise. There was grit on the firm floor and a tighter turn which Vedant remembered, but memory did not get to flatten the small uneven place before it. He stopped there, looked from both directions and asked Sitara to check the space beside him without using her hurt hand against the wall.

They passed it on their feet. They would bring back what they had found, not a story about how courage made a narrow place wide.

---

At the second bend, the air smelled of wet stone.

Sitara tried the call they had agreed to test. It did not connect. She tried the other phone without moving farther to chase a bar. That failed too. Vedant spoke towards the first turn; the reply from the attendant became indistinct, then disappeared when rock came between them.

They returned to the last place where a sentence could be heard plainly and checked it again. Beyond that point, a person would have to walk back to carry a message. No one would be promised a call through the passage.

Then they continued together.

The far opening came without a latch or anything which asked to be awakened. Light lay across the floor. Vedant waited while Sitara looked at the last shallow slope, then both stepped out.

Swarg was not a ceiling full of clouds.

There was ground under her ordinary shoes, pale and fine-grained, and a paved way broad enough for people to pass without turning sideways. Beyond it rose terraces edged with flowering shrubs. Water fell somewhere she could hear but not yet see. White surfaces gathered the daylight and returned it softened beneath long stretches of hanging cloth.

A woman carried folded lengths of that cloth across the paving. Two children followed her with shorter bundles and a dispute about whose piece had been dropped.

For a few seconds Sitara simply listened.

The woman told the children to watch where they were going. They did not fall silent because visitors had appeared.

Vedant looked back at the opening before looking farther into the realm.

They walked only to the nearest broad resting place. There was a shaded bench with a clear turning space beside it, and, across the small court, an open window before which people were waiting with papers.

"Sawaal dena ho toh?" Sitara asked the woman when she passed nearer.

She pointed towards that window without putting her bundle down.

It was a lead, not an appointment with a Devta. They did not join the waiting people today. Neither phone connected from beside the bench; Sitara checked before they left it.

---

The return required the slope in the other direction.

They walked it, stopped at the uneven place again, checked the two bends from this side and came out to the attendants waiting in Earth light. Sitara's next call connected beside the vehicle. She told Dhruv that both of them were back.

They drank, rested and reviewed the finding with the two men before asking whether they wished to make the chair check. The men could now ask the people who had actually walked the passage what had changed beyond the first turn.

Both chose to continue within the remaining time.

This time the attendants handled the unfolded chair. Vedant walked ahead of the wheels where he could be seen. Sitara stayed alongside without putting her covered hand on the handles.

The chair negotiated the tighter bend slowly. Its foot supports needed a different angle; they stopped to change it rather than lift a wheel and declare the problem solved. On the far side, the men turned it beside the shaded bench, set the brakes and tested that the seat could be approached without stepping backwards over an edge.

Nobody sat in it to turn the inspection into an unplanned patient trip. Empty wheels established room, not how Rudra would tolerate a loaded journey.

They came back through the same bends with the supports in the usable position. The attendants folded the chair by the car. Vedant checked the return opening once more from the road side before taking his seat.

They were leaving before eleven.

The driver did not have to decide whether two hours meant until somebody's curiosity was satisfied.

---

The vehicle returned to the agreed pickup point in Mirzapur while the afternoon still lay ahead of it.

The attendants unloaded the chair and checked it with the hiring worker: wheels, brakes, altered foot-support position, no damage concealed beneath a folded seat. Sitara paid the quoted vehicle amount from the inspection money and heard the driver's acknowledgment. The attendants received the full agreed fee for their accepted work. The unused part of the booking did not become a debt to the next trip.

One of them said he would consider another booking after seeing the actual care plan. The other wanted to check his dates. Neither answer was written down as a permanent promise.

Their car left. The two men took their own bags and went away.

Vedant gave Dhruv the final return report before going for his own rest. Sitara had her covered hand checked at the interval already due. The passage had not hurt it again; that was a finding for today, not a reason to begin carrying things with it.

That evening, Rudra heard that the inspection was finished. The fuller account would fit after the next morning's care, when the people who had done it could speak without hurrying through their own needs.

He slept badly for an ordinary reason. The man in the next bed snored in short, convincing bursts of silence and then changed his mind.

Rudra asked for the door to be drawn nearer closed.

For once, anticipation was not the only thing keeping him awake.

---

Vedant and Sitara gave their account together on the hundred-and-seventy-first morning. Rudra listened from the supported chair. The interval was chosen; they had asked whether he wanted the practical finding before the view.

"Pehle raasta."

They had gone in and come back themselves, then taken the empty chair through with the attendants and returned again. The narrower turn required a particular angle and a foot-support adjustment. The floor beyond it had a shallow slope in both directions, not a staircase which somebody would have to carry him up. There was a bench and room to turn at the first court.

Rudra asked about the return when everybody was tired.

They had checked that direction separately. The work still needed suitable hands; neither his wrist nor Vedant's forearm had been counted among them. The route finding did not decide how long his back could tolerate sitting or whether the adjusted supports would suit his joints. Those belonged in the next assessment.

Beyond the first bend, voice could not be relied upon. Calls had failed inside and at the far bench. On Earth, both arrival and return calls had connected beside the vehicle. A visit would have to carry its immediate care and its means of getting back, not depend upon somebody hearing a shout from the wrong side of stone.

"Aur wahan?" Rudra asked.

Sitara told him about the hanging cloth and the children arguing beneath it. She described the shrubs on the terraces, the sound of water she had not followed, and the pale paving which made the shaded space matter.

"Kisi ne roka nahi?"

"Us chhote hisse mein nahi. Humne poora Swarg nahi dekha."

He tried to imagine being ordinary enough in another realm for children to quarrel past him.

The thought pleased him more than a welcome would have done.

---

There was also a real place to begin asking: the open window the cloth-carrying woman had indicated, across the first court. They had seen people bringing papers there. They had not asked for private records, named Ram, repeated the account about Kaal or claimed that a Devta was expecting Rudra.

"Toh bas udhar jaake khade nahi hona hai," he said.

"Tum khade hone ka kaam chhod bhi sakte ho," Sitara replied.

He glanced at the chair beneath him and laughed.

They finished with the limits of the inspected line, rather than letting the beauty swallow them. Rudra asked permission to give these findings to the three friends and the clinician considering his ordinary function. Both agreed. A short account went to the friends after the call, offered for listening when ready. Ira received the shade she had asked about, not a photograph she would have to strain to interpret.

The clinician's discussion was arranged for that afternoon.

---

The time between the report and the appointment was full of little things which had existed before Swarg acquired a route.

There was a meal. The next dressing check. A worker who had finished her shift came to retrieve the cardigan she had left on the chair, and Rudra had to wait for the next person before changing position. They had handed over his care, not promised that the same hands would remain until his story was ready to leave them.

He asked for the comb again.

This time, from his supported position, he could see the mirror somebody held.

He looked younger than the people at the road would have described him. Tired. Irritated at his own hair. Nineteen, with a mouth which still became stubborn when someone asked a question twice.

The clinician arrived with the therapist after their arranged work, and Sitara joined by the short call she had accepted. They had the route finding. They also had the record of his actual weeks, including the stopped rise and the grip which still failed with sustained effort.

Rudra waited until the clinician had examined the ankle and asked about the back.

Then he put the two accounts together without asking either to erase the other.

"Agar main ladne nahi, sirf sawaal poochhne jaaun—toh kya is safar ka tareeqa mere liye ban sakta hai?"

# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-TWO — THE SEATS THEY CHOSE

---

> *"Nine of us went beyond the last working phone.*
> *That was not the same as nine people able to lift."*
> *— Ira's later register, first Swarg visit*

---

"Ban sakta hai," the clinician said. "Shartein poori hui toh. Sirf baat karne ka iraada tumhaari peeth ko safar se chhutti nahi dega."

Rudra looked at the therapist. She had not brought an exercise sheet shaped like a ticket.

They discussed a short inquiry visit, not a move into another realm. He would need the supported position which the vehicle could actually provide, an observed trial of the ordinary journey's demands, help that did not depend on his injured friends and a return before the next care was due. The inspected line had no compulsory stairs. Its tighter turn still had to accept the foot position his body needed.

Nobody at this bedside could certify an unfamiliar world harmless.

"Wahan se phone nahi laga," Sitara said through the speaker.

The clinician asked her to repeat where contact had worked, how far the chair had gone, and what getting back would require if somebody could no longer walk comfortably. Then she asked Rudra whether he understood that immediate help there would be only the help they took with them.

He said what he understood instead of saying that he was not frightened.

The appointment produced a sequence of tasks, not a departure date. Sitara's call ended before her next work, and the therapist adjusted the plan for the coming days.

Rudra stayed in the chair a little longer, looking at the cupboard shelf he had chosen.

For the first time, leaving it was work someone was willing to help him consider.

---

On day one hundred and seventy-four, they tried the foot-support position on the actual reclining transport chair.

The ankle stayed supported. The knee did not have to fold into the narrower turn; the chair's approach and the support's outward angle changed together. Rudra reported the pressure at the heel before the trial began. A worker changed the padding, and the therapist checked again before anyone rolled him through a measured turn in the ward.

Two uninjured workers handled the movement. His hands stayed where they had been put.

The first stop was deliberate. So was the return.

He was accustomed to noticing a floor's defects as things a body should overcome. From this seat, a raised strip of metal at the doorway belonged equally to the person controlling the wheel. One worker slowed, told him what was coming and took the chair over it without tipping his back into a surprise.

Afterwards they checked the ankle, knee and sore elbow. The grip had not been tested by asking him to save himself.

He had travelled the length of a corridor without making it a training ground.

---

The longer sitting trial was less flattering.

Halfway through the interval, the ache spread along the place beside his spine. Rudra wanted to wait until the therapist returned, to report it after completing the number everyone could see on the clock.

He called the worker instead.

They stopped the trial, changed the supported position and had him reviewed. The next attempt would be shorter and the road plan would need a real pause, with space for the chair to recline while stationary. They did not erase the failed interval because he had already managed the doorway.

That evening he sent Veer a message saying the chair was better at corners than he was at time.

"Toh chair ko class representative bana do," Veer replied.

Rudra laughed with the phone propped beside him, then let himself be annoyed for the rest of the evening.

He could want to go without pretending the setback was secretly a gift.

---

The second funded fortnight of care was coming to its end. The ward gave the next proposed period and charges before it did. Rudra and Ram discussed a bounded extension through the planned reassessments and return care; payment arrived and was acknowledged. There was still no departure to pay for merely because another week had been paid for here.

On day one hundred and seventy-nine, after further ordinary work and a fresh examination, Rudra made the short road trial with the clinical team arranged for it.

This was a new hired run with its own driver and end time. The chair was secured in an accessible vehicle, its occupant restraint checked separately. A qualified worker travelled beside him. At the agreed stopping place they waited, adjusted his position as planned and asked about symptoms before continuing the return.

The journey actually brought him back to the ward. The agreed vehicle-and-worker charge was paid through the booking contact and acknowledged; the driver left. The travelling worker handed the current findings to the receiving nurse and ended that run.

There had been no faint, white-edged sight or new breathlessness. The back ached within the position changes they had used; the joints needed their supports afterwards. The next morning's review included that ache, not just the absence of an alarm.

The clinician considered the finding with him.

A limited visit with a rest stop and suitable help was now something they could plan. Fighting, walking the whole way or lifting somebody else was not hidden inside that sentence.

Rudra asked that the actual finding, including the stop, go to his friends.

---

Aadhya had already asked her own clinician about the walking, the road and the hours between hand care.

She did not arrive at the conversation with permission borrowed from Rudra. The coverings were lighter, the skin reviewed, the altered feeling still present. She could make the proposed ordinary walking interval with rest; carrying, force through the protected hands and treating somebody else were not part of it.

"Main tumhaari first-aid bag nahi hoon," she told Rudra when the six-person audio call began.

"Bag ka zip toh khul jaata hai."

"Phir bag se baat kar lena."

He grinned towards the propped phone. Vedant let the joke finish before giving the actual proposal.

The hundred-and-eighty-fourth day, if the final findings held. A short first visit, on the already inspected line, no more than ninety minutes beyond the last reliable phone point including the walking both ways. Earlier return for anybody's care or request. A clinical worker with them, another on Earth, two suitable attendants, no injured friend assigned a handle.

Those people still had to accept those jobs. The proposal would not become a booking by being repeated confidently enough.

"Devta se usi din baat hogi?" Veer asked.

"Pakka nahi," Vedant said. "Pehla kaam us jagah par apna sawaal pahunchana hai jahan se sahi sunne waale tak jaa sake."

"Matlab queue."

"Ho sakti hai."

"Swarg mein bhi. Achha."

Rudra heard Ira laugh once, quietly, and then ask for her phone to be moved out of the glare.

---

Veer's own trial had been a walk out and back with a seat where his knee needed one, not a march in which the last person to sit lost.

The shoulder remained restricted under load. The ribs had allowed a fuller breath without the old catch during his review; discomfort at the end of a longer effort still needed reporting. He could accompany the proposed visit as a passenger and a walker with pauses, not its emergency carrying plan.

"Tumko mujhe uthaana nahi hai," Rudra said.

"Pata hai."

"Bas bol raha hoon."

"Mujhe bhi wahan dekhna hai. Har jagah tumhaare peeche bhaagne nahi aata main."

There was enough irritation in it for Rudra to hear the affection without being asked to thank him for it.

He was making fun of himself. She heard it and let him.

"Tumhe apne waale din kya achha laga tha?" she asked.

Rudra considered giving her the view.

He could describe it. The pale paving, the water coming into light, the flowers which had looked almost insulting after the chair was told to move. Those were his own recollections. He did not have to borrow Aadhya's eyes for them.

"Haan. Bas utna bhi."

"Woh tumne bataaya."

"Galat cheez bhi wahin hui."

"Woh bhi suna."

He felt the defence loosen. She was not making him prosecute the whole place before he could admit he had liked sitting beneath its cloth.

The nurse at his end said there were two minutes left of the eight both sides had agreed.

"Aapne khaaya?" he asked his mother.

She had. She asked what he had left on his plate. He objected to the assumption, then admitted one thing, and she said she was not going to send instructions about vegetables through two care teams.

"Aap bhej sakti ho," he said. "Woh log meri side lenge."

"Tumhe lagta hai."

He could hear her smile.

Their ordinary conversation had a shape he knew well: meal, sleep, the shirt which was easier to change with help, the reminder that an easier hour did not cancel the next care. He had often mistaken that shape for the whole of her. Motherhood had made her familiar before he had thought to ask whether familiarity was the same as knowledge.

She knew the child who had come in dusty from the courtyard. She had already answered for what she had seen and failed to stop there; this pleasant call had not taken that answer back.

He knew which spoon she disliked and how she looked at him when he said he had eaten outside.

There was so much before those things which he had never asked her to describe.

---

His father's earlier name had once seemed to put Ram at an impossible distance.

The distance had become less simple after the telling: a baby sent away, people who had raised him, a man who had fought before his son was born and still brought fear into a home which needed more than his victories. Learning that history had not made the courtyard harmless. It had made the father more than the courtyard.

Rudra had let his mother remain almost entirely inside the house.

Part of that had been how she chose to speak. Part had been the habit of a child for whom a mother's past was merely a longer version of waiting for him to arrive.

He looked towards the cupboard, then away. There was no question to be routed through a window. No form to open, no right paper he could insist must already contain her.

The man with the bucket had a life which was not his to summon. His mother had one which he could ask to hear. Those were not the same relationship, and he did not need to make them the same to notice how often he had filled a silence with the role someone performed for him.

He wanted to ask without making the asking sound like an accusation.

Or a demand to be reassured that nothing important existed before their family.

---

"Ma," he said.

"Haan."

He could have asked what she thought of the hearing, whether she was frightened for him, when she would be well enough to come. There were real questions there. None was the one he had just discovered he wanted.

Her water was near. His body was supported. There was a little time left, and the people who would help them end the call had not been asked to disappear.

He thought of how readily he could picture the kitchen in Varanasi and how little he knew of where she had been a child. He did not picture a hidden room, a secret sign or a face waiting to recognise his. He had no new evidence about her in the folded cloth or in his own blood.

He had a mother who had listened to him describe a place.

Before the rest of the interval could become another practical question about tomorrow, he asked the ordinary thing he had somehow never made ordinary enough to say.

"Aap bachpan mein kahan rehti thi?"

Rudra heard the answer without anything arriving behind it: no time, no request for a vehicle, no instruction that he should become well enough to make it happen.

"Jahan se nikaala gaya, wahaan jaana chaah sakti hoon," she continued. "Iska matlab yeh nahi ki yahaan jo jeeya, woh chhodne ko keh rahi hoon."

He looked towards the window. The light there belonged to the afternoon they were actually in.

"Aaj ki baat nahi kar rahi," she said.

"Haan. Samajh raha hoon."

He was trying to. His mind had already reached for a practical difficulty before she finished the first sentence. How to take her, who would come, what a body still needing help could endure. He had nearly answered a wish with the beginning of a refusal made out of care.

She had not asked him to arrange her life.

"Bas dekhna hai?" he asked, and heard the small, careless word himself.

"Bas chhota kaam nahi hota," she said.

He waited while she found the rest of the answer.

"Woh mera ghar tha. Jo mere saath hua, woh bhi wahin hua. Main yaad karoon toh har baar sirf wahi yaad aaye, yeh nahi chahti."

The accusation remained false. The exile remained something done to her. She was not correcting either statement into a misunderstanding for the sake of a happier memory.

She wanted a birthplace which contained more than the act that had taken it away.

Rudra could understand wanting that, though he could not yet picture the place as she had known it. His own time beneath its cloth was not a borrowed childhood. The court he remembered had no obligation to contain the right corner of her house, no right to become a map of everything she had lost.

"Aur yahaan?" he asked.

"Yahaan bhi mera ghar hai."

The answer was firm, not soothing.

"Main yeh nahi keh raha tha ki aap—"

"Pata hai. Bata rahi hoon."

She let him stop explaining. For a little while they were quiet together, with the phone supported at both ends and no journey beginning in the silence.

The accepted eight-minute interval reached its end.

Rudra wanted to keep her voice near, but he did not bargain for another answer under the name of saying goodbye. His mother said she needed the next rest. He said that he did too. They ended the call, and the people waiting to help did not have to interrupt a second beginning.

The nurse asked about his present comfort before changing the position. He gave the back's familiar feeling, the weak hand's limit, the absence of new dizziness. The old faint still had no explanation supplied by these quiet days. Through the movement his elbow and wrist were protected; afterwards the ankle and knee needed their ordinary supports.

He was tired in a way which did not require a crisis to justify it.

The phone went away. He lay with the answer and let it be smaller than a plan, larger than a passing remark.

His mother wanted something before he could be useful to it.

He had spent so many years loving through usefulness that the order felt unfamiliar.

In Varanasi, Rukmini asked for the support to be checked before settling.

Dadi came when called, heard the present shoulder pain and helped within the current care. The phone was put aside, the water left where the next help could be given without a hurried reach. Rukmini did not need to remain available to discuss Swarg because she had finally said she wanted to see it again.

She closed her eyes.

The wish had survived being spoken. It had not acquired a date. For this afternoon, that was enough.

At the other end of the finished call, Rudra slept after a while.

He woke before supper to a small argument beyond the curtain about where a chair had been left.

Somebody needed the way through. Somebody else thought it had been clear enough. The chair was moved, the argument lost its subject, and the room returned to the ordinary sounds of work.

He thought of his mother's answer again.

There were things which could be put right by moving what stood in another person's way. There were things which could not be made whole by a son lifting them, even if his hands had been sound.

He did not have to know the difference perfectly tonight.

When the worker came to ask about supper, he answered about supper. He ate with the help he needed, then let the evening continue without asking anyone to fetch the hearing papers or telephone a mentor about a possible departure.

No one in the room had been offered that job.

Day two hundred and four began with the review, breakfast and an argument Rudra lost to the position of a page.

He wanted it a little farther left. The nurse pointed out what his back was doing when he tried to follow it there. They moved the stand instead. He admitted the new angle was better, which did not stop him feeling briefly annoyed that even a small preference needed negotiation with a body.

After the chosen short reading, he asked to stop.

The page stayed unfinished without becoming the day's failure.

Elsewhere in the facility, Aadhya had a different small thing to finish.

The loose thread on the blue scarf had begun catching on the fabric when it was unfolded. After her hand care, she asked the attendant who was helping with the clothes whether an ordinary repair could be arranged without her going out for it.

Aadhya agreed. Her own money, one edge, no extra work added because the rest of the scarf could also be made more perfect.

She let the feeling pass without giving it the scarf.

The new stitches were small and visible. The woman showed the repaired part, ran the edge lightly through her fingers to check that nothing caught and waited for Aadhya to say whether it was the job she had asked for.

It was.

The attendant helped with the ordinary payment. The woman received the full quoted amount, acknowledged it, put away the needle and her spare thread, and left with the collection. Aadhya asked for the scarf to be folded where she could see the new edge once before it went into the bag.

It looked like her scarf.

Not a souvenir from Swarg, not an object which had become wiser because of where it had been carried. A length of blue cloth with one part made less likely to catch on itself.

She liked the visible stitch more than she had expected to.

Ira heard part of a radio programme that morning through the ordinary ward sounds.

Somebody farther away kept answering a question before the person on the radio had finished asking it. The answer was wrong. The next one was wrong too, delivered with the same undamaged confidence.

Ira smiled with her uncovered eye closed.

She did not need to record who had won, look for a pattern in the guesses or turn pleasure into work merely because she had been trained to notice things. When the volume became too much, she asked for it to be lowered. It was.

The left covering stayed in place. Later, when she used the ordinary right eye for the brief part allowed in her own day, the room remained a room, with its familiar glare limits and a reason to close the eye again before comfort ran out.

There was no message from Rudra waiting to make her the chronicler of his mother's private life.

She could have an unimportant morning and keep it.

He had time to think before they spoke.

For today, though, listening was an action she had actually chosen with him.

He let that be the beginning, not the whole of what he would ever be able to offer.

When the worker asked whether he wanted the page brought back while they waited, he considered it and said no. He wanted a little time with nothing to finish. The refusal belonged to this afternoon; it did not require him to promise that he would read twice as much tomorrow.

"Haan."

He had been preparing to apologise for a whole realm.

"Baithkar bol paaye?" she asked.

"Haan."

It was a mother's question in a conversation which had almost become too large for one. He had been so intent on the question he had taken there that he had forgotten the relief of not needing to stretch his back merely to be visible.

She said she was glad.

He could tell her something about Swarg without becoming the keeper of her memories there. She could receive it without pretending that being born in a place made every present person in it her acquaintance.

For a while, that exchange felt easier than either account alone.

He asked whether wanting to go back made her feel disloyal to the life she had here.

The question came carefully this time. He was not asking for a reassurance about himself before she could answer.

"Kabhi apne ko samjhaana padta hai," she said. "Ki yahaan ka pyaar rakhkar wahaan ki yaad aa sakti hai. Aur wahaan jo galat hua, use galat kehkar bhi us jagah ki yaad aa sakti hai."

She had not taken the words to make herself innocent. The old mornings remained part of their house. So did the meals, the laughter, the particular way somebody could call his name from another room and expect him to understand everything inside it.

He could want a home without pretending it had never hurt him.

His mother could too.

Someone had moved a stack so another person could speak. The act had not answered everything. It had made an answer possible to ask for.

Rudra had not heard the programme. He did not need a reconstruction of its questions to understand the argument. Veer was defending the right to be confidently wrong; Ira was enjoying that confidence without needing to rescue it.

"Aaj bas baat karni thi," Rudra answered.

"Toh kar rahe hain."

It was an oddly generous reply because it contained no promise to make conversation useful.

Both of them missed being able to go somewhere because the thought had occurred to them.

Later, he thought of the absence of Aadhya's voice.

There had been room for her in the invitation, and room for her refusal in the actual morning. The call had not become a lesser thing because one of the four had chosen something else. Nobody had been made to recover in public so the group could look whole.

He wanted that kind of room around his mother's answer too.

Not the right to speak only when everybody was ready to hear the same thing, or to want home only once wanting could be made convenient. A real choice included the inconvenient possibility that another person would not choose the version which kept his own life easiest to picture.

This time he did not rehearse an offer.

He wanted to listen to the part of her life which did not become his simply because he had finally learned it existed.

"Kaam ki baat hui?" she asked.

"Bas company thi. Bore hone mein bhi company ho sakti hai."

"Phone set karne ki. Bore main apni marzi se ho sakta hoon."

His mother laughed softly. He was glad he could give her something which did not require her past to be made pleasant before she was allowed to enjoy the present. He was describing his own morning, not forwarding his friends' words. He had told no part of her private account either.

"Tum hase?" she asked.

"Dhyaan se. Check bhi hua."

"Achha."

He could hear the ordinary satisfaction in that last word. She had not needed him miserable through every interval in order to believe he had listened to her.

He was less afraid to ask now, which made the remaining fear easier to notice.

He had begun to hear the people in his mother's account as family: his mother, her sister, the father whose title he had only just learned. The closeness of the relations kept making the story intimate. Intimacy, in turn, tempted him to make it small enough to fit inside a house.

He said some of that awkwardly.

"Behen bola toh mere dimaag mein ghar ke andar ki baat ban jaati hai. Jaise log naraaz hue hon, baat bigdi ho. Par aapko ghar se jaana pada."

"Nikaala gaya," she said.

"Haan."

He accepted the correction without telling her he had meant the same thing. He had been using the easier phrase because it did not require him to keep another person's action in the sentence.

She had not chosen a different life after an unpleasant family evening. The home had been taken from her through accusations she had told him were false. He could keep the account private without reducing it to a quarrel which a son ought to be able to settle by asking everyone to behave better.

He could also stop looking for a childhood anecdote which would make her sister understandable before he had properly heard what the sister had done.

"Main unki taraf se bhi kuch sochne lagta hoon," he admitted. "Bina kuch jaane. Ki shayad—"

He stopped before the imagined excuse reached his mother.

Rukmini waited, then said, "Samajhna chaahte ho toh poochh sakte ho. Jo maine tumhe bataaya hai, use pehle se galat maan kar mat poochhna."

"Main aapko galat nahi bol raha."

"Theek. Toh jhoothe aarop ko jhootha rehne do. Baaki sawaal uske baad bhi hain."

He had been preparing, again, for an argument which she had not demanded. She was asking for her answer to remain an answer while the rest of the history was still being told.

He said he would try.

It was a more honest reply than promising that love would make him incapable of misunderstanding her again.

He was still allowed to ask how something had happened, or what she had meant by a word. He could still question the decisions she had made about him. She had not bought his silence by telling him about somebody else's cruelty. What she wanted now was simpler and harder: that he not make an excuse for the person who had hurt her before he knew anything beyond the relationship. A sister could be loved, trusted, feared or missed. The word itself was not a defence. He had used the word mother to understand his own house for years; it had not answered every question there either.

The nurse asked a brief comfort question. Rudra answered, and Rukmini took the little pause for a sip of water with the help already near her. Neither of them needed to race the other person's care to keep the thread of a conversation.

He looked towards the cupboard while they waited.

There were actual papers there, recording an actual question carried to a hearing and the limited answer brought back. They had their own purpose. Nothing in the cupboard could turn his mother's spoken account into a court scene he had witnessed, or tell him the names she had not yet given.

He did not ask to bring them out.

When she was ready again, he said, "Main baat ko chhota bana raha tha. Shayad isliye ki phir samajhna aasaan lage."

"Aasaan hona zaroori nahi hai," she answered. "Saaf poochh sakte ho."

He could.

He had asked whether she wanted to go back, and she had answered. He had asked whether there was a real home for her here, and she had answered that too. Neither statement had made the exile harmless. Neither had made him responsible for reversing it before he could ask another question.

"Meri behen akeli nahi thi," Rukmini said.

Rudra let the sentence reach him without adding a roomful of faces to it.

An ally would not turn the sister's part into innocence. Nor did the existence of another person mean he could now fold every enemy he had heard about into the same account. He knew only that his mother's sentence had opened a space beside the person he had been picturing.

He wanted the name from her, not from a resemblance, a remembered colour or a guess made to keep his anger occupied.

There was still time in the accepted call. His mother had returned to the comfortable position and was willing to continue. He did not need to ask anyone else to speak in her place.

He kept the question within what she had just told him.

"Ma, us waqt aapki behen ke saath kaun tha?"

She was not asking him to forget what he was learning about her. She was giving him room not to spend every quiet hour proving that the knowledge hurt enough.

He had done that with his father's story too: tried to make the right intensity of feeling into a debt which could finally be paid. It had not brought back a dead person or repaired the courtyard mornings.

He told her, a little defensively, that he still had questions about being kept outside so much of their lives.

"Haan," she said. "Woh tumhaare hain. Mere saath galat hua tha, isliye tum unhe chhod do, yeh nahi keh rahi."

---

The ten minutes ended with both questions still alive.

They said goodbye. The connection closed before the next care; phones were put away, the finite setup help finished. Rukmini rested after her own check. Rudra gave his current answers, accepted the protected movement and did not reach towards the stand as it was moved aside.

There was a pull when the position changed. He reported it immediately, let the nurse attend to it and waited until the support was comfortable again. It did not become a new secret to preserve the good ending of a difficult call.

Afterwards he lay still and watched a patch of afternoon travel along the curtain.

He could ask somebody to sit with his anger. He did not have to make that person carry the story which had caused it.

The thought was modest enough that he nearly dismissed it. Then he considered how much damage he could do by choosing a larger one merely because it felt more equal to the size of the hurt.

He let the modest thought stay.

When the worker came to help with the water, he asked for that help and no other errand. There was no need to find a messenger for the private name. For now, it belonged where his mother had chosen to tell it.

After rest and a fresh comfort check, Rudra asked whether Aadhya wanted a short call that evening.

He gave the request its proper size. He was angry about things he was still understanding and did not want to explain the details. He wanted a little company, not someone to tell him what to do. If she did not have room for that, he could wait.

She chose four minutes after her own care and rest.

He accepted. The supported setup was agreed with the ward help, and when the call began both had answered the present questions their own people needed to ask. Aadhya's right thumb and index finger were not made reliable by her willingness to listen.

"Chaar minute," she said.

"Haan."

"Advice mere paas waise bhi kam hai aaj."

He smiled before he answered. "Inventory dekh li?"

"Khali dabba hai. Company hai."

---

For a few moments, he had nothing to say.

He was used to arriving at Aadhya with some part of himself arranged for her: a joke sharpened enough to hide the tremor in it, a practical question which would make the contact look necessary, a confession already edited into the version least likely to make her worry.

Tonight she had agreed to hear the unproductive beginning too.

"Gussa aa raha hai," he said at last. "Aur uske baad sochta hoon ki isse kuch hona chahiye."

"Abhi?"

"Bas hona chahiye. Warna jaise main—"

"Feel karne ka proof de rahe ho?"

He let the words settle.

"Shayad."

She did not identify the object of the anger or ask him to confirm a guess about his mother's private life. Knowing a birthplace and a name had not made her the recipient of every sentence which followed them.

"Mujhe dekhna tha scarf ka edge theek hua ya nahi," she said. "Main khud karne lag jaati toh bhi haath ko meri baat se farq nahi padta."

He looked towards his own supported wrist.

"Hua theek?"

"Haan. Karwaaya. Thoda alag blue hai."

"Pasand hai?"

"Haan. Yeh mat bolna ki tumko dekhna padega tab opinion doge."

He laughed softly enough to keep his back from objecting, then answered the nurse's brief check before he replied to Aadhya.

"Tera opinion kaafi hai."

---

She told him only what she wanted to tell: one edge repaired, the work accepted and paid for, the scarf back in her bag. The woman who had done it had left. There was no favour he needed to return on her behalf, no thing for him to inspect in order for the choice to become complete.

He liked hearing about a problem whose size had not changed while it was being solved.

Aadhya said she had nearly asked for the new thread to match perfectly, then decided she did not want to wait on perfection for that little thing. It had been her choice about an ordinary scarf, not a conclusion she was offering him about his family.

He was grateful she did not make the comparison for him.

"Abhi bhi gussa hai," he admitted.

"Hona band karwaane ke liye phone nahi kiya tha."

"Nahi."

"Toh theek."

There was no challenge in it. She had heard the feeling and the request he had actually made. She did not need him to arrive at gratitude, a plan or a better mood by the end of four minutes so she could believe she had been good company.

He wished he knew how to give that to himself as easily.

---

At the limit, Aadhya said she needed to stop.

Rudra thanked her for the time, not for solving anything. She said goodbye. The connection ended; both phones were put away and both took the next care and rest without a private second conversation being smuggled into a farewell message.

He had not spoken his mother's wish, the false framing, the sister or Daksh's name.

He had spoken his own anger. Somebody he loved had heard it and remained a person with a scarf, a hand which needed care and a four-minute limit. She had not become the container for everything he could not yet hold.

For a while he lay listening to the ordinary room.

His mother did not know every detail of the call and did not need to. She had allowed him to speak about his own feelings; he had chosen someone willing to listen. He had not passed one woman's story to another in order to make the two kinds of love easier for him to manage.

It was possible to leave people separate and still feel less alone.

---

After supper, he slept sooner than he expected.

The anger had not been discharged into a promise. The history had not become harmless. But there had been an interval in which it did not have to occupy every part of him, and nobody had accused him of forgetting.

He woke briefly when care was due, answered honestly and let himself settle again.

The next morning would have its own questions. It did not need to be borrowed tonight merely because he could imagine it.

Day two hundred and seven began without the name being spoken.

It had not gone away. Rudra knew that before the review began. He gave the nurse the actual night's account, including the brief waking for care, and the present state of the back and hand. The unanswered faint remained on its own uncertain side of the history; a family name could not be written into its cause.

Breakfast was followed by rest, and then he chose the page again.

He did not start at the beginning to make yesterday's failed effort look more diligent. He asked for the stand at the checked angle and found the sentence where his attention had left him.

The first line stayed. So did the second.

The third was difficult for a reason belonging to the third line. There was an argument in it which he did not quite agree with, and he wanted to know whether the next paragraph would defend it.

For a few minutes, he was a student irritated by a paragraph.

It was an unexpectedly complete occupation.

---

He stopped at the chosen limit instead of using interest as proof he could double it.

The worker who came to put the stand aside asked whether the page should be marked where he had reached. He said yes. A plain slip went between the sheets, and the reading was put away without needing his wrist to pinch the paper.

He could return to a thought without holding it in his hand all morning.

The realisation was so ordinary that he almost laughed. It was also the kind of help he had spent years making difficult to accept, as if another person touching the edge of a task meant it no longer belonged to him.

This task still did.

He had not inherited a different syllabus because his mother had been born elsewhere. He did not have to decide that the work was trivial in order to make her history important. There might be days when he could not read at all. This morning there had been a little of both lives in him, and neither had destroyed the other.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini asked Dadi to help sort the clean clothes already in the bag.

The request was small and specific. She wanted the softer cloth nearer the top for the next change, not a new bundle brought from home or an outing arranged to find one. Her shoulder stayed supported while Dadi took out the things which needed moving.

One piece had been folded so that its edge disappeared beneath another. Rukmini tried to explain which she meant by describing the colour, then realised there were two of the same colour in the open bag.

They looked at each other.

"Upar waala," Rukmini said.

"Abhi dono upar hain."

She laughed softly and pointed with the hand she could use without disturbing the support. Dadi lifted the right one. The brief adjustment was done, the rest of the bag closed, and the chair moved back out of the way before the next care.

Nothing had been discovered inside the clothes. No forgotten object arrived to prove the life she was telling her son about. She had simply wanted the next change to be less awkward.

It was good to want that too.

---

After Dadi went to her own rest, Rukmini lay looking at the familiar room.

She had given her son a name from a painful part of her life. He had not become able to mend that life by hearing it, and she had not become only the woman to whom the harm had been done.

He could still ask her how the night had been. She could still be annoyed by a fold of cloth or want the water before another question. There would be no dignity in making herself endlessly available to the telling merely because it mattered.

When the next check came, she said what hurt now and what support was comfortable. The account she was giving Rudra was not a reason to shorten the rest which followed.

She slept.

---

Rudra's request for a later call reached her after his own lunch and rest, not during hers.

She chose eight minutes that evening after the current care, if both reviews and comfort checks allowed. He accepted the limit. The ordinary help needed to set up the phones was agreed at each end; nobody was sent to fetch a family witness or stand by for an explanation of the past.

He spent the waiting time with the next small parts of his actual day.

A supported change of position. Water with help. The pause before the back was comfortable enough for him to stop thinking about it. He did not make a drill out of the pause or set himself a count to conquer it.

The new knowledge had reached a life which was already happening.

He thought of the plain slip in the page. The reading would still be where he had left it. His mother would remain his mother when the accepted conversation ended. A question did not vanish because he let it wait until the person he wanted to ask was ready to answer.

He could carry it into the evening without rehearsing a verdict.

He wanted to understand what had been hidden from him, and why the hiding had felt so complete.

The evening call began after both current checks.

Rukmini had rested and taken the care due before it. Rudra's back was comfortable in the supported position; the hand did not need to hold the phone. Neither had new symptoms to hide in order to earn the eight minutes they had chosen.

For the first part of the conversation, they spoke about the page.

He had read a little and disagreed with a sentence. His mother asked what it said. He tried to explain without reciting the whole thing, became more irritated with his own explanation than with the original argument, and heard her trying not to laugh.

"Ab aap bolo mat ki writer ko bhi poochh lo," he said.

"Nahi bol rahi. Pehle samajh toh aane do mujhe ki likha kya hai."

He began again.

It was good to be told he was being unclear about something which had no connection to a hidden birthright. He could fail to explain a paragraph in the ordinary way and try again without either of them fearing they had broken the new truth between them.

She understood the second version.

She still thought he might need to read the next part before being quite so certain the writer was wrong.

---

"Theek hai," he conceded. "Aage padhunga. Abhi nahi."

"Abhi main hoon."

There was no jealousy in it. Only a mother claiming the few minutes her son had asked to spend with her, while the page could wait where he had left it.

He looked towards the cupboard. The two hearing sheets were still where they belonged, untouched by this different conversation. He had not needed their authority to let her correct his explanation of a paragraph, or to let her account of exile remain hers.

"Aapko meri taraf se koi promise chahiye nahi?" he asked.

"Kis cheez ka?"

"Ki yeh sab sunke main—"

"Jo nahi samjhe, uska vaada mat karo."

The interruption was gentle enough to make the instruction harder to evade.

He had wanted to offer her a good ending before he knew the middle. His mother was not asking him to pretend he knew a road merely to make her feel less alone on it.

That did not mean every road she had chosen for him had been right.

---

He said that last part with more difficulty than he expected.

Not the metaphor, only the thing beneath it: he could hear what had been done to her and still want answers about what had been decided for him.

"Mujhe pata hai chhota tha," he said. "Bahut kuch aapko hi karna hota. Par phir main bada bhi hua."

"Haan," she answered.

"Bas itna bolne se bura lag raha hai. Jaise abhi aapki baat se apni taraf mod raha hoon."

"Ek baat poori zindagi ko chup nahi kar deti."

He let the reply come to rest.

Her pain did not demand that he forget the years in which he had asked badly, guessed wildly or stopped asking because the answer always seemed to be somewhere beyond the age he had reached. His questions did not require him to turn her exile into a story invented for sympathy.

Both could remain true in the same call.

---

He thought of Ram's voice on the earlier call from Varanasi, back when Rudra had still been in Prayagraj.

The father he had always known had named Pataal, named himself Daavansh, named the Asuran inheritance concealed in his son. The kavach had hidden that part too. Rudra had heard him say it; he did not need the newly learned name to make the old answer real.

At the time, the paternal origin had seemed large enough to account for every hidden thing.

It no longer occupied the whole space.

His mother had come from somewhere too. The seal was her act. He knew those things now without needing to imagine a birth he could not remember, or search his body for an image of the person she had been then.

He had a memory of being told about the lock, years of living with it and the actual loss of it. Those were not the same as remembering its making.

---

The nurse asked about the present position. He answered before continuing. Rukmini used the pause for her own comfort check, and both remained willing to speak within the time left.

The question taking shape in him was not how to restore the kavach. It was broken; nothing in this conversation had put it back. He did not reach beneath a dressing or ask his mother to try anything through the phone.

He wanted the purpose in the words of the person who had placed it.

Knowing the reason would not make the infant he had been into somebody who had agreed to it. It would not settle every later choice. But he had been listening to the story as if only one parent could have brought a hidden identity into his life.

Now he knew his mother's beginning was not contained by the kitchen in which she had finally spoken about the seal.

He could ask without supplying an answer for her. He could keep the question separate from a technique, a royal demand or a promise to go anywhere.

There was still time in the accepted call.

"Ma, jo kavach aapne mere janm par lagaaya tha—woh sirf Papa ki pehchaan chhupa raha tha, ya aapki taraf se mujh mein jo hai use bhi?"
