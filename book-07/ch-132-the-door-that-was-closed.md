# CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-TWO — THE DOOR THAT WAS CLOSED

---

> *"Being sent away was not the same as choosing to leave.*
> *Neither fact could choose her next home for her."*
> *— Ira's later register, beside a family history*

---

"Maine apni marzi se nahi chhoda tha," Rukmini said. "Mujhe Swarg se nikaala gaya tha."

Rudra waited. The phone remained supported between him and the answer.

"Meri apni behen ne mujh par jhoothe aarop lagaaye the. Woh nahi chahti thi ki main raani banoon. Mujhe nirvaasit kar diya gaya."

Her own sister.

The relation arrived before the enormity of the rest. He had barely begun to picture his mother as somebody's child. Now somebody's sister stood there too, made real by an act which had helped take that home from her.

"Raani?" he said.

"Mere pita Swarg ke Highest Lord the."

She gave him the relation and the title without a name or a claim that the title explained every person he had met in the court. He could hear the words plainly. They did not make the next breath easy to place.

"Aur jo aarop lagaaye the..."

"Jhoothe the," she said. "Jo mere khilaaf kaha gaya, woh sach nahi tha."

She did not make him supply the rest from a silence. This was not a departure whose reason she had merely forgotten to mention. She was telling him she had been wronged and forced out of the place in which she had grown up.

He had asked why she left.

The first honest correction was that leaving had not been her choice.

---

He wanted to ask too many questions in the time remaining.

Some had the shape of anger rather than inquiry. Others were ordinary and almost unbearable: whom she had been able to speak to, what she had missed first. He knew none of those answers yet. The title did not supply them. Nor could he manufacture them from the bench, the window or the man who had kicked the bucket.

Those people had occupied a few minutes of his life. His mother's account had begun years before he was born.

"Aapko jaana pada," he said.

"Haan."

"Yeh aapka ghoomne nikalna nahi tha."

"Nahi."

The second answer was quieter.

He heard her pause and asked whether she needed help with the present position. She called for the small adjustment instead of telling him to ignore the body because the story was important. Rudra waited while the worker checked and helped. At his end, the nurse asked about his own back. He answered before his mother returned to the conversation.

Neither check required the family history to be spoken across the ward.

---

There was little of the accepted eight minutes left.

Rukmini said she could hear that he wanted to keep asking. She wanted to tell him more too. Wanting did not give either person another unchosen interval in this one.

They agreed to ask about a later time after care and actual rest.

Then they said goodbye. The morning call ended at the limit, with the question he had actually asked answered, not held hostage to a promise of a longer story.

He stayed still while the nurse checked him.

The hand had not become reliable. The back had not made room for a sudden movement. He described the anger and the present physical feeling separately, then accepted the planned help with the position. His elbow and wrist were protected through it; the ankle and knee needed their supports when the rest was done.

He had not been asked to stand up in defence of a title.

He could remain here and believe that something terrible had been done to his mother.

---

The words returned in a different order once the room was quiet.

Highest Lord. Queen. Sister. False accusations.

Then the first sentence again, stripped of everything he had been tempted to put around it: she had not chosen to leave.

He had thought the new name might conceal a grander mother, one who could finally explain why their ordinary home had contained so much fear. Instead it had given him another person who had once had decisions made about where she could live.

That did not answer for the decisions she had later made about him.

It did make him ashamed of how easily he had imagined a departure with luggage chosen at leisure and a doorway kept available for return. He let the shame correct the picture without turning it into a demand that she comfort him for having pictured badly.

---

In Varanasi, Rukmini accepted the rest she had said she needed.

Dadi helped after the current check. No one asked her to remain upright until an extraordinary account reached a satisfying pause. When the shoulder was settled and the phone put away, she closed her eyes for a while.

Saying *my sister* to her son had made the relation audible again in a new room.

She had not given him a name to pursue. She had given him a truth about her own life, and for this interval that had been enough.

The afternoon did not begin where the morning call had stopped.

There was lunch first, and the next care, and a rest during which Rudra actually slept. He woke with the same back and the same unhelpful hand. Before requesting another call, he answered the current questions instead of offering the useful-looking statement that he had only been lying down.

Rukmini chose a later ten-minute interval after her own care and rest. Both ends accepted the time and the present conditions. The phone setup belonged to those few minutes; nobody was asked to stay listening until the family had solved everything.

He was calmer when they connected.

It did not mean he was less angry about her sister. The anger had merely stopped trying to become the first sentence of every answer.

"Aapne apni behen bola tha," he said.

"Haan. Apni."

He had not misheard a distant relation or a stranger described as family for politeness. Rukmini let the exact relation remain painful without making it vague enough to hurt less.

"Unhe pata tha ki yeh aapke saath ho raha hai."

"Mere khilaaf unhone hi aarop lagaaye the," she said.

She was repeating the part she had actually told him, not pretending a new witness had entered the room. He could believe his mother without pretending that he had stood beside the events she described. The difference did not make his belief smaller. It kept him from filling her pauses with scenes of a room he had never seen.

---

For a moment he wanted a person he could be angry at aloud.

A name, a face, some definite shape against which the helplessness might feel like preparation. The wish came so readily that it frightened him. He had heard his mother say that she had lost a home, and part of him had already begun searching for the useful work he could make out of it.

"Mera kuch karne ka mann ho raha hai," he admitted.

"Abhi kya?"

"Pata nahi. Bas... yeh sunke yahaan pada hoon."

He disliked how young the sentence sounded. She did not answer it by telling him to become older faster.

"Mera dard tumhaara hukm nahi hai," she said.

He looked towards the hand resting where the nurse had placed it.

"Aapko lagta hai main badla lene ki baat kar raha hoon."

"Maine tumse badla maanga nahi hai. Tum kya mehsoos kar rahe ho, woh bataa sakte ho. Usse abhi kuch karna zaroori nahi ho jaata."

The distinction left him nowhere to put a heroic promise.

It also left his mother in the conversation, instead of turning her into the reason for a journey she had not chosen.

---

He told her he had not given her name or family history at the court.

He had not known it. There was no virtue in the ignorance, but the practical fact mattered now. He had asked his own question there, not announced that somebody's daughter had sent him to demand a place.

"Ab bhi nahi diya," he added. "Koi message nahi bheja."

"Theek hai."

She did not tell him that he should have been recognised anyway. There was no invitation to take the empty patch of shade on Rudra's first visit as an insult to their family rather than an ordinary wrong which would have mattered whoever needed the chair.

He felt embarrassed by a thought he had not quite allowed to finish: perhaps the men in the court would have behaved differently if they had known.

Perhaps they would. He did not know.

It would not have changed what was wrong with the kick, or made the right to a place out of the sun depend on a name nobody had given.

"Wahan mujhe baithne ki zaroorat thi," he said slowly. "Kisi ka naati hone ki nahi."

His mother was quiet for a moment.

"Haan."

---

The title was still real.

He did not have to make it meaningless in order not to use it as a command. His mother's father had held a place in Swarg which Rudra had not understood existed in her life. That relationship changed what he knew about her. It did not place a title over the nurse's next question or make the finite help through the passage into work people now owed him forever.

Sampoorna had been a daughter as well as a sister.

Rukmini was here, speaking for herself.

He tried to keep both facts nearer than the picture of a stranger who could be made to pay for everything he had only just begun to hear.

When the worker at his mother's end asked about her present comfort, Rudra waited. She accepted the brief check, said what the shoulder needed and returned once the adjustment was done.

He did not count that interruption as time stolen from him. The person whose life he was asking about needed to remain able to live the rest of this day, not only tell him about an earlier one.

"Ek baat ajeeb lag rahi hai," he said when they were ready again.

Rukmini waited.

"Aapki baat sunke apni waali baat bolna... jaise main kuch chhota ginwa raha hoon."

He regretted the sentence before it was finished. There was a way it could sound as if he resented her for having suffered, or wanted her to stop telling him so his own grievances could remain the largest thing between them.

He tried to correct it.

"Aap bataao. Main sunna chahta hoon. Bas ghar ki jo baat hui thi, woh bhi—"

"Woh bhi sach hai," she said.

The answer came without an argument about whose lost years had been longer.

"Mere saath anyaay hua tha. Tumhare saath jo humne kiya, woh usse sahi nahi ho jaata."

He looked away from the phone.

His mother's voice had not become calm enough to make the sentence effortless. He could hear the work of keeping it clear. She was refusing an escape which he had almost offered her, and the refusal hurt in a way he trusted more than a reassurance that none of them had ever meant any harm.

She had been wronged.

She had also been there when he was hurt.

He could love her without asking one fact to eat the other.

---

"Main abhi bhi nahi jaanta ki maaf kaise karte hain," he said.

"Aaj yeh baat sunne ke badle mein maafi nahi deni hai."

"Aapko bura lagta hai."

"Lagta hai. Usse tumhe jaldi karni pade, yeh nahi."

He could not make the exchange painless by being a better listener. That was part of what he was learning about these conversations: attention was something he could offer, not a treatment which would leave the other person without grief.

The wish to fix her history had been easier to recognise than this smaller wish to manage her present sadness. Both could make him stop hearing what she actually wanted to say.

He stayed with the difficult thing instead.

"Aapko bataate rehna hai," he said. "Main sab ek saath nahi samajh paa raha."

"Ek saath khatam bhi nahi karna."

She was not withdrawing the answer she had given. The exile, the false accusations and the sister's part in them remained real statements in her own voice. There was more he did not know; that did not push what he knew back into mystery.

---

Before they ended, she spoke about the part just told.

"Aaj jo behen aur nirvaasan ki baat kahi hai, abhi humare beech rehne do."

"Haan."

She did not retract the permission already given for the three friends to know her birthplace and name. Rudra understood the new boundary beside the old one. He did not need to send a correction to people who had received only what he had been allowed to tell them.

He had made no recording of this call either.

"Apna jo lag raha hai, kisi se bol sakte ho," she added. "Meri poori baat kehna zaroori nahi hai uske liye."

He was glad she had said it. His own need for company did not have to be hidden to keep her history private.

At the final reminder they stopped. The ten-minute afternoon call ended with actual goodbyes, not a promise to keep the line quiet while nurses worked around them. Rukmini accepted the next care at her end. Rudra answered the current check and asked for help settling the back before somebody put the phone away.

The ordinary sequence carried him out of the conversation without pretending he had finished feeling it.

---

He rested for a while with no task in his hand.

The relation *sister* remained harder to absorb than the title. A title had distance built into it. A sister belonged in the same human part of speech as a mother, a friend, somebody whose presence ought not to need a search for danger every time they entered a room.

He knew that was a wish about family, not a law which family always obeyed.

It still explained something of why his mother had paused.

He did not try to turn the pause into an imagined scene of betrayal. He could ask for her account when she wanted to continue. Tonight he could also want somebody to talk about a different thing with him, without making that wish a denial of what he had heard.

After his next care he requested one short message to Aadhya.

He would like an ordinary few minutes later if she felt like speaking. Nothing to read or solve. There was more from his mother, still private, but he did not want to make another lonely evening out of believing that privacy meant he must have no company.

The message went after he checked it.

Aadhya answered after her hand care that she would like four minutes following rest, if the current arrangements suited both of them. Rudra accepted. The ward workers agreed to the brief supported setup at the chosen time; no ended travelling worker or resting mentor was recalled for it.

They spoke after the rest they had agreed, not in place of it.

Rudra's current position had been checked. Aadhya answered about her hand before the short setup was finished; the phone stayed on its support, not balanced in a grip she could not trust. The brief help was accepted and done. When the call connected, neither person was waiting for the other to discover a hidden problem from the sound of a brave voice.

"Aaj aur baat hui," Rudra said. "Details abhi nahi bataa sakta."

"Theek hai."

"Isliye call nahi kiya ki tu guess kare."

"Main kar bhi nahi rahi."

He could hear the difference between a question being politely withheld and one not being demanded in the first place.

For a few seconds he said nothing. Aadhya let the quiet remain ordinary, without filling it with assurances about how everything would be all right. She did not know everything. He did not need her to pretend otherwise in order to be glad she was there.

---

"Tu kuch bata," he said eventually.

"Bahut interesting nahi hai."

"Aaj simple waali chahiye."

A scooter was trying to start somewhere beyond her room. He could hear the short, stubborn attempts between her words. Aadhya told him it had been at it long enough to deserve its own care plan.

"Usko rest bol," Rudra said.

"Sunega nahi. Kisi ko jaanti hoon main aisa."

"Veer ko mat ghaseet."

"Maine naam nahi liya."

He smiled. The back stayed within its present familiar feeling; he answered when the worker glanced over and asked. The call did not need him to spend pain on proving that the joke had worked.

The scooter finally caught, ran unevenly for a little while and moved away. For no useful reason, both of them were pleased.

There was a whole world in which a small engine starting was the resolution of the current problem. He could listen to it without forgetting his mother.

---

Aadhya asked whether he had managed any of the college page.

"Thoda. Ek sentence do baar suna."

"Do baar mein samajh aaya?"

"Haan."

"Toh usne kaam kiya."

He had been preparing to describe the repeated sentence as another way in which he had fallen behind. Her answer made the repetition sound more like reading than failure.

He told her the ordinary part: he had wanted to nod so the worker could finish, then asked for the sentence again. She said she was glad he had asked. She did not offer her own recovering hands for a job which somebody else had already agreed to do, or turn the remaining minutes into a new lesson.

"Aaj padhna nahi hai," he said.

"Maine book kholi bhi nahi."

He could hear the smile in that.

It was enough to make the call a conversation rather than a service he had requested and now needed to justify. She had chosen four minutes too. She could enjoy the small foolishness of the scooter without being appointed the person responsible for his mood after a difficult family call.

---

At her limit, Aadhya said she wanted to stop.

Rudra thanked her. Not for knowing the correct thing to do with what his mother had told him, but for being willing to speak without possessing it.

"Kal ki booking nahi kar rahe," she said when he began a sentence about another time.

"Pata hai. Bas good night bol raha tha."

"Achha. Good night."

They ended the call. The four minutes did not acquire an afterlife in messages sent while either person was meant to rest. The workers helped put the phones aside within the brief work accepted. Aadhya took her own next care, not another task from his conversation.

Rudra gave the current back feeling before the nurse changed the position. She heard him, protected the elbow and wrist through the movement and checked the ankle and knee supports. The familiar need for help was not a failure to have been sufficiently moved by his mother's history.

He could stop trying to convert every feeling into a different body.

---

Later, alone with the room's ordinary sounds, he thought about what he had not promised.

He had not told his mother he would punish anyone. He had not told her the home she had lost would be returned by him, as though a place could be lifted and handed back whole. He had not asked his friends to prepare for a journey under the disguise of keeping him company.

Those omissions did not feel like emptiness now.

There was room in them for a person he had only begun to hear.

He could still be furious about the betrayal. He could still want to know the people and events beyond the little he had heard. None of that required his next act to be the loudest one available.

When sleep began to come, he let the phone remain away from the bed. His mother was resting in another city. He did not need to send a declaration across the distance to make that rest count as part of the story.

Day two hundred and three arrived without a departure.

There was the morning review, the present account of sleep and pain, the help with positions and ordinary things which still could not safely be done one-handed. Rudra answered about the back, elbow and grip before anybody asked what he wanted to do with the day. The faint had acquired no new explanation in these quiet mornings. His ankle and knee still needed the current plan.

He had slept. He did not confuse the fact with being ready for a road.

After breakfast and care, he asked whether another short call with his mother could be considered for later. The request went to her, not to somebody deciding for her that she had already told enough or ought to tell more.

She chose the afternoon, following her own care and rest.

Eight minutes, with the current checks at both ends and the ordinary help needed for the phones. Rudra accepted. No transport was reserved alongside the conversation. The people whose earlier jobs had ended remained finished with those jobs; the ward's present work continued under its own arrangement.

He let the intervening hours contain lunch and real rest.

---

When the call connected, she asked whether he was comfortable.

He gave the answer the nurse had just checked, not an account improved for his mother's benefit. She told him about her own present shoulder and the support which was helping. Help remained near; the conversation was private without requiring either person to manage alone.

"Kal Aadhya se thodi baat hui thi," he said. "Jo aapne humare beech rakhne ko kaha tha, woh nahi bataaya."

The part she had asked him to keep private had stayed private.

"Apni baat kar paaye?"

"Haan. Aur faaltu ki bhi."

"Achha kiya."

The approval held no demand for a transcript. Aadhya's few minutes could remain hers as well as his. He did not repeat the scooter joke because he needed to prove the conversation had been harmless.

For a while they spoke about the ordinary present: rest which had actually happened, the next care, whether he wanted the phone moved a little nearer. He did; the worker made the small change without handing it to him. His mother waited through that help as she had waited when he described the view.

---

There was something she had not asked him for.

A promise.

He had been so ready to give one that the absence now had a shape. He could see the familiar route from hearing pain to offering his body as the useful answer, even when nobody had said that was what they wanted. It was a route he knew from the courtyard and from battles in which stopping had once seemed indistinguishable from abandoning someone.

His mother had interrupted it with a sentence about her pain not being his command.

He was beginning to understand the sentence as more than permission to remain injured today. Even if his hand had been sound, even if a journey could have been made without difficulty, a son did not become the owner of every decision in the life which had made him.

He could ask what she wanted.

He had not done that yet.

---

The realization made him quiet.

"Sun rahe ho?" she asked.

"Haan. Soch raha tha."

"Jaldi nahi hai."

The call did have an end. She was not promising endless minutes. She was telling him that the little pause inside these minutes could exist without being filled by the first impressive thing he thought of saying.

He let it exist.

Somewhere beyond his room, a worker was asking for a passageway to be kept clear. Ordinary people needed to move through it with things he had not been asked to carry. The sound gave him a scale for the room again. He was not suspended above the city because his mother's father had held a title elsewhere.

Rukmini was still listening.

He could remember her saying *Sampoorna* and keep hearing *Ma*. Neither word told him whether the woman speaking now wanted the same thing that somebody had once tried to prevent her becoming.

---

Queen was the loudest word in the account if he allowed it to be.

A stolen possibility invited a picture of restoration so complete that the person restored might have no room left to be different from who she had been before the loss. He had nearly offered his mother that picture in place of a question.

He did not know what had happened to every person in her earlier life. He did not know the whole of what had followed her exile. Those unknowns remained real, and he wanted to hear more when she chose to continue.

They did not have to be filled before she could speak about herself in the present.

Her wish would not be a booking, an instruction to the mentors or a new measure of how much love an injured son must prove. It would be something he had made room to hear before he planned an answer to it.

---

The nurse checked that the present position was still comfortable. It was. At the other end, his mother had the same right to pause or finish, and said she could continue within the time left.

Rudra kept his hand where it was supported.

He did not ask whether she could forgive her sister. He did not ask whom he should confront, which title he ought to claim or how soon he must be strong enough to take her somewhere.

He asked the person who had been sent away, before deciding what return would mean for her.

"Ma, aap khud phir se wahan jaana chahti ho?"

### END OF CHAPTER 132
