# CHAPTER FIFTY-THREE — WHAT RISES FROM THE FALL

---

> *"The witness recorded how many times he got up. The account omits where he put his feet.*
> *At the end, he no longer returned to the place from which he had fallen. He stood where the others needed a way through."*
>
> *— Turanan landing inquiry, appended to the manual of returning rather than the book of victories*

---

That afternoon, before the gate at the Machli bend moved, Veer had been unable to button his own cuff.

The button was small. His fingers were large. His left forearm, which had spent the morning helping to hold a cart above a woman's knee, had begun its old deep ache. None of these facts ought to have felt like an accusation.

He left the cuff open.

On his bed the steel tin lay beside a clean wrist-wrap. He lifted out his grandfather's manual, meaning to read one page before the night lesson. The book opened where it always did.

*Pehle: tan. Doosra: disha. Teesra: dono jhooth.*

His grandmother's green correction remained underneath. He did not need to run a finger along it any more. He could have traced the words with his eyes closed.

He understood them. He had stood empty-handed at the ghat, after the baton broke, and understood them with his whole frightened body.

So why had thirteen repetitions in a sand pit reduced him to a pair of rigid wrists?

He shut the manual before the question could turn into an answer borrowed from somebody else's handwriting.

Aadhya had not looked victorious when they brought her back. She had looked grey around the mouth and furious at a cup. Veer kept seeing the right thumb that did not know where the handle was.

He was glad she had done it. That gladness was uncomplicated.

The fear beside it was not. The next time she had to hold a road, he wanted to be able to give her somewhere to put it down.

He put the manual back in the tin, wrapped his arm without overtightening it, and went out.

---

Ira was at the lower verandah, sorting bandages for the night stations. The tremor had made folding a slow business. She had solved it by holding one end beneath her paperweight and drawing the other towards her.

"Turanan?" she asked.

"Haan."

"Seedha jaoge?"

"Thoda London ghoom ke."

She looked at him until he regretted the joke.

"Landing pe rakh dena," she said, pushing a cloth parcel towards him. "Upar wala relief bench. Main notices leke aati hoon."

He tested the parcel's weight. "Isme aadmi toh nahi?"

"Tumhare liye bandage zyada rakhe hain."

He wanted to say she would not need them. Instead he asked her to fasten the pin on his wrist-wrap. She did it carefully, avoiding the place where the skin had thickened under the old splint.

"Dheela toh nahi?"

"Theek hai."

Her hand remained near the pin.

"Khulta dikhe toh dobara baandh lena," she said.

"Lesson mein?"

"Jahan khule."

He could hear the other instruction in it. Do not pretend the fastening is sound because people are watching.

He tucked the parcel beneath his good arm. "Tum bhi pencil girne do kabhi. Har baar pakadna zaroori nahi."

She moved the paperweight off the cloth. Her fingers shook. The fold stayed.

"Jao," she said.

---

The river bent east beyond Machli-ghat, turned round the old rock, and returned upon itself in the oxbow. In the last light, the current looked as though it had reconsidered a decision.

Veer left the bandages at the upper relief bench. Ira would work there with the night-watch clerk, well away from the training pit. Dhruv's lesson still had one student, no torch, and no audience to impress.

Bhanu met him at the akhada's river door.

"Aaj bhi barabar?" the wrestler asked.

"Aaj khud se haarne aaya hoon. Suna hai expert yahin milte hain."

Bhanu grinned and slapped the uninjured shoulder.

He had been helping repair the landing below the akhada. Through the open wall Veer could see its iron gate, raised between two stone guides over the narrow gangway. A counterweight hung in a shaft on the bank side, joined to the gate by chains passing over an overhead wheel. The gate kept loaded cargo from rolling down onto a boat before the boatmen were ready.

Tonight the cargo had been cleared. A few families still waited on the floating landing beyond it for the late ferry. The watchman had tied the gate up as an extra precaution and locked the wheel.

Bhanu showed Veer the knot because he was proud of it.

"Ira se seekha?" Veer asked.

"Meri maa se."

"Same syllabus."

Dhruv called his name from the pit.

The light had thinned enough to leave only the sky's reflection on the river. Veer put his bag at the wall, stepped onto the sand, and found the first star without trying to name it.

"Giro," Dhruv said.

Veer lowered himself.

"Woh baithna hua."

So Bhanu threw him.

The fall drove air out of his chest. He lay still for the two breaths the akhada allowed, set his heel, and came up in the familiar wave from ankle to shoulder.

Bhanu caught the shoulder before it completed the turn and put him down again.

"Pehli jagah pe kyun laut rahe ho?" Dhruv asked.

Veer spat a grain of sand off his lip. "Wahin se gira tha."

"Woh jagah ab uske paas hai."

Bhanu stood exactly where Veer had been standing. His feet had not moved since the throw.

Veer looked from the feet to the strip of empty sand behind them.

He had learned how to rise. Without noticing, he had made a habit of rising into the same answer.

Down at the landing, a chain clicked.

Bhanu turned towards the sound at once.

---

Bhanu was halfway down the steps when the chain clicked again.

The counterweight had risen two notches inside its shaft. The gate had come down by the same amount. Its securing rope was stretched tight enough to flatten against the iron bar.

Bhanu checked the locked wheel, then put a hand on the rope and took it away sharply.

"Sabko upar lao," he told the watchman.

Nobody argued about a ferry fare. The watchman opened the side wicket and began bringing the waiting families towards the gangway. Bhanu wedged a timber beneath the gate while Veer got to his feet.

Dhruv was beside him before he reached the steps.

"Lesson khatam," the teacher said.

Below, the oxbow had changed colour. A narrow dark run crossed the reflected sky, moving towards the landing against the slower surface current. At its far end a low service launch lay almost motionless beside a mooring post. A man on it leaned over a metal case.

Veer could see his hands. One held the case open. The other turned a short handle inside it.

No shout came from the launch. No explanation.

The landing-gate dropped hard onto Bhanu's timber.

Wood cracked. The securing rope parted with a noise like a palm striking water.

Veer reached the lower bar before it could take the gangway. His left arm caught too much weight. He shifted it onto his good shoulder, bent his knees, and drove upwards.

For a moment the gate rose.

"Nikaalo," he said.

Bhanu pulled the first two people through. An old man came after them on a stick, moving as fast as his knees allowed. The next family stayed back to give him room.

It would have worked if the gangway had been on solid ground.

Its outer end rested on the floating landing. The landing's upstream mooring line had begun to slip through a cleat that should have been pinned. Each time Veer pushed against the gate, his feet pushed the gangway farther down.

The gap above him narrowed.

Dhruv caught the mooring line before the pin tore free. He wrapped it round a stone bollard, turned his angle-ring flat against the top, and drew the loose span towards him.

Pale light touched the line. Its wet fibres stopped sliding.

The floating landing still yawed in the current. Dhruv could hold its distance from the bank; he could not make its deck stay level as well.

"Bank main rok raha hoon," he called. "Pair ki jagah badlo."

Veer tried to set his right foot nearer the bank.

The gangway rolled beneath it.

He fell to one knee. The gate struck his shoulder, and the brief pale shell of VELUM flared over his back, breaking the impact into a dozen smaller pains. Metal scraped cloth. Something warm ran down his shin.

He pushed up from the knee.

The gate rose with him, just enough for Bhanu to bring the old man's stick through. Then it came down again, heavier than before.

On the launch, the short handle turned.

Veer saw it between the bars. Anger helped him straighten his back and did nothing useful for the left forearm.

He could have dropped out from beneath the gate and gone after the man. The bank path was clear. Three strides, a jump from the mooring post, and the metal case would be within reach.

Behind him a child asked whether the boat was still coming.

Veer put his shoulder back under the bar.

"Bhanu. Kitne?"

"Chhe. Aur main."

The watchman had stayed on the floating side to help the last family. The six included him. Veer checked that without turning his head: two small pairs of feet, three adults, one bare-legged watchman.

He did not look at the launch again.

---

He rose the way he had practised.

Heel, ankle, knee, hip, shoulder. The wave came cleanly through his body. The gate lifted a hand's breadth.

Then the chain tightened and sent the same weight straight down through him into the floating gangway.

His knees bent. The iron returned to his shoulder. The people behind him had gained no room at all.

He tried a second time, letting the old forearm stay close instead of extending it. Better for the arm. No better for the people.

Sand had accepted the force beneath his palms that morning. The gangway could not. Every perfect rise was pushing away the floor he needed.

Bhanu threw a rope round the gate's side bar and hauled from the bank. It changed the angle enough for one child to crawl underneath. The child's mother slid after him, caught his ankle, and passed him up into the watchman's waiting hands on the safe side.

The watchman had crossed without Veer seeing him. Good. Four left on the floating deck now, including Bhanu, who had gone back through to help.

The other child would not crawl. He clung to the waist of the man beside him and screamed whenever the deck moved.

"Mujhe dekho," Veer said.

The child looked at the gate crushing his shoulder instead.

The gangway's outer support slipped off the landing.

Veer's left leg dropped into the river.

Cold struck his hip. His shoulder lost contact with the gate. He caught the lower bar with his right hand, hit the stone guide with his ribs, and felt his left forearm take a wrenching load as he tried to keep his head above the water.

The bar descended another inch.

For a few seconds there was no counted breath, no stance, nothing that could be written in a manual. There was iron in his palm and a boy crying behind it. His shin struck a submerged step. He almost lost the bar in the effort to pull himself back onto the gangway.

Back where he had been.

Again.

He stopped pulling.

Under the bank-side guide, the old stone step continued beneath the water. He could feel its edge with his heel. It was fixed. Not level, not comfortable, but fixed.

Dhruv's voice came from above him, strained now.

"Veer. Main zyada der nahi—"

"Pata hai."

He moved his right hand along the lower bar towards the bank. His ribs scraped the stone. He put the whole sole of his left foot on the submerged step and let himself sink low enough to bend the knee.

There was no room to stand where he had fallen from.

There was room beside it.

Above his head, the gate shuddered in its guides. The next pull would either wrench him loose or take the stonework with it.

Veer turned his shoulder away from the load and opened his empty left hand.

---

His fingers closed around a pale line.

At first Veer thought it was the reflection of the chain on the water. Then the line resisted his grip. A shaft lengthened beneath his palm, straight and hard, its lower end finding the edge of the submerged step.

The upper end pressed against the gate's crossbar.

Not a replacement for the broken baton. Too long, too narrow at the point, balanced along a different purpose. A spear, its light held so closely that the wet iron cast a shadow against it.

Veer could not have lifted it with his left arm alone.

He did not try.

He let the shaft take the angle between gate and stone, brought his right foot into a shallow notch in the bank, and rose from the bent knee.

This time the ground stayed beneath him.

The force entered at the crossbar and travelled down the shaft. He felt it through his shoulder, ribs, hips, the places a fall had taught him to keep separate. Pale bands opened across those places and joined. The shell over his back did not break at the first impact. It shifted, taking the pressure from one joint to the next as his body came upright.

His right hand left the gate.

For a heartbeat he stood without holding himself up.

**"VELUM: SKANDA."**

The skin over his knee tore against the step. Pain struck up his old forearm so fiercely that he nearly dropped the new weapon.

He caught it against his hip instead. The load passed through the broad muscle of his thigh. His arm stayed bent, close to his body, protected without being spared.

The gate rose.

On the floating side, Bhanu stared through the opening.

"Chalo," Veer said.

Bhanu moved at once.

He sent the remaining woman first. She came bent double, dragging one foot. Ira appeared at the bank-side end of the gangway and reached past the watchman to take her elbow.

The man with the frightened child tried to follow too quickly. The deck dipped. The child screamed again and wrapped both legs round his father's waist.

"Mujhe nahi," Veer told him. "Raste ko dekho."

The man lowered his gaze to the strip of gangway beneath the gate.

One foot. Then the other.

The force on Veer's spear increased.

At the launch the handle had stopped turning. The man over the case was pulling a second lever now, short hard strokes with both hands. A thin line ran from the launch into the dark run crossing the water.

The gate twisted towards the river.

Veer's first instinct was to lock his knees. He felt the error start, the old attempt to become a wall because a wall was what stood between people and injury.

He let the knee bend instead.

The spear slid a little along the crossbar, its point biting a new notch into the iron. The pale bands across his back shifted with it. His body went down six inches, not in defeat this time but to keep the path open while the weight changed direction.

The child and his father passed through.

Only Bhanu remained.

The gangway's loose end struck the landing and bounced. Bhanu lost his footing, caught a side rail, and hung for an instant above the water.

Veer took his right hand off the spear.

The shaft wavered. He drove its butt farther into the stone notch and reached towards the wrestler.

Too far.

Bhanu swung a knee over the rail, released one hand, and pushed himself towards the gap. He was rising too, badly, painfully, using everything the akhada had taught him and nothing resembling a beautiful stance.

Veer caught his wrist.

The pull almost tore the weapon free.

He let his own body sink with Bhanu's weight, kept his heel on the fixed step, then came up into the next rise with the wrestler moving beside him. No clean lift. No triumphant straightening. Two men hauled through a narrowing gap, one of them swearing steadily enough for the watchman to know they were both still breathing.

Ira and the watchman took Bhanu's shoulders.

Veer released his wrist.

The floating landing was empty.

"Khaali!" Ira called.

Dhruv looked once along its deck to make certain. Then he let the mooring line go in a controlled spill, turning the angle-ring edgeways against the bollard. The pale light left the wet fibres.

The pontoon swung free of the gangway before it could roll beneath the gate. It drifted towards the shallow inside of the bend, where the watchman's spare rope could catch it later.

Veer remained below the descending iron.

He could hold it. For a little longer he could. The discovery was dizzying: a margin where there had been none, strength not exhausted by its first use.

He nearly spent it proving that the margin existed.

Then he saw Ira on the bank, one hand over the eye nearest the spray, waiting for him to come through rather than staring at what he had become.

The people were safe. There was no reason to hold the gate.

He turned the spear so its point travelled out of the notch. Kept its butt planted while he moved his right foot onto the next stone. Let the gate descend along the shaft instead of dropping onto his shoulder.

The upper bar scraped past his ear.

He stepped clear.

Iron smashed the empty gangway. The broken planks flew into the river. The counterweight struck the top of its shaft and hung there, chain singing through the wheel.

Veer staggered onto the bank.

The spear remained in his hand for three steps. At the fourth, its point thinned to a line. The joined bands across his body opened at the joints and went out.

His left knee buckled.

Bhanu caught him under the good arm this time. Veer tried to help, discovered that helping required a forearm he could no longer persuade to work, and let himself be lowered against the bollard.

The service launch was moving away.

He could see the metal case beneath a cloth at the man's feet. The short handle had been removed. Something small and bright lay on the launch's gunwale, draining water back into the river.

He had no strength left to follow.

"Jaane do," Dhruv said.

Veer watched the boat pass beyond the old rock.

He was still angry. Saving people had not cured that. It had simply left him in no doubt about where he would have put his feet if he had to choose again.

Beside him, the watchman put a wet hand on the bollard and began saying the names of the people who had crossed.

This time Veer listened without counting over him.

---

At the relief bench, Veer recognized the bandage parcel by Ira's uneven knot.

He laughed when the watchwoman opened it.

"Kya hua?" she asked.

"Advance booking."

She did not find that funny. She cut his trouser leg above the knee and poured water over the grit. The laugh left him in a sharp breath.

The wound was shallow and wide. His ribs were less obliging. Each deep intake found the place where the stone guide had struck him. The watchwoman made him breathe while she pressed carefully along the side; when he tried to turn it into a demonstration of how well he could endure pain, she stopped and waited for him to behave normally.

Ira stood at the end of the bench with a lantern screened towards the wall. The lesson was over; the rescued children needed to see their way up the steps. Even so, the small shaded light looked apologetic beneath Dhruv's sky.

"Left arm," Ira said.

Veer held it out.

His fingers did not close properly.

The old splint had been gone long enough for people to stop asking about it. He had begun to like that silence. Now the watchwoman padded the arm, tied it close to his body, and told him that the doctor would decide in the morning how much damage he had done.

"Toota nahi hai," he said.

"Tumhara kehna note kar liya. Abhi hilao mat."

He stopped arguing.

Below them, Bhanu and the watchman secured the empty pontoon in the inside shallows. Dhruv inspected the mooring line by touch. The rope had left two raw burns across his palm. He wrapped them without mentioning them to Veer.

The people from the landing had gone to the upper court to wait together. Nobody wanted the ferry now. One of the children had fallen asleep sitting upright against his father's chest.

Veer could hear the father speaking softly to him. Not a story. Just the names of things that were still there: bench, lamp, shoulder, shoe.

Veer shut his eyes for a moment.

---

Bhanu brought the recovered fittings up on a board.

The gate's ordinary brake had not failed. A second line had been spliced beneath the covered bearing, where it could pull the counterweight without moving the locked handwheel. Its end ran through a narrow metal eye towards the river. The eye had torn loose when the gate hit the gangway.

"Doosra haath," Bhanu said, showing the splice.

Not a name. A description of the work.

The line led to the place where the launch had waited. Nobody pretended that this proved who had sat in it.

Ira copied the arrangement with ordinary sight. Then she turned the metal eye over.

Its underside was polished, slightly hollow, too clean for a piece that had spent a season beneath a river landing. A tiny circular plate sat inside the hollow. She tilted it towards the lantern, and Veer saw a faint pattern of slots.

"Roof wala?" he asked.

"Uska rishtedaar," she said. "Same nahi bolungi."

She put it beside the drawing rather than against her eye.

Veer appreciated that more than he could explain. It was like watching someone put a weapon down.

"Jo neeche hua," she said after a while, "uska sketch banaungi. Sahi batana."

"Main achha dikhna chahiye."

"Iron ke neeche the. Profile nahi mila."

She drew the fixed step, the gate, the angle of the spear. Her pencil trembled at the long diagonal. She braced the heel of her hand against the board and completed it in short sections.

He corrected one thing. The shaft had not stayed in the first notch. It had moved as he bent his knee.

She erased the single straight arrow and drew several smaller ones.

"Har baar khade nahi hue," she said.

"Hua toh tha."

"Beech mein neeche bhi gaye. Jaan-boojh ke."

Veer watched the new arrows. Down as part of the rise. Not down as a mistake that had to be hidden between two successful positions.

He thought of his manual in its steel tin, the old lesson he had nearly used as proof that there was nothing left to learn.

"Haan," he said. "Woh likhna."

---

Later, when Bhanu went to fetch his bag, Dhruv sat beside him.

The teacher's angle-ring lay across his bandaged palm. One edge had been worn bright by the mooring rope.

Veer nodded towards it. "Aapka bhi equipment gaya."

"Chalega."

"Mera bhi?"

Dhruv glanced at the sling. "Doctor bolega."

Veer looked away first.

From the upper court came the sound of a relieved woman scolding her husband. He had lost one sandal and was proposing to go down in search of it. Her refusal was so complete that even the river seemed to listen.

"Unke peechhe jaa sakta tha," Veer said.

Dhruv did not ask whom he meant.

"Jaate toh yahan kaun rehta?"

"Aap."

The teacher lifted his rope-burned hand. Not to shame him. Simply to include it in the account.

Veer nodded.

"Dono jagah nahi tha main," Dhruv said. "Tum bhi nahi the. Isliye log ek se zyada hote hain."

For a while neither spoke.

Then Veer asked for a scrap of paper. With his good hand he wrote a note to put inside the tin, separate from the old handwriting.

*Uthna aata hai. Ab jagah chunni hai.*

He knew how to rise. Now he had to choose where.

He let the paper dry before folding it. Some things did not improve by being hurried into a pocket.

---

Ira stayed behind while the watchwoman arranged a slow walk home for him. Bhanu would go on his good side. There would be no celebratory run through the lanes.

She wanted one more ordinary look at the metal eye before Vedant collected it.

The board was wet. A shallow basin beneath the bench held the water left from cleaning Veer's knee. In it the lantern made a narrow trembling road.

Ira lifted the fitting by its unpolished rim.

For an instant the wet hollow reflected Veer's pale spear exactly as she had seen it beneath the gate. She knew it was gone; she had watched it go. She lowered the fitting and waited for the afterimage to fade.

It faded.

Good. She wrote *afterimage — unconfirmed* beside the sketch.

Then she noticed the little plate inside the hollow had turned.

The lantern had not moved. Neither had the board. She shifted her face to the right, and the plate followed a fraction behind, its slots gathering into a dark point at the centre of her reflected eye.

She stopped breathing for a moment.

From the steps Veer called, "Ira?"

She set the fitting down with its polished side against the wood.

The plate clicked once underneath.

She had turned it towards the light to examine it.

It had taken the angle of her eye instead.
