# CHAPTER FIFTY-TWO — THE ROOT THROUGH THE CRACK

---

> *"The roots were present before the failure. So were the drain, the loose stones, and the people waiting to cross.*
> *The repair account must not describe the girl as additional material. Her hands were injured in the work."*
>
> *— west-threshold inquiry, correction entered after the first report was returned unsigned*

---

By sunrise Aadhya had learned that a cracked pot could cut through cloth without making a sound.

A wet line spread across the sling beneath it. She adjusted the bundle against her hip, felt the broken rim press through to her skin, and stopped trying to carry it like an unbroken thing.

"Neeche se," Sitara said.

Aadhya put both palms beneath the base. The cloth stopped pulling against the crack.

They had kept it in a shallow grain tray overnight, with loose soil banked around the split. The new shoot had opened two small leaves. It grew sideways through the clay, then turned upwards. One leaf was caught against the edge.

Aadhya wanted to free it with her injured index finger. She used the corner of a clean reed instead.

There had been no second shimmer in her hand. No answer to her attempt to summon the green before bed. Whatever had happened to the pot had not left her with a new obedience she could command.

That should have disappointed her. Mostly she was tired of wanting the right thing so fiercely that she stopped seeing what was in front of her.

At the kitchen door Rukmini gave them two rotis folded around salt and onion. Sitara took both portions because Aadhya's hands were full.

"Ghar hote jaana," Rukmini told her. "Tumhare pitaji kal bhi poochh rahe the."

"Pata hai."

"Unko bhi pata hai ki tumko pata hai. Isse kaam nahi chalta."

So they stopped at the Sharma house on the way to the west threshold.

Her father had kept the tea on the unlit side of the stove. He did not ask whether she had succeeded. He looked at the cloth around her finger, then moved the cup to the side where she could reach it with her left hand.

She set the tulsi bundle on the floor.

"Iska gamla toot gaya," she said.

"Dikh raha hai."

"Phenkna nahi tha."

He crouched, studied the little shoot, and brought an old steel plate from the cupboard. He put it beneath the bundle so the broken clay could not cut her again.

"Paudha bachana hai toh gamla sar pe leke mat ghoomo. Jagah do."

It was very like Sitara's advice. From him it sounded less mysterious and much harder to evade.

Aadhya drank the cold tea. Her father talked about a milk bill someone had paid twice and a sack of flour that had arrived damp. Before the siege she would have waited for an opening to leave. This morning she listened until he finished.

When they rose, he tied the plate to the cloth sling himself. The knot was so thorough that Sitara raised an eyebrow.

"Ab kaatna padega," Aadhya said.

"Toh kaat lena."

He did not let go until she had both hands properly underneath.

---

The west-threshold half-ring had lost more pots than it had plants. After the siege, people had pushed the surviving root-balls together in the shade of the milestone, then gone to help where the injuries were human.

Aadhya could not blame them. She had done the same.

Now the exposed roots had begun drying into one another. Tulsi stems leaned across marigold leaves; a length of grass had found the damp under a cracked brick. The half-ring looked untidy and stubbornly alive.

Beyond it the road dipped over a masonry drain. The retaining wall on the riverward side had been patched after the fighting, leaving a narrow passage for handcarts. Two women waited there with water cans while a boy guided a loaded cart across the planks.

Aadhya knelt in the bare place nearest the milestone.

The soil felt cool on top and wrong beneath.

She kept her palm down. A little lower there should have been the slow dampness she knew from watering. Instead she felt a pull, as if the roots were holding on to water that was being taken away from them.

"Kal raat bhi," she said.

Sitara lowered herself beside her without asking her to explain the unfinished sentence.

They set the pot down. Aadhya cut her father's knot, folded the cloth away from the crack, and tried to ease the plant free. The soil held together; the root through the drainage hole would not pass back through it.

"Toh toda jaaye?" Sitara asked.

Aadhya looked at her.

"Gamla," Sitara clarified.

"Pata hai."

Still she hesitated. Something she had carried so carefully ought not to be broken on purpose. She hated finding that little feeling in herself, as if the care had been for the clay all along.

She pressed her palm against one side and broke off a loose piece.

The tulsi came away with most of its soil. The new shoot lay undamaged across her wrist.

Under the root-ball, where the dark earth met the old drainage hole, the roots were curled tightly round themselves.

"Rasta tha," Aadhya said. "Main upar dekh rahi thi. Yeh neeche dhoondh raha tha."

She lowered it into the gap in the half-ring. Instead of packing the soil flat, she worked it between the roots a little at a time, leaving room for water to enter.

Sitara gave her the lota.

Aadhya poured. Water darkened the soil, paused, and vanished too quickly.

Both women watched the wet patch shrink.

Down at the drain mouth a thin stream had begun running out of a joint that should have been dry.

Aadhya put her palm back on the ground. The pull beneath it strengthened.

At the crossing, a cart wheel bumped the patched wall. Dust sifted from between two stones.

She stood so fast that the lota tipped over.

"Cart roko," she said.

The boy guiding it looked at her hand, saw no green, and looked away.

Aadhya stepped into the road and caught the wooden shaft with both hands.

"Roko. Neeche se mitti jaa rahi hai."

This time he felt the tremor through the wood too.

---

They unloaded the cart before moving it.

The boy protested about the sacks getting dirty. One of the women waiting with water cans told him dirt could be brushed off and a leg could not. She carried the first sack herself. By the time the others joined in, Sitara had stretched a rope across the approach.

Aadhya walked the edge on hands and knees.

The drain ran from a shallow settling basin beyond the road, under the patched crossing, and out towards the lower river path. She had watered the plants from that basin for weeks. Now its surface was sinking visibly, leaving a wet brown band on the stone.

A mason came from the houses with his tool bag half closed. He pressed the handle of a hammer against the retaining wall, listened, and stopped the cart boy from climbing down for a better look.

"Paani mitti kheench raha hai. Patthar abhi baithe nahi."

The stones had not moved yet. The word *yet* changed how everyone stood.

Sitara sent the boy for Vedant. Aadhya asked the women to move people back from the road, then knelt beside the first exposed root-ball.

She could make a plant hold water. She had done it before without knowing what to call it. If the roots could keep some of what the drain was taking, the soil might stop slipping long enough for the mason to brace the wall.

She set her uninjured hand among them.

Warmth gathered. Green reached the first leaves and brightened them, absurdly pretty in the dust.

Nothing happened below.

She shifted closer, let her knees settle, tried to feel the place where the little feeder roots met the deeper ones. The leaves lifted higher. Water beaded along their edges.

The wet band in the basin continued falling.

She pulled her hand away before she wasted any more.

"Upar aa raha hai," she said.

Sitara nodded.

"Maine neeche bheja tha."

"Tumne bheja. Unhone liya. Dono ek baat nahi hain."

Aadhya wanted, briefly and viciously, for somebody to give her an instruction that did not have two halves.

Then a woman behind the rope asked if the east lane was still clear, and the feeling passed. Aadhya knew the answer to that. She got up and gave it.

Vedant arrived carrying his basin and a length of clear pipe. Rudra and Veer came behind him. Rudra stopped when he saw the broken pot's cloth on the ground, then followed Aadhya's pointing hand to the drain instead.

"Kya chahiye?" he asked.

Not *shall I try*. She was grateful enough not to look at him for too long.

"Woh planks. Seedhe nahi, peechhe se. Zameen beech mein khokhli hai."

He and Veer took the planks round the safe side. They laid them where the mason indicated, spreading the load over stones that still held.

Vedant lowered one end of the clear pipe into the settling basin. Water rose in it, stopped, then jerked backwards.

His expression changed.

Under the drain's outer grille, a brass tongue had been pushed between two stones. Most of it lay in shadow. Aadhya could see a little slit on the exposed end, like the fitting on the haveli roof.

"Mat kheenchna," Vedant told the mason.

"Yahi toh atka hai."

"Atka nahi. Lagaya hai."

He used a stick to show them the thin cord running from the tongue into the closed service channel. It was tight enough to hum when he touched the stone beside it.

Rudra stepped back of his own accord.

The sight hurt Aadhya more than his seam had. A month ago she would have thought caution the opposite of courage in him. Now he stood holding a plank while something inside his sleeve lit and faded, and she understood the effort of remaining useful without being allowed to be spectacular.

She took the other end of the plank.

"Neeche rakhte hain," she said.

Together they slid it to the mason.

---

For the next half-hour the work was almost ordinary.

They dug a shallow diversion upstream of the settling basin. The water-can women carried away the loose earth. Veer and Rudra held the brace while the mason packed wedges beneath it. Sitara worked beside Aadhya, opening little pockets around the root-balls so that they would not be torn when the soil shifted.

Aadhya's injured fingertip burned each time grit entered the dressing. She changed hands rather than ask for a fresh cloth; when Sitara noticed, she changed the cloth for her without comment.

The new tulsi shoot stood in the middle of the loosened bed. Its two leaves did not face the sun. They faced the drain.

Aadhya touched the soil beside it.

This time she did not push warmth towards the leaves. She followed the cool path the water had made. It passed below the tulsi, under a buried root thicker than her thumb, and towards the old tree on the lower path.

She had walked over that root every day without noticing it.

Something answered from farther away than her hand could reach. The tree's leaves rattled, though the morning air was still.

Aadhya took her palm away in alarm.

"Uska tha," she said. "Maine kheench liya."

Sitara looked towards the tree. "Wapas kar sakti ho?"

Aadhya tried. A little of the warmth left her wrist. The leaves steadied. Her injured nail began bleeding again.

There was the route. There was the price of taking it in one direction.

Before she could say so, Vedant called for everyone to move back.

The diversion had filled. For a moment its shallow current ran exactly where they had dug it. Then the water stopped at the far end as cleanly as if someone had put a lid against it.

The brass tongue under the grille clicked.

Aadhya felt the buried root pull hard against her knee.

The settling basin emptied in one long gulp.

The mason's wedges shot out of the wall.

One struck the rope-post. The rope fell, and the people waiting behind it stepped back into the mouth of the east lane, where a loaded handcart had just arrived from the other side.

For an instant the two streams of people could not pass each other.

The retaining wall leaned towards them.

---

The front of the wall tipped riverward.

Behind it, the last intact paving slab sank at one end and rose at the other. Its lifted edge caught the handcart's axle. The cart swung sideways across the east lane, trapping the waiting people between its load and the house-wall.

Veer reached the axle first. He got his good shoulder beneath the shaft and stopped it crushing a woman's knee. Rudra hauled a sack off the top. The shifting load gave them a gap no wider than a forearm.

Then another stone dropped out of the retaining wall.

The gap closed.

Aadhya felt the movement through her knees before she saw it. The buried root was being stretched across an opening in the soil. If it tore, the tree would go with the wall, and the lower path would take the whole weight.

She put her palm over it.

Green ran through the nearest leaves and stopped at the edge of the broken ground.

Her old reach. Enough to lift a plant. Not enough to keep a road beneath people's feet.

"Aadhya, peechhe," Sitara said.

There was time to get back. Aadhya knew that. Sitara was not asking her to abandon anyone; she was making room for a second attempt that might not kill the person attempting it.

But the route was already under Aadhya's hand.

Not a line she had to throw across empty air. The root crossed the break. The soil rested on it. Farther down, the tree's weight held it in place. Water ran along the underside, taking a little earth each time it passed.

She had been trying to make the plants obey the shape of her reach.

She turned her hand so the whole palm touched.

Her other hand went to the old threshold stone beside the milestone. Sun had warmed its upper face; underneath, the root touched the damp foundation. One palm on living ground, one on the place where people crossed it.

For once she did not send the green ahead.

She let the strain arrive.

It struck through her wrist like a cart rolling over bone.

Her shoulders folded. Her injured nail split to the quick. She heard herself make a thin animal sound and would have pulled away if the next movement had not come from her feet: toes spreading against earth, knees taking weight, back straightening just enough to give the force somewhere else to go.

The green passed her fingertip without stopping there.

It climbed her arms in fine branching lines. Not light resting on skin: a change beneath it, warm channels opening from palm to elbow to shoulder, her whole body answering where one exhausted hand had failed. She could feel the root's far end as distinctly as the stone beneath her left thumb.

The air smelled of bruised tulsi.

Aadhya lifted her head.

**"SHAKTI: ADI SHAKTI."**

Blood ran from beneath three nails at once. Down on the lower path, the old tree shed a scatter of yellowing leaves.

The root tightened.

The road did not leap back into place. It stopped moving for half a heartbeat, lurched, and held again. Aadhya felt each stone drag against the rootwork beneath it. Every shift threatened to tear her palms apart.

"Ek-ek," she said.

Nobody heard.

Sitara turned towards the cart. "Ek-ek niklo. Bhaagna nahi."

Rudra lifted the sacks down one at a time. Veer lowered the axle enough for the first woman to slip past. Behind her, the cart boy pressed himself against the house-wall and pulled a smaller child through the gap by the back of his shirt.

The last woman's water can caught between the axle and the slab.

"Chhod do," Aadhya said.

The woman did not let go. It was a good can, soldered twice, half her household's stored water inside it.

Aadhya could not blame her for the half-second it took to choose.

Then the handle tore. The woman fell backwards into Rudra's arms, and the can burst against the stone.

Water ran over Aadhya's left palm.

She let it pass into the dry soil instead of pulling it towards the plant. The buried root eased by a fraction. More roots answered beneath the adjoining bed: existing threads, fine and pale, taking the strain into a wider patch of ground.

The new tulsi shoot bent flat. Aadhya felt the tiny stem begin to tear and loosened her grip on that part of the bed. It sprang up again, shaking.

She could not ask one new thing to carry everything.

"Neeche wala rasta khaali?" she called.

The mason shouted back. Clear.

"Tree ke paas mat jaana."

He caught the cart boy before he could run that way.

Aadhya drew on the broader root beneath her right hand. The tree's leaves rustled hard. Its bark roughened against a pressure she could feel in her own forearms, and the warmth rising into her was no longer gentle.

Too much taken in one direction.

She pressed her left palm harder against the threshold. Let the heat travel out with the water. Let the wet earth receive what the leaves could not spare.

The brass tongue beneath the grille rang like a spoon struck against a cup.

Vedant looked from it to her hands.

"Roko mat," he said. "Dhalne do."

She did. Not away from the people, not back into herself: down the open diversion they had already dug.

The small current broke through its invisible stop. Muddy water struck the far bank and ran free.

Vedant cut the cord with the mason's chisel.

The brass tongue snapped sideways. A narrow piece of its edge peeled back, red with heat, and the pull beneath Aadhya's knees slackened.

"Ab," she whispered.

Veer shoved the empty cart into the lane. Rudra and the mason dragged the brace clear. The last man stepped onto solid paving.

Aadhya let the road go.

The riverward wall collapsed in three heavy blows. Dust struck the half-ring. A slab rolled down the empty lower path and stopped against the base of the tree.

Her arms were still green.

For one terrified instant she thought she would not be able to stop either.

She loosened her fingers one by one. Lifted the uninjured edge of each palm. Allowed the warmth to leave her shoulders before taking her knees off the ground.

The branching lines faded towards her wrists.

She could no longer feel her right thumb.

When she tried to stand, the leg beneath her folded. Sitara caught her under the arms and lowered her onto the threshold stone.

The people were on the safe side. The water was running down the dug channel. Aadhya looked from one fact to the other, needing both before she could believe she had finished.

Then she bent over her hands and was sick into the dust.

---

She did not hear the cheering until someone asked people to stop it.

Aadhya sat with her back against the milestone while Sitara washed grit from her hands. Her right thumb lay across her lap at an angle that looked wrong, although she could move it when she watched closely. She simply could not feel it move.

The index finger beside it was going numb too.

"Wapas aa jayega?" she asked.

Sitara examined the nail-bed before answering. "Abhi bandhaai karte hain. Uske baad dekhte hain."

Aadhya looked away. She preferred the honest answer to reassurance and wished, bitterly, that honesty felt better.

The woman whose water can had broken came through the crowd carrying its flattened handle. She kept turning it over, trying to understand where it might be soldered again.

"Kisi ko laga?" Aadhya asked her.

"Meri kohni chhili. Aur tum—"

"Bachcha?"

"Ghar bhej diya."

Aadhya nodded. The woman crouched, set the broken handle beside her, and touched her shoulder once before leaving.

It was the only thanks she could take without wanting to cry.

Across the road, Rudra and the mason were checking the lower path from a safe distance. Veer held up the fallen rope for people to pass under on their way to the longer eastern route. Nobody needed green hands to do that work. Nobody could have crossed without it.

Aadhya watched until her stomach settled.

"Kitni der?" she asked.

"Tumhari?" Sitara said. "Log nikal sake. Itni."

"Mujhe measurement chahiye."

"Phir Vedant se poochhna. Mujhe tumhara chehra dikh raha tha."

Aadhya leaned her head back against the milestone. The stone was warm. For the first time that day she felt only stone, not everything touching it underneath.

---

By afternoon, the half-ring was being dug out carefully rather than admired.

The root she had used lay just below the surface. It was thicker near the tree and badly stripped where the paving had shifted against it. The mason wrapped that place in damp sacking, more out of respect than knowledge. Aadhya asked him not to pack the earth too tightly over it.

He listened. That almost undid her again.

The tree had lost a whole patch of leaves on its riverward side. Its branches there looked thin enough for the afternoon sky to show through. The new tulsi shoot stood upright, alive, with a brown line where it had bent.

She had not made a road from nothing. She could see, now, what had carried the road and what the carrying had taken.

Vedant set the recovered brass tongue on the steel plate her father had provided. He had wrapped its cut cord around a stick so nobody would touch the bare end.

"Paani band nahi kiya tha," he said. "Doosri taraf ka dabaav badhaya. Humne bypass khola toh usko bhi pakad liya."

Aadhya followed his finger along the peeled metal. A row of little dents marked its underside. Their spacing changed halfway down.

"Tune kiya tha?" Ira asked.

She had arrived with two volunteers and a stack of diversion notices. Her pencil was tied to a strip of cloth so she would not keep dropping it.

"Dobara set hua," Vedant said. "Kab, abhi nahi kahunga."

He did not call it a trap built for Aadhya. The evidence was not yet that kind. A fitting had altered under pressure, and their attempted repair had been anticipated. That was enough to make the recovered piece dangerous.

Ira made a copy of the marks without opening her second sight. The line wandered once. She left the error visible and labelled it.

Rudra watched from the edge of the group, his sleeve rolled low over the new seam.

"Aaj raat mat try karna," Aadhya told him.

He looked offended. "Main paani peene aaya tha."

She moved the lota towards him, then almost knocked it over with her numb thumb.

He caught it by the rim. Neither of them commented.

After a moment he said, "Jo naam bola tumne—"

She waited.

"Yaad tha?"

Aadhya looked at her palms. She could remember the shape of the work more clearly than the saying of the words: two hands receiving the same strain, the root's far end no longer impossibly far away.

"Us waqt sahi laga," she said. "Abhi bolungi toh sirf shabd honge."

He accepted that. He of all people knew the difference between knowing a count and being able to live inside it.

---

Her father found her at the haveli before evening.

He brought no flowers and asked no one what the district was calling her. He set a cup on the table, saw her try to grip its handle, and put a small bowl beside it instead.

Aadhya stared at the bowl.

"Dono haath se," he said.

She lifted it between the heels of her palms. Warm tea touched her mouth. The effort required such care that she could not speak until she had set it down.

"Plate wahan reh gayi."

"Le aayenge."

"Uspe woh cheez rakhi hai."

"Toh baad mein le aayenge."

He sat while she finished. When the tea cooled, he did not offer to replace it. She drank what he had made.

Outside, Veer was arguing with a volunteer who wanted to repaint the diversion sign with a new name for the crossing. Ira was making him keep the practical directions legible. Sitara had gone to check the tree's exposed root before the light failed.

Aadhya could hear all of them. For once she did not have to go and help.

Her father untied the empty sling. The knot he had made that morning lay cut in the middle of the cloth.

He smoothed it flat with his thumb.

---

At dusk the crossing received its first official notice. ROAD CLOSED. EAST LANE OPEN. NO CARTS AFTER DARK.

Below it, in smaller writing, Aadhya Sharma had signed for the placement of the rope. Her letters were awkward because she had used her left hand.

The rumours travelled faster than the notice. At the milk stall they said a tree had lifted the road. At the well they said a girl had lain underneath it. The mason, asked for the fourth time, said that he had seen two hands on the ground and people walking past. He would swear to that much.

Farther downriver, at the Machli bend, the iron landing-gate had been checked and tied off before the lamps were lowered for Dhruv's night lesson.

Its wheel was locked. Its handle was empty.

Nevertheless, as the first star showed above the oxbow, the gate's counterweight rose one notch.

Then another.
