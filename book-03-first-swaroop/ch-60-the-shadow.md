# CHAPTER SIXTY — THE SHADOW

---

> *"The story said they raised four suns over the city.*
> *The sweets came in a cardboard box. That is the part I saw."*
>
> *— relief-kitchen tallybook, margin copied into the winter report*

---

The nurse ended the visit while Ram still had a word left.

Rudra saw it waiting in the movement of his father's mouth. He bent a little closer, then saw the effort the next breath required and stopped.

"Baad mein," he said.

Ram's fingers rested against his hand. When the nurse eased the chair back, Rudra lifted his own fingers slowly enough for his father to feel him going.

Not slipping away. Letting the visit end.

"Bahar hoon."

Ram looked at him until the doorway took the bed from view.

---

Vedant was waiting at the marked line. He took the chair without asking Rudra to report in front of the other families.

Aadhya had fallen asleep sitting up, her dressed hands apart on her knees. Veer watched Rudra's face over the sling. Ira turned towards the wheels.

"Baat hui?" Veer asked.

Rudra nodded.

"Haath hilaaya," he said.

The change in Veer's face was small enough that someone passing them might not have seen it. Aadhya opened her eyes at his voice.

"Khud?"

"Khud."

She leaned back without taking her eyes off him. Her shoulders lowered as though somebody had finally set down a weight she had not been able to reach.

Ira asked whether he had been awake for the whole visit.

"Haan. Thak gaye. Nurse ne bhej diya."

"Achha kiya," she said.

It was the first answer which made him almost smile.

Vedant rolled the chair a little farther, leaving the doorway clear. When the others had settled, Rudra told him the rest quietly: Ram believed there was someone behind Aegis, someone who had been waiting a very long time. He gave him no name because his father had given none.

Vedant listened without looking towards the door as if another question were reason enough to go back through it.

"Aur bataya?"

"Nahi."

"Toh abhi itna hi. Sitara ko main bataaunga. Unse poochhne koi andar nahi jaayega."

Rudra looked down at his father's touch still remembered against his skin.

"Aapko pata hai?"

"Jo nahi pata uska jawaab banaakar nahi doonga. Ram jab bolne laayak honge, unse baat karenge. Doctor se ladkar nahi."

It was not an answer to the thing waiting beyond Aegis.

It was an answer about what they would do this morning. Rudra could use that one.

---

A blood-donation announcement sounded from the hospital desk.

Aadhya began to rise.

The nurse coming down the corridor looked at her hands, then at all four of them.

"Aap log nahi. Bilkul nahi."

Aadhya sat back before the second sentence was finished.

Vedant went to the desk to ask what the blood bank needed and who could be sent there for screening. He came back with the correct contact number, not a promise that any willing pair of arms would do.

"Yeh ghar pe bhej sakte hain," he said. "Bina patient ki photo ke."

Veer asked him to send it to his family. Aadhya gave him a family number to call directly rather than forwarding an alarming message without context. Ira dictated a short explanation while the nurse checked the dressing at Aadhya's left palm.

Rudra listened to them choosing exact words. Someone in this building needed something none of them could safely give. It did not mean there was nothing they could do.

Then the nurse separated them for the rest they had been avoiding.

Ira would stay for further eye observation. Aadhya needed the hands raised, not hanging down all morning. Veer's family had found a room near the hospital; a porter would take him to the vehicle. Vedant would go with him and return.

"Main bhi wapas aaunga," Veer told Rudra.

"Pehle sona."

"Tum bol rahe ho?"

Rudra looked towards the nurse.

"Inhone bola. Main forward kar raha hoon."

Veer breathed out a laugh and put his good hand against the sore ribs before it could become another.

The porter turned his chair. He did not promise to arrive walking.

---

They put Rudra on an observation bed where he could keep the foot raised and someone could check the reopened wounds. Sitara knew which bed. Dadi knew which bed. The critical-care desk had Vedant's number and the place where they could find him without making a boy stay awake to be reachable.

He had to hear that twice before he would lie back.

"Papa—"

"Kuch badla toh bulaayenge," Sitara said. "Tumhare sone se unki dekhbhaal band nahi hogi."

She drew the curtain enough to take the corridor light out of his eyes, leaving the nurse's side open.

Rudra watched her hand leave the cloth.

On the road he had been unable to keep his fingers around the stretcher. Beside the bed he had finally been able to let his father's hand go without believing that was the same as losing him.

He lay still. The ankle ached under its raised support. His throat hurt when he swallowed. Beyond the curtain a trolley rattled across a floor joint and carried on.

Sleep came without asking him to enter the bell's dark interior.

For once, there was only a tired body on a bed, and people awake nearby who would call if he was needed.

---

When Rudra woke, the daylight had moved across the curtain.

His ankle had not improved enough to forget it. The wrist still pulled when he reached for the cup; a nurse moved it closer. He drank, then asked the question she had been expecting.

There had been no further fall in Ram's blood pressure for several hours. The drain stayed in. The injured lung still needed watching. He would remain in critical care that night.

"Better ka matlab ghar nahi," she said before Rudra could turn the first fact into somewhere it had not reached.

He nodded. Asked when Rukmini could receive another update.

"Unko diya hai. Aapko bhi wahi."

For once, two people had heard the same answer without either needing to soften it for the other.

---

Sitara came while the nurse was checking the foot.

Rukmini wanted to see Ram. Her own doctor had allowed a brief visit in a chair, with the shoulder supported, if critical care would accept it. No walk down the corridor. No leaving the hospital afterwards.

The nurse telephoned the unit. One visitor at a time still meant one.

"Main bahar rahoonga," Rudra said.

Sitara took that answer to his mother.

He was already waiting by the unit when they brought her. Her chair moved slowly over the joint in the floor. She held her good hand clear of the armrest until it had crossed, then set it down again. Dadi walked beside her with a cloth bag she would not let anyone else carry.

"Khaaya?" Rukmini asked him.

"Thoda."

"Soya?"

"Haan."

She studied his face long enough to decide he had not borrowed the answer from someone else.

"Tum?" he asked.

"Maine bhi."

Dadi made a small sound which meant she could provide quantities if required.

For the first time that day, Rukmini smiled. It vanished when a deeper breath pulled at the shoulder, but it had been there.

The nurse took her chair at the marked line. Sitara stayed outside beside Rudra.

He watched until the door closed. He did not ask to follow.

---

Dadi sat on the bench and set her bag on the free place beside her.

Inside were plain clothes, a comb, the small ordinary preparations with which she had come to resist a hospital's power to turn everyone into the worst moment of their day. She took out a clean shirt for Rudra and found he could not raise his arm enough to change without help.

"Nurse ko bolenge," she said, folding it again. "Koi jaldi nahi."

He ran one fingertip along the crease.

"Papa ka shirt kaatna pada."

"Kapda tha."

"Rakh diya hai."

"Theek. Baad mein dekhenge."

She did not demand that he throw it away to prove he understood what mattered. She had brought another shirt for Ram too. It stayed folded beneath the first.

A phone rang at the other end of the corridor. A man answered it with a cheer, then covered his mouth and lowered his voice. Someone had been found in another hospital. Rudra could not hear who.

Dadi listened until the man's words changed into relieved scolding. Then she looked back at the closed unit door.

They let the stranger keep his good news without trying to borrow it as a forecast.

---

Rukmini came out before the nurse had to ask her twice.

Her right hand lay open in her lap. The left stayed supported where it had been put. She looked towards Rudra, and the porter brought her chair near his.

"Dekha?" he asked.

She nodded.

"Bole?"

"Thoda."

A breath, careful of the neck.

"Jis ghar ke aangan se tum dono aaye. Un logon ka poochh rahe the."

Rudra looked down. The householder had opened both doors so the wounded could pass. One had fallen from the cracked wall afterwards. He had not yet told his mother that part.

"Darwaaza toot gaya," he said. "Ghar khada hai."

"Pata hai. Sitara ne bataya."

"Deewar bhi. Abhi andar nahi jaana. Dhruv ne roka hai."

"Unko bhi bola."

The family who had opened their courtyard could not sleep safely in it tonight. The house would have to wait for people who knew how to make it safe.

"Kya kaha?" Rudra asked.

Rukmini's fingers moved once on the cloth over her knees.

"Kehte hain, lakdi achhi hai. Phenkna mat."

Rudra could see him saying it, even with the tube at his nose and the nurse limiting each sentence. Still noticing whose doorway had been used to get him out.

"Ghar waale se poochhenge," he said. "Unko kya chahiye."

Rukmini nodded. It was a question they could send through Dhruv, not a task either patient had to get up and perform.

The porter turned her chair back towards her bay.

Rudra watched the supported shoulder disappear through the adjoining doorway. Then he laid Dadi's clean shirt across his knees and asked the nurse for help changing into it.

The torn one could go in a separate packet. It did not need to remain against his skin to prove that the night had happened.

---

Dhruv arrived in the late afternoon with dust dried white at his ankles.

He had handed the wreck to a relief watch before leaving. The frame had not moved. The opening had not returned. Someone qualified to assess the buildings was coming; until then, the connected houses would stay empty. The residents had been found places under intact roofs, not told to be grateful for a dangerous room.

He gave Vedant the names of the watchmen who could be reached. Then he sat down because Sitara had seen his palm.

The reopened rope-burns were raw beneath the ring. He took it off for the nurse to clean the wounds, leaving it on the tray where he could see it. Afterwards he put it in his pouch rather than force its edge across the fresh dressing.

Rudra waited until the fresh dressing was secured before asking about the people brought up after them.

Some had reached other hospitals. Some families were still moving between casualty desks with a name written on a scrap of paper. Others had been sent to the mortuary.

Dhruv gave no total. A number guessed beside a bed would travel faster than a correction.

The boy from the grain-store was with his mother. The injured leg had reached a doctor without its improvised supports being stripped away on the road. The watchwoman had found out which ward, so the family did not have to start the search again each time someone asked.

Rudra nodded. He would tell Ram that part when a visit was allowed.

"Unke ghar ka poochh rahe the," he said. "Aangan waale."

"Pata hai. Sitara ka message aaya."

Dhruv looked towards the entrance.

The householder had come up with the relief van. He was helping unload blankets. Someone called him in before he could leave with the empty crates.

---

He stood at the end of the corridor holding a cardboard sweet box by its string.

The towel which had been over his shoulder at the ghat was gone. In its place lay a cheap new gamchha, still folded along the shop crease. He asked about Ram before saying anything about the house.

Rudra told him the current medical news. He kept the words from the end of the visit within the small circle which had heard them.

"Aapke darwaaze ki lakdi—" he began.

The man shook his head.

"Pehle unki pasli jud jaaye. Lakdi bhaagi nahi jaa rahi."

He would speak to the building people when they came. Tonight his family had a place with a relative. He did not need four injured visitors turning up to help carry furniture.

"Yeh kis liye?" Rudra asked, looking at the box.

"Rasoi se. Sabke liye. Bahar dena. Andar dene nahi dete toh wapas mat bhej dena, kisi aur ko de dena."

"Abhi mithai?"

The man settled the box on the bench instead of making Rudra carry its weight.

"Beta, jeet hui hai. Sab theek nahi hua. Dono baat sach hain."

Rudra looked towards the adjoining bay where his mother was resting. The critical-care door was farther down.

The box stayed on the bench between them.

From beside it came the smell of cardamom.

"Rasoi mein pehle chai bani thi," the man said. "Phir kisi ne bola, meetha bhi. Kaam karte-karte kha liya sabne. Tum log bhi kha lena."

A kitchen had kept working after the road cleared, and somebody there had remembered they were allowed a different taste in their mouths.

Rudra thanked him.

The man asked which desk could tell him whether volunteers were needed for the next van. Vedant took him there. He left the sweets behind.

---

Veer came back before evening visiting time, in the same sling, with his mother insisting on handing the food she had brought to an uninjured adult. Sleep had taken the grey edge off his face. It had not fixed the arm, knee or ribs.

Ira was brought down after her next eye check. The covered left eye remained covered; the light still troubled the right. She chose a chair where she would not face the window directly.

A porter brought Aadhya in a chair, her hands resting on a folded pillow. She had been told to keep the numb fingers protected, including from grips which felt harmless because she could not feel them at all.

Veer saw the sweet box.

"Kisne bheja?"

Rudra told him.

Veer looked at each of them, then at the unopened lid.

"Toh permission lo. Kholo."

It took a few questions. The food would stay out of treatment areas. The visitor courtyard was open; there was a level ramp, a bench and space for the chairs without blocking anyone. Those allowed down could go for a short sitting after their checks, with someone to bring them back.

Rudra asked whether the desk could still find him there.

The nurse showed him the courtyard through the glass at the far end of the passage.

"Dikhenge aap. Har jagah nazar aana zaroori nahi."

He followed her pointing hand. For a moment he could see the place only as a distance from his father's bed.

Then Ira asked which side had shade. Aadhya asked whether they could keep the pillow. Veer asked Vedant to bring something to open the string.

Rudra looked again.

There was room for all four of them.

---

The ramp ended beside a square of open sky.

A neem grew inside the courtyard wall. Its lower branches had been trimmed away from the windows; above them, the leaves moved in an ordinary evening breeze. The evening light stayed on one high branch after it had left the bench.

Aadhya watched it while the chairs were placed.

"Udhar theek?" Vedant asked Ira.

She angled her uncovered side towards the others and nodded.

Veer's chair went beside the bench. Rudra's faced the passage glass, where a nurse could signal without making someone run out looking for him. Aadhya sat with the pillow beneath both hands.

Dhruv put the box on a low stool between them. He had asked for the string to be cut at the desk; the lid came away without requiring anyone to prove a grip.

Inside were small besan laddoos, uneven enough to have been rolled in a kitchen still busy with other work.

Veer looked at them with solemn attention.

"Bohot kam hain."

"Sabke liye hain," Ira said.

"Maine bhi wahi bola. Bahut kam hain."

Aadhya laughed first. It surprised her so much that she looked at Rudra as if he had done something to cause it.

He smiled before deciding whether it was the right time.

The laddoos went round on paper plates. Vedant broke Rudra's into smaller pieces with a clean spoon so he would not have to open the sore mouth too far. Ira asked for her plate to be put against the chair's right arm and found it from there herself.

Veer's mother had sent extra food with him. He studied the size of the pieces being divided and asked whether that counted as an independent contribution or a claim on another sweet.

"Receipt laao," Ira said.

"Maa ki receipt nahi hoti."

"Toh claim reject."

This time the laugh reached all four of them. Veer caught his ribs, then lifted the same hand until they let him breathe. Aadhya wiped one eye with the uninjured part of her wrist. Rudra's mouth hurt. The sound still came out.

Dhruv sat farther along the bench, keeping the route clear. Vedant went back for the tea they had been allowed and brought water as well. Sitara came to the courtyard door after checking on Rukmini and stood there until Rudra saw her.

"Soyi hain," she said. "Dadi paas hain."

He nodded.

The tea was warm by the time the cups reached them. Nobody minded.

---

For a little while they talked about food.

Veer maintained that canteen potatoes had developed a personal dislike for him. Aadhya said that was because he had spent ten minutes interrogating a nurse about which meal came next. Ira offered to keep a list of enemies beginning with the potatoes, provided somebody else held the pencil.

"Tumhara register?" Veer asked.

"Aaj chhutti."

He looked impressed by the scale of the announcement.

"Written mein de do."

She turned the good eye towards him.

"Rejected," he said before she had to answer.

Rudra took a small piece of the sweet and let it soften against his tongue. For the first time since the ghat he could taste cardamom without iron behind it.

Across the courtyard, two attendants shared the other bench. One took a phone call, listened, and held the handset out so his companion could speak. The evening visiting queue kept moving through the passage beyond the glass.

The four had been given these minutes. Rudra stopped using every one of them to listen for the end.

He put his plate down.

"Hum jeet gaye," he said.

The words sounded strange without someone immediately adding what remained to be done.

Aadhya looked at him. Then at the other two.

"Haan," she said.

Veer lifted his cup an inch from its place. Ira put the fingertips of her free hand beside it, too far away to risk knocking hot tea into his sling. Aadhya's left hand rested against the edge of her plate; the protected right stayed on the pillow.

Rudra raised his water.

"Darwaaza band hai," he said.

"Band hi rahega," Veer answered, and then glanced at Dhruv. "Wahi waala. Hospital ka nahi."

Dhruv nodded gravely.

"Achha clarification hai. Warna duty badh jaati."

Veer laughed too soon, regretted it, and shook his head at them all. That only made Aadhya start again.

The cups came down safely. A little tea had spilled on the stool. Vedant wiped it away with the corner of a paper napkin while they were still laughing.

---

The last laddoo sat at the box's corner.

Four people noticed it.

Ira waited. Aadhya looked away with an innocence which did not survive Veer looking directly at her. Rudra watched the three of them preparing their claims and felt something loosen in his chest which no instruction to relax had managed all day.

Vedant put a clean spoon through the middle of the sweet. Then across it.

"Problem solve."

Veer stared at the four small pieces.

"Aapne dosti mein intervention kiya hai."

"Successful?"

"Review kal milega."

Rudra took his quarter. Aadhya took hers with the left hand, slowly enough to keep the plate still. Ira found the last piece after somebody named its position. Veer ate his as though making an important point about compromise.

Above them, the light left the neem's highest leaf.

None of them asked it to stay.

---

The courtyard lamps came on one at a time.

A watchman paused at the door with a bundle of folded sheets under his arm. He recognized Veer first, then looked from the sling to the other three.

"Subah se sun rahe hain," he said. "Chaar suraj nikle the ghat pe."

Veer looked down into the empty box.

"Chaar laddoo bhi mushkil se mile."

The watchman laughed. He had been at the hospital through the night and had heard several versions of how the river had stopped and the city had risen, none from anyone who could tell him which lane had opened for the ambulances.

"Phir kya hua tha?" he asked.

"Log nikle," Rudra said. "Gate band hua. Baaki Dhruv se poochh lena. Inke paas sahi jagah ka naam hai."

Dhruv asked the man which relief post had sent him. They stepped aside to exchange the useful parts of what each had heard.

The rumour would go on without waiting for permission. Somewhere by the next morning the laddoos would become a feast, the courtyard a square filled with people, four tired friends a sight nobody had actually seen. Rudra could leave that to the city for tonight.

The watchman would carry the correct ambulance route with him too.

---

"Photo nahi li," Aadhya said after he had gone.

Ira turned a little towards her.

"Bura laga?"

"Nahi. Khaate waqt yaad hi nahi aaya."

"Evidence gaya," Veer said.

He tipped the empty box towards them with his good fingers. A scatter of crumbs moved into the corner.

"Yeh hai," Rudra said.

"Kal kahenge maine sab kha liya."

"Tumne claim toh kiya tha."

"Tum log bhoolna kuch nahi."

Ira looked round the small sitting: the footrest, the sling, Aadhya's hands raised on a hospital pillow, Rudra in the clean shirt Dadi had brought, the dressing still visible above its collar.

"Nahi bhoolenge," she said.

For a moment the joke rested beside everything else she meant. Then Veer lowered the box before the crumbs could get into his lap, and she told him he had almost destroyed his own evidence.

Rudra laughed with them.

He would remember the other things. There were people in this building who would need a name found, a journey made, money or blood or simply somebody willing to sit through the waiting. There was a frightened part of him still listening to his father's unfinished warning. None of it had left because they had opened a sweet box.

But this had happened too.

The four had broken the machine. His mother was asleep with Dadi beside her. His father's hand had answered his own. His friends had come back to the same small patch of evening, carrying all their damage, and made room for him.

He did not owe the darkness the laugh as well.

---

Sitara looked at the clock above the courtyard door.

"Paanch minute," she said. "Phir wapas."

Veer pretended to calculate whether another box could be found within that allowance. Vedant offered him a second cup of water instead. Ira leaned back where the light would not strike her uncovered eye, and Aadhya shifted the pillow without trying to make the numb fingers help.

Rudra rested his empty cup on the stool.

Tomorrow there would be dressings and questions. The ankle would still need lifting onto a support. A held form did not make him a master of the force that had passed through him; neither did its absence make that one victory a mistake.

"Kal milte hain?" he asked.

Veer looked offended.

"Review dena hai mujhe. Bhool gaye?"

Aadhya nodded. Ira said they would have to ask which visiting hours allowed a complaint that long.

The laugh went softly through the courtyard, beneath the neem, along the glass where the hospital's lights were coming on.

Above that glass rose the outer wall. Beyond it lay the narrow street, the closed shutters of a pharmacy, a stair fixed to the building opposite, rising to a flat roof.

On the roof, between a water tank and the parapet, stood a figure with ten overlapping silhouettes.

The streetlamp below threw one band of light across the concrete. At the figure's feet, the outlines crossed. At its shoulders, they parted, each a fraction from the next.

Below, Veer said something else. Aadhya laughed.

The figure's head inclined towards the courtyard.

A whisper crossed the parapet.

"The cycle is ending. And the child of Ardhanarishwar has finally awakened."

---

In Sutala, white light lay along veins of mineral in the black stone walls.

Pillars rose beyond it into the high chamber's darkness. At the far end stood the throne, carved from a single piece of obsidian.

It was empty.

Before it, the tall, gaunt Lord lowered himself to his knees. His robes spread over the rock. The sword which had once rested across his knee lay flat beside him, its hilt to his right.

He bowed his head before the vacant seat.

"He is ready, my Lord."

For a moment there was no sound in the chamber.

Then a voice answered from the darkness.

"Good. Let him come."
