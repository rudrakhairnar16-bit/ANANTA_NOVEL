# CHAPTER FIFTY-SIX — THE BREATH THAT WAS HIS

---

> *"The lamp arrived separately from the woman. In the first report they were entered on the same line.*
> *Strike that out. We carried a person. The lamp was delivered later."*
>
> *— emergency transport register, Manikarnika, correction in the receiving attendant's hand*

---

"Maa. Neeche."

Rudra did not raise his voice. There was no time to find a louder one.

Rukmini bent, putting the lamp on the threshold. Ram saw his son's face and lowered the stretcher's upper end. Behind the patient, the driver crouched with both padded rails still in his hands.

At the portable desk, the dark sleeve moved.

The lever went down.

Every iron saddle across the service lane took its load at once. The replacement boards sprang upwards. One struck Rudra across the shins and put him on his knees before he reached the door.

The sighting pointer passed over his mother's bent head.

A line opened in the wall where her throat had been.

White dust fell onto her hair. She put a hand over the patient's face before looking at the cut stone above them.

Then the rings beside the winch turned.

The river between them ceased to be visible.

Not hidden by smoke. Rudra could still see smoke crossing the gap, thin blue threads from the funeral fires farther along the ghat. Where the threads entered the iron frame, they went down instead of across. They disappeared against a strip of darkness no wider than his wrist.

The strip widened.

Cold came out of it, dry enough to carry the smell of old ash without the river's damp. A loosened bolt fell from the frame. Rudra heard it strike stone below, much farther below than the steps could possibly go.

Vedant stood up from the cable saddle.

"Darwaaza khul gaya. Peechhe ki gali khaali karo."

The command travelled away from the river. Not a call to attack. Room for the injured, first.

At the desk a punched card dropped into a receiving tray. The man behind the lid moved one finger along its edge and set a second handle at an angle.

Water rose from the drain between Rudra and the grain-store door.

He knew the first barrier had been farther down the passage. This one stood across the few feet he still had to cross. A flat wet ridge, knee-high, holding loose grit in its face. He touched a broom handle to it.

The wood came back with a clean bite taken out of it.

Ram had seen.

"Haath mat lagana."

Rudra threw the broken handle aside. "Andar jagah hai?"

"Patient ke peechhe. Bas."

The rear room had been safe because it had no river-facing window. Now pressure was entering through the service channel beneath it. The grain bins shivered against their wall. A sack seam opened, spilling wheat across the stone.

Rukmini moved the sleeping cloth away from the patient's mouth. The woman had begun to gasp.

"Meri taraf dekho," she said. "Pair seedha hai. Humne moda nahi."

The patient stared at her, trying to believe the part she could not feel through pain.

Rudra gripped the edge of a sound paving slab. He wanted to tear a path through the barrier with his bare hands. Under his collar the gold tightened at the thought; the four-cadence offered its first short push.

He released the slab.

Not because he had no strength. Because he had seen what an answer did when the instruments were waiting for it.

Behind him Veer got down from the supply cart. His left arm stayed in its sling. He planted his good foot, lowered the bad knee onto the rolled sack, and set a pale spear against the broken board nearest the cable.

"Rasta dikhata hoon," he said. "Chhota sa."

The joined bands across his shoulders lit. A narrow protective curve pressed against the wet ridge.

For a moment it made a gap.

At the frame, the plate facing the bank-side guide turned one slot. The ridge folded round the curve, closing over it from both sides.

Veer pulled his hand back before the returning edge could reach his wrist.

His right elbow was bleeding through its new dressing.

Ira heard the scrape and asked Sitara to move her towards the terrace wall, not towards the water. Her left eye remained covered. With her right she watched the exposed plates turn.

"Shield ki jagah yaad hai isko," she said.

Rudra looked at the man behind the desk lid. The same hand that had adjusted his mother's sightline rested beside the second handle. Its middle finger wore a guard of polished metal, the sort used to guide paper into a punch.

The receiving tray carried two stamped words: RIGHT HAND.

No name. No face offered in return.

Rudra got one foot beneath him. "Seedhi taraf se nahi jaa sakte?"

Dhruv was already above the first service stair, holding back the crowd with his body. Beyond him, mourners were trying to lift a bier without tipping it. The riverward steps had acquired the same standing ridges of water.

"Wahan bhi," he called.

A boy slipped beside the bier. Dhruv caught his upper arm and put him back with the men carrying it. Nobody abandoned what they had brought to the ghat.

Rudra turned back to his mother.

She had wedged a folded blanket beneath the patient's shoulders. Now she took off her own shawl and handed it to Ram to pad the rail where the bandages had begun slipping.

"Lamp bahar pahuncha do," she told Rudra, nodding at the little flame between them. "Pairon mein aa raha hai."

A household instruction. Clear the thing someone might trip over.

He found that he could obey it. Using the unbroken end of the broom, he drew the lamp round the edge of the ridge to the safe side.

The pointer at the desk did not follow it.

It remained aimed into the room.

---

Aadhya could see a way beneath the ridge. She could not yet see a way to make it safe.

The channel ran under the threshold, not through its centre. On the wall side, a peepal root from the neighbouring well had lifted a corner of the old paving. She had caught her sandal on it coming in. Now the loose stone rose and fell with each small adjustment at the frame.

The instruments were using the wet channel. The root occupied a little strip of ground beside it.

She knelt there.

Grit entered the dressing beneath her right palm. The thumb and index finger still gave her no warning of pressure; she had to look at them to make certain they were not folded underneath. She moved them straight with her left hand, then put down the heel of the palm.

"Is patthar ko haath mat lagana," she told Rudra.

He stopped reaching for it.

Green lit the skin above her bandages. The change travelled through her arms and settled across her shoulders, the fuller reach she had found at the west threshold. It did not bring back sensation to the two fingers.

She felt the root's grip under the paving. Small. Old. Not enough to lift the room.

Enough, perhaps, to stop one stone dropping while people passed it.

"Veer. Ek saans ka source de sakte ho?"

He shifted his right elbow off the bloody cart rail. "Kahan?"

She pointed to the low corner of the barrier. "Wahan nahi. Ira ki taraf."

Ira understood before Aadhya finished.

"Mujhe angle bolna padega. Main depth nahi dekh paa rahi."

Sitara put a folded sack beneath Ira's knees. She did not lift the eye covering or suggest that need had made the injury less real.

Vedant moved to the riverward side of the channel with his basin. Dhruv stayed with the people above the steps. Ram listened from inside the door, hands on the padded rails.

The whole opening was narrower than a man's shoulders. They would feed the stretcher through lengthways, keeping its long edge close to the wall. The waiting cart could remain where it was.

"Pehle maa-bete ko dekhne do," Rukmini said.

A volunteer lifted the patient's boy high enough for her to see him beyond the threshold. The woman reached towards him and nearly twisted her injured leg.

"Seedha," Ram said. Not sharply. A reminder.

She lay back.

Aadhya let the root take the paving stone's weight. A line of warm strain crossed her back. At the neighbouring well the peepal's nearest leaves began to tremble, drawing the attention of a man waiting there with an empty bucket.

"Ped ke neeche se hato," she called.

He stepped away at once.

Veer's pale curve opened over his good hand. Ira turned her uncovered eye towards it. A second curve appeared near the ground, the same width, the same thin interruption at its lower edge.

"Wall se ek ungli," Aadhya said. "Bas. Ab ghumao."

The reflection turned edge-on to the wet ridge. Rather than try to hold the whole barrier back, it divided the narrow place where the pressure returned into the channel.

Vedant tipped his basin. The little moving edge of his water caught the released trickle and led it into the empty drain on his side.

The ridge lowered by a hand's breadth.

"Ab," Aadhya said.

Ram and the driver lifted the rails. Rudra took the leading end as it emerged, keeping his hands on wood, away from the wet face of the boundary. The blanket went through. The woman's feet, braced straight beneath the shawl. Her hips.

She made a sound when the stretcher tilted.

"Seedha hai," Rukmini told her again. "Humne chhoda nahi."

Aadhya felt the root bark strip against a stone. Her right palm was bleeding into its cloth, invisible until the red reached the edge.

She kept looking at the two numb fingers. Still straight. Still clear of the opening.

At the desk a narrow plate turned towards the divided return. The frame's note changed pitch.

"Jaldi," Ira said.

Her reflected curve flickered. Veer's source had begun to close as his ribs resisted the sustained breath.

The patient's shoulders cleared the threshold.

Rudra and the driver took her weight fully. Ram let go of the upper rail only when the volunteer on the far side said he had it. They moved towards the watch post, where the boy was calling to his mother.

Aadhya released the root before it tore out of the paving.

"Band."

Veer lowered his hand. Ira's reflection went with it. Sitara caught her shoulder as she leaned too far to follow the stretcher with her good eye.

For one second the doorway was clear.

Ram turned back for Rukmini.

The opening in the rings gave a sound like a heavy breath drawn through stone. The grain-store roof shifted towards the river. A section of its inner lintel came loose where the first shot had weakened the wall below it.

Ram got his shoulder beneath the falling end.

"Rukmini. Neeche se niklo."

She bent to come under his arm.

The ridge snapped upright between them and the lane.

Aadhya reached for the root again. The green answered; the stripped bark did not. Soil loosened under her palm. She stopped before she could pull the small living hold apart.

At the portable desk, the Right Hand moved the sighting handle rather than the main lever.

Rukmini put her hand against the wall to steady herself. A narrow red line appeared across the outer edge of her shoulder.

Her fingers opened.

---

Rudra saw her hand open before he saw the blood.

For a moment the two things refused to belong together. That was the hand which had put food aside for him. Which had rested on his chest before the drill. It should have been able to close again.

Blood darkened the fabric from the outside of her left shoulder towards the collarbone. She pressed her right hand over it and backed against the inner wall.

The sighting handle moved a little farther.

Another thin cut opened above the first.

"Ruko," Rudra said.

He did not know to whom.

Ram pulled a loose packing batten from beside the grain bin and drove one end beneath the lintel. The beam settled onto it, and for half a second he could move. He reached Rukmini, pulled her away from the first line, and pressed a clean dressing from the stack by the bench against the bleeding shoulder.

The force followed.

It struck the batten where the beam rested. Wood split. Ram got his shoulder under the lintel again before the falling stone could reach her.

The bandage across his palm had opened. He wiped that hand down his shirt and gripped the broken upper length of the batten.

"Kapda dabaye raho," he told her. "Meri taraf mat mudna."

Rukmini stayed facing the lane. Her right hand was red to the wrist.

Rudra put his right foot into the wet ridge.

Pain cut across his ankle. He pulled back with blood running into his sandal, furious that the body could still retreat without consulting him.

Veer caught him by the back of his shirt.

"Seedha nahi."

"Chhod."

Veer let go. Not because he agreed. Because keeping him by force would take the only good arm with which he could still help.

He shoved the timber brace into the ridge instead. The wet edge scored it, slowed, then began eating through. For a moment the solid wood interrupted its line.

Rudra lunged through the opening.

At the frame, the fourth plate jolted on its broken mount.

The four-cadence seized his chest. His right shoulder locked behind him while the left tried to go forward. He hit the threshold on one hip, close enough now to see the grain dust stuck to his mother's blood.

Not close enough to touch her.

Dhruv came down from the upper stair after handing the crowd to the watchmen. He set his angle-ring against the sound jamb outside the room. A thin pale line crossed the falling lintel.

Ram gained an inch beneath it. Enough to shift the splintered support. Not enough to leave the beam unsupported.

"Neeche ka patthar," Dhruv told Aadhya.

She helped a volunteer push a dry wedge beneath the remaining foot of the batten. No green this time. The root beside the channel had already begun lifting out of its bed. Pulling harder would bring down the paving beneath all of them.

Ira sat against the terrace wall with Sitara's hand on her shoulder. She kept the injured eye covered. With her good one she watched the sighting bars.

"Rudra. Gardan wala handle. Doosra."

He could see it. Above the desk, the Right Hand's finger-guard flashed as the handle advanced over a short toothed scale.

No hurried movement. One tooth, then a pause while the reading settled. Another tooth.

The pressure at Rukmini's shoulder travelled upwards.

She pulled the blood-soaked cloth higher with her right hand. Her elbow shook. The cloth slipped.

Rudra tried the nine-breath allowance. Not to find a form. To get enough air to stand.

The first intake met the four halfway through.

Heat opened along the new line at the base of his neck. He tasted blood where he had bitten the inside of his mouth. The dark pressure gathered in his right hand, eager and useless while he could not turn the hand towards what he needed to stop.

He had failed this an hour ago. He could remember every instruction. Remembering did not make his lungs obey.

"Rudra," his mother said.

It was not a summons to become something. Her voice was low with pain. She was looking at the blood on his ankle.

Even then.

"Main aa raha hoon," he told her.

Ram thrust the broken length of batten across the moving line below her chin. The pressure bit into it. He held it with the bandaged hand while his shoulder kept the lintel from falling.

For a few seconds it held the edge away from her throat.

Rukmini could not lift her left arm. With the right she tried to keep pressure on the shoulder and could not manage both. Blood ran between her fingers. Her knees bent; the force at her neck kept her from sliding down the wall.

Rudra saw that it was holding her up.

The understanding emptied him.

He could not retreat to stop himself hurting someone. Could not let the failed outline break into an empty yard. There was no empty space left in front of him. His mother occupied it, and the next movement of that patient hand at the desk would cut through the wood.

The inside of the held bell closed round him for one blind instant.

He heard no answer there. No new number. No instruction he had missed during nineteen years of mornings.

Only the effort of drawing air, and the need to reach her before she could not draw any at all.

Outside, the broken wood began to split lengthways.

Ram drove his thumb into the opening to keep the two halves together.

The handle reached its last tooth.

Rukmini's mouth opened. The sound that came from it was not enough to be a breath.

---

Rudra took the air without finishing either count.

It hurt. The gold tightened as if the breath were tearing him out of an old fastening. The red-black pressure struck against it, found no agreement, and came through anyway.

This time it came through with him.

He had no other chest to give it. No uninjured body waiting beyond this one. He put his bleeding foot under his hip and rose towards his mother's open mouth.

A sound left his own, low and clear.

**"UNBOUND: JAAGRUT."**

Skin parted along the gold line above his collar. Blood ran into the shirt Rukmini had trimmed for him. The force in his hand turned his wrist until the old scar burned, then stopped turning it.

His fingers closed because he chose to close them.

Red-black gathered against his skin, dense enough that the lamp on the ground threw no light through it. Gold ran through that darkness in broken branching lines, outlining muscle, torn cloth, the flex of a hand. The colours crossed without becoming one colour. Where they met, the air gave a thin metallic shiver.

Veer took one involuntary step back.

Rudra heard it. The sound hurt somewhere the seam did not reach.

Then the wood at his mother's throat broke.

He put his forearm into the moving line.

The impact drove his new wound open. His feet slid across stone. For a moment he thought the line would take him with the wood, straighten both of them against his mother's neck and finish what the hand at the desk had begun.

He bent his elbow.

The red-black force thickened along it. Gold held the outline of the bend, bright enough for him to see the bones of his own fingers through the light. He could feel the two demands still fighting inside his ribs. Neither had learned the other's arithmetic.

His arm stayed where he put it.

The edge moved an inch away from Rukmini's throat.

Air entered her with a sound that made Ram shut his eyes for half a heartbeat.

"Neeche baitho," Rudra told her.

Her knees went. He caught the uninjured side of her back before her head could strike the wall. The shoulder cloth slipped; blood followed it, warm over the heel of his hand.

He had reached her. That had not closed the wound.

The force above his forearm tried to fold round it. A second line bit at the gold along his neck. He turned his whole body between the sighting desk and his mother, taking away the straight path instead of trying to become stronger along its length.

For one breath, his shadow covered her.

"Ab," he said.

Sitara came through the space behind his back. She had left Ira seated against the sound wall, within the watchwoman's reach. Now she put both hands beneath Rukmini's good shoulder and eased her through the doorway, keeping the injured arm from dragging.

Aadhya passed her a folded clean dressing from the aid-room parcel. She could not hold her own fingers steady; she would not trust the failing green inside an open wound. She pressed the spare cloth into Sitara's hand and made room for the work she could actually do.

Dhruv's pale line steadied the lintel while the volunteer drove the wedge the last inch under the broken prop.

"Hua," Dhruv said.

Ram took his shoulder out slowly. The stone held on wood and wedge. He came through beside his wife without testing how long the improvised support would last.

Rudra backed after them.

The pressure followed him to the threshold. He caught its narrow returning edge with his other hand, felt the red-black thicken against it, and pulled the two edges apart.

Not far. A man's width.

Enough for the last volunteer to get clear.

Then he turned the loose force towards the empty lower service steps.

Dust burst off the stone. A broken water pot went down the stair in fragments. Nobody stood there now; Dhruv had cleared that side before coming to the room.

The wet ridge across the doorway collapsed.

At the desk the sighting handle kicked against its stop. The hand above it lifted sharply. A dark fleck of blood appeared beside the dial, where the finger-guard had driven into skin.

The main lever remained down.

Between the rings the opening widened another fraction.

---

Rukmini lay on the supply cart with a rolled blanket beneath her good side.

Veer had pulled his own knee sack off it and folded the clean face uppermost. Sitara held the dressing against the shoulder while the watchwoman checked whether the bleeding had slowed. The blood was dark, steady, still much too much.

Rukmini drew another breath. Coughed. Opened her eyes.

Rudra crouched where she could see him.

The gold and darkness moved with him. Her gaze travelled across his torn collar, the changed shape of the light round his shoulders, the blood on his neck. He could not tell whether she was afraid of him or for him, and for one dreadful moment he needed the difference more than he needed air.

"Maa. Main—"

"Rudra," she said.

Hoarse. Painful. His name, with no hesitation in it.

He nodded too quickly.

Ram put a hand near the dressing, checked Sitara's grip and called for the waiting ambulance to be brought to the nearest open lane. His own palm was wet through the bandage. He wrapped it in the loose end of his shirt rather than take cloth from his wife.

Then he laid two fingers beside the dressing. His lips moved over words Rudra could not hear. Warmth passed beneath them. The steady flow into the cloth slowed, though the wound remained open. Ram's hand shook when he lifted it; his own raw palm was no better.

"Hospital. Abhi," he said.

Rudra reached for the cart handle. A little red-black pressure gathered around the wood. It began to creak.

He opened his hand at once.

"Tum nahi," Ram said.

For a moment Rudra could hear only the refusal.

Then his father put the handle in the driver's hands and moved himself between the cart and the river.

A place for each of them. Not a judgement on who was allowed to love her.

Rudra rose to stand beside him.

The form rose with him and held.

---

The cart could turn in the rear alley. Rukmini's legs did not need the long straight support the first patient had required. That small, cruel difference gave the rescuers a route.

A householder opened both doors of his inner courtyard to avoid the blocked corner. The watchwoman went first. Sitara stayed beside Rukmini's head; Aadhya helped the driver steer, pressing with her left palm. The patient's boy watched them pass from beside his mother's bench, no longer asking to go down the steps.

"Main saath hoon," Sitara told Ram.

He nodded without taking his eyes off his wife.

Rudra kept the space between the cart and the river clear. He could feel the pressure gathering against his back. It wanted the doorway again, the reference point where the lamp and the woman had stood. He moved sideways with the cart until the line fell against him rather than the turning wheels.

His cut ankle hurt with every step. The form carried weight through it; it did not make the torn skin whole.

The first volunteer through the courtyard stopped when Rudra's shadow crossed the paving. Fear moved across the man's face before recognition did.

Rudra lowered his hands.

"Raasta khaali rakho," he said.

The man moved. Not away from him. Ahead, to hold the second door.

Behind the last turn, the ambulance's waiting light flashed against a pale wall. The cart disappeared towards it with Sitara walking at its head.

Rudra listened until he could distinguish the driver's instructions from the ringing in his ears.

Then he turned towards the frame.

---

"Akele nahi," Ram said.

"Aap bhi nahi."

His father glanced at him. Neither made it into a challenge.

Vedant stood beside the opened service channel, keeping the released water below the level of the occupied lane. Dhruv had secured the lintel and returned to the river stair. The remaining mourners were moving onto the upper landing, carrying the bier level between them.

Veer stayed by Ira. His spear was gone. He had a length of ordinary wood across his knees and was using it to point out the exposed fittings without making her turn the covered eye towards them.

"Shoulder wala toot sakta hai," Ira called to Rudra. "Main frame ko mat kheenchna. Neeche sab judaa hai."

The sighting assembly faced the room. The larger nested rings stood behind it, anchored through cables that went under the steps. They were not the same target.

Rudra nodded.

The Right Hand reached for the sighting handle again.

A new line struck the stone in front of Rudra's feet.

He moved before it climbed.

Not a leap that took him over the whole stair. One hard step onto the dry strip beside the channel, a turn of the hip, the same small change of direction Ram had corrected through a thousand mornings. The new force arrived with the movement rather than dragging his shoulder behind it.

Stone cracked beneath his heel.

He shortened the next step.

Gold tightened across his ribs. The darker pressure pressed for a straight rush at the desk. He could feel how easy it would be to mistake that urgency for his own decision.

He went towards the sighting assembly instead.

One of the workmen swung a cable hook across his path. Rudra caught the shaft below the metal head, turned it down and let the man's own momentum take him to the ground. The force in Rudra's fingers cracked the shaft after the man had released it.

He stared at the split wood for half a heartbeat.

Then he put it down. Hands empty again.

At the assembly a polished lip opened towards his chest. He brought his forearm across it as the pressure struck.

The impact took him back a step. His shoulder jarred in its socket. Red-black thickened along the blow, threaded through with gold that held the shape of his bent elbow. The form did not split down the centre.

He was still there when the first impact ended.

He got his other hand beneath the sighting bracket.

Iron bent slowly. Not an effortless tearing. The bolts dragged through their slots; one came away with a piece of stone attached. The edge of the plate cut across his left knuckles. He kept the sharp face turned away from the lane and wrenched the assembly down onto the empty step beside him.

The sighting handle at the desk fell loose.

Its pointer struck the lower stop and stayed there.

The grain-store doorway was no longer under aim.

For one second Rudra heard the ordinary river beyond the opening: a boat-chain knocking, water against a hull. He had gained something real. A line no longer following the cart. A way his father could take back towards his mother when the stair was clear.

It was not the machine's defeat.

The main lever was still down.

---

The Right Hand took the damaged sighting card out of its slot. Blood from the finger-guard had marked one corner. He pressed that corner against the cut for a moment, folded it away from the reading surface, and laid the card flat.

Then he turned the fourth mount towards Rudra.

The split in its plate remained visible. Through it a sliver of the opening showed black against black.

Rudra felt his next breath catch at the base of his neck.

The new wound was bleeding again. Gold held close along its edges without closing them. Under it the four still beat against the longer allowance, each insisting on a rhythm his body had not learned to bear for long.

He planted his feet. Took air.

The needle on the fourth scale moved.

Ira sat up against the wall.

"Rudra. Hilna mat."

He went still.

The needle moved again.

Not with his arm. Not with his feet.

With his chest.

He could see the Right Hand making one small correction at the base of the plate. No new order, no lifted main lever. Just an adjustment to a reading that had been broken since the first failed attempt.

Rudra let the breath go.

Across the scale, the fourth trace completed its return.

He drew another.

Between the iron rings, the lower edge of the opening moved with it.
