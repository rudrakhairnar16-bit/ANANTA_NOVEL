# CHAPTER FIFTY-FOUR — THE MIRROR THAT COUNTED BACK

---

> *"First description, struck out: I became as strong as the person opposite me.*
> *Correction: I returned what reached me. When the source stopped, so did I.*
> *The distinction was the part we could least afford to learn late."*
>
> *— Ira Mishra, loose sheet later sewn into the field register*

---

The morning after the landing failed, Ira drew her own eye and put a line through it.

Not the eye. The arrow pointing out of it.

She had drawn the instrument as something being inspected, the way she would draw a damaged hinge. Last night its little plate had turned towards the angle of her gaze. Her diagram contained no arrow for what had come back.

She added one.

The two arrowheads met in the narrow space between the pencilled eye and the polished fitting. Her hand shook enough to make the meeting look accidental.

Across the kitchen, Veer tried to lower himself onto a chair without admitting that his knee hurt. The doctor's film had shown no fresh break in his forearm. That was the good news. The doctor's instructions about rest, strain and the sling had taken longer to repeat.

Ira waited until he was seated before moving his tea closer.

"Meri aankh itni badi hai?" he asked, looking at the drawing.

"Tumhari nahi."

"Phir theek hai."

He picked up the cup with his right hand. His left stayed where the doctor had put it.

Aadhya sat beside the open door with her palms resting on folded cloth. The right thumb and index finger were still numb. She could spread the other fingers, slowly. A little pile of clean dressings lay beside her; no one had suggested that the new form made dressings unnecessary.

Rudra came in carrying bread. He set it down without bringing his hands near the covered instrument on the table.

Ira noticed that too.

They ate before trying anything. Rukmini made certain of it. She had packed an extra tiffin for the aid post above Manikarnika, where the night watch had run out of clean cloth and patience in approximately equal measure.

"Aap wahan jaa rahi ho?" Rudra asked.

"Dopahar tak. Upar wala kamra. Ram saath jaayenge."

"Kal roof pe—"

"Isliye aaj bina bataye nahi jaa rahi."

She did not soften the sentence. Neither did she refuse the escort or the inspection Vedant had insisted on. Her work would happen in a room they had checked, above a route they could clear.

Ira watched Rudra accept a plan that was not the same as keeping his mother indoors. She was glad not to be the one who had to make that choice.

Rukmini tied the tiffin in a square of cloth. Before handing it over, she put a second packet beside Ira's maps.

"Tumhare liye."

"Main sirf fittings dekhne—"

"Khaana fittings se alag rakhna. Tel lag jaata hai."

Ira moved the packet to the dry side of her bag.

On its paper cover, in Rukmini's broad hand, was her name. Nothing else was required to say it had been packed for somebody expected to return.

---

They tested the metal eye in the courtyard with a wall between it and the street.

Vedant set it on a brick. He did not open the little plate. Sitara sat beside Ira, while Veer watched from a chair with his bandaged leg stretched out. Rudra stayed by the door; he had agreed not to offer the seam as a test source.

First Ira looked at the fitting without reaching through her sight.

The plate moved slightly. Not enough to prove it had followed her. Vedant marked its position in chalk.

Next he passed a hand between it and her face. No change.

Then she let the courtyard's edges loosen, the small act of attention that usually showed where a movement was about to go.

The plate turned a clear fraction.

She stopped. It stopped.

"Roshni nahi," she said.

"Sirf roshni nahi," Vedant corrected.

He covered the polished hollow with cloth. This time the motion was slower, but it did not cease until he put the whole fitting in a clay bowl and filled the hollow with wet sand.

Ira felt a faint release behind her eyes.

The thing had not been looking at her face in the ordinary sense. That much they could write down.

She wrote it, carefully leaving out the rest.

"Kal spear kyun dikha?" Veer asked.

"Us waqt tumhara tha. Main dekh rahi thi. Baad wala image rukha nahi."

"Toh mera copy nahi?"

"Proof nahi."

He nodded. No disappointment, no urging her to try harder. He had learned what the empty space after *proof* could cost.

Sitara slid a plain steel plate in front of Ira. Its worn surface reflected the neem above them in broken patches.

"Isme kya hai?"

"Tree."

"Tree nikalo."

Ira looked at her.

"Nahi nikal sakti," Sitara agreed. "Par roshni ki jagah badal sakti ho. Bina tree hue."

She tipped the plate. The reflected patch moved across the wall.

It was a demonstration, not an explanation of Ira's blood or dreams. She was grateful for that. Too many things in her life seemed to wait for an origin before anybody would let them have a present use.

Veer rested his good elbow on the chair's arm.

"Chhota shield," he said. "Haath nahi uthaunga."

"Pain hua toh band," Ira told him.

"Tum bhi."

A pale curve showed above his right palm, no wider than a tea saucer. Ira looked at it, then at the broken reflection in the steel.

Two curves appeared.

One belonged to Veer.

She could not yet tell whether the other belonged anywhere at all.

---

The second curve vanished when Veer closed his hand.

Ira kept looking. Nothing remained for her to hold or call back. When he opened his palm again, the faint line reappeared a little to the left of the first.

She put a loose thread against it.

The thread fell through.

Image, not force. Not yet.

She tried once more, this time watching the place where the line began instead of the outline she wanted to complete. Pressure gathered behind her nose. The courtyard doubled at its far edge.

She closed her eyes before the snap could come.

"Band," she said.

Veer's pale curve went out at once.

Neither asked for another attempt.

Sitara took the steel plate away. "Jo tumhari taraf aata hai, usko jagah de rahi ho. Jo banaana chahti ho, usko nahi. Farq hai."

Ira washed her face in the courtyard water. She could understand the distinction on paper. Her eyes still wanted to invent a whole shape from the promise of one.

She wrote three observations without using the word *success*.

*Live source required. Image shifts with angle. No contact confirmed.*

Below them she left room for the observation she did not yet have.

---

They went to the ghat after the morning rush had thinned.

Veer rode with the recovered gate fittings on the supply handcart. His bandaged knee rested on a rolled sack, and the doctor would have recognized no part of the arrangement as an excuse for exercise. Ira walked beside the cart. Sitara carried the bowl in which the polished eye lay packed with wet sand.

The air changed before they reached the main steps. Woodsmoke settled low between the houses. A priest rinsed ash from a brass spoon. Two men rested a bier on their knees while another cleared the way ahead, speaking quietly rather than shouting. Ira folded her map until there was room for them to pass.

Nothing she was looking for had the right to make these people background.

At the upper aid room, Ram had checked the window brackets, the floor and the passage behind the grain bins. Rudra was helping a volunteer mark the longer evacuation route with lengths of white cloth. He had moved the marks high enough to remain visible above a crowd.

Rukmini arrived with the tiffin and clean dressings. She put the food where the night attendants could find it without asking, then began replacing the wicks in the room's small lamps. The windows had to remain partly shuttered against sparks from below.

Ira set her own food packet beside the maps, still unopened.

Rukmini looked at it once.

"Baad mein," Ira said.

"Main bhi yahi bolke nikli thi."

She left half a roti on a clean square of paper beside Ira's hand. Ira ate it while reading the riverwatch list.

Three fittings had been found since dawn. Two were ordinary gauge collars, badly installed in the hurried repairs. The third sat in a shallow niche below the terrace, its polished face turned away from the river.

Towards the people on the stairs.

Vedant was already beside it, standing clear of its front. He had wedged a wooden board between its bracket and the stone rather than let a workman pull it loose.

"Neeche aur line hai," he told Ira. "Upar ka kholenge toh doosri taraf load jaayega. Pehle jagah khaali."

Two volunteers began moving traffic to the far stair. Sitara stayed with them. Ram took Rukmini back into the upper room while Rudra cleared the passage beside it.

Ira knelt on the safe side of the niche.

The gauge face contained two apertures. One was dark. In the other, light moved over a narrow polished scale.

She looked without reaching.

Nothing.

She let her sight find the pressure beneath the bracket.

The dark aperture brightened.

It was not gathering force from the stone. A thin trace came in from farther downriver, passed through the fitting, and went out again along a different angle. She followed the outgoing trace until it touched her own position on the terrace.

There it stopped being a line she could observe from outside.

A little pressure touched the back of her left eye.

She withdrew.

The aperture darkened at once.

"Wapas jaata hai," she said.

Vedant kept his hands still. "Kya?"

"Hum dekhte hain. Woh dekhna naapta hai. Phir wapas bhejta hai."

She drew two arrows in the dust. One out. One back. Her finger shook between them.

The roof fitting, the drain tongue, the eye beneath the gate: she could not prove every connection yet. But the same mistake ran through their assumptions. They had treated the instruments as passive witnesses.

Each time the team answered one, the answer went somewhere.

Vedant filled the bright aperture with wet sand from the bowl. Its light ceased.

Farther down the stairs, beneath a stack of empty wicker baskets, something clicked in reply.

He looked at Ira.

"Aur hain."

---

The second fitting did not wait for them to inspect it.

A low note pressed against Ira's teeth. Dust jumped out of the seam between two steps. The wooden board over the first bracket split down its middle.

Veer turned on the handcart.

"Neeche mat utro," Ira said.

He stayed seated, swung his good hand towards the stairs, and opened a protective curve above his palm.

A line of compressed air struck it.

The curve flattened. His right elbow drove into the cart rail. The impact made him gasp against the bruised ribs.

The people on the far stair stopped. Then those behind them, unable to see why, took another step forward.

Sitara lifted both arms to halt the upper crowd. Rudra moved along the white-cloth route, making space without touching his seam. The mark beneath his sleeve was bright enough for Ira to see through the cloth.

Another low note sounded.

This time the dust jumped closer to Veer's cart.

The aperture had changed angle after meeting his shield.

It was correcting for the answer.

Ira looked at the pale curve above his good hand. In her loosened sight a second curve appeared, clear enough to show the little gap where his concentration was failing.

She could see it.

The thread in the courtyard had fallen through.

Now there was no time to test with a thread.

---

Ira tried to move the second curve to the foot of the stairs.

It came apart before it reached the stone.

She had asked it to exist where she wanted it. The source was still over Veer's hand. Between the two places there was nothing but her certainty that a reflection ought to be able to move.

The fitting sounded again.

A strip of plaster flew off the cart rail. Veer pulled his good hand in by an inch, caught the pressure on his curve, and kept it from reaching his chest.

"Aur kitna?" Ira asked.

"Pata nahi. Jaldi."

That was better than a lie. She could work with it.

The lower stairs offered three possible lines in her second sight. On one, the pulse went past the cart into the wall. On another, Veer turned his wrist too late. On the third, it broke across the place where the people were trying to descend.

She could spend the remaining seconds choosing which future she liked.

Or look at what was actually there.

She let the three lines go.

The real curve above Veer's hand shook. Its pressure touched her sight, and with it came an unpleasant awareness of her own position: face turned towards the stairs, left eye nearest the bright aperture, shoulder within the return angle.

She had been leaving herself out of every diagram.

"Mujhe bhi gin raha hai," she said.

Vedant heard. "Peechhe ho jaao."

If she stepped back, she would be safer. The fitting would continue correcting against Veer's defence until it found the gap. If she closed her sight altogether, she would lose the thin line that showed where its return entered the niche.

She lowered herself behind the stone lip, keeping only her eyes above it.

"Main iska raasta dekh sakti hoon."

"Ira—"

"Apna bhi."

She turned her palm towards Veer, not towards the stair she wanted to save. The faint second curve returned, close to the first. This time she did not drag it away.

She received its edge.

The pressure was smaller than she expected. Exact. A narrow piece of somebody else's work arriving through the place where her sight met it. Beyond that edge, nothing belonged to her: not Veer's strength, not his weapon, not the margin he had found under the gate.

Only this contact, while he maintained it.

She shifted her own angle instead of moving the image by force.

The second curve turned.

A dropped chip of plaster struck it and bounced.

Ira drew breath. The blurred rim of the world sharpened into two clean faces, one facing the source and one facing away. Fine silver lines crossed her irises. She could feel a flatness opening beyond her palm, no thicker than the instant in which an image becomes recognizable.

**"DRISHTI: DARPAN."**

Blood warmed the lower lid of her left eye. A dark blur spread over that side of the ghat, swallowing half the stair she had been trying to see.

She kept the right eye open.

The reflected curve held.

Its strength did not feel like Veer's whole body entering hers. It felt like the curve itself: limited, trembling, with the same weak gap near its base. She could not erase that gap by wanting a better version.

But she could choose where the gap faced.

Ira turned her wrist a fraction.

The reflected shield appeared against the inside rim of the lower fitting, its convex face pointing into the polished hollow. Exactly the size of Veer's small curve. Large enough to cover the aperture. No larger.

The next note struck it from within.

Veer gasped. The source shivered above his hand. Ira's reflection shivered with it, and the pressure that should have crossed the stairs drove sideways into the brass bracket instead.

One of its screws pulled out of the stone.

"Left," Veer said.

She could no longer see what he meant. The blur had spread to the edge of her right eye's field, taking depth with it.

"Tumhara left?"

"Fitting ka. Neeche. Chhota."

She moved the angle a little.

"Haan. Wahi."

Another screw lifted. The niche rang. A long split opened along the lower plate.

The fitting turned its dark aperture towards her.

She felt the return touch behind her eyes with horrible precision, no longer a general pressure but an attempt to find the exact place from which she was looking.

Closing her sight would release it.

Closing her sight would also release the curve inside the fitting before the last fixing tore.

She stayed.

"Padh lo," she whispered. "Is baar main bhi dekh rahi hoon."

The dark trace leading downriver brightened in her one clear eye. There was a junction beneath the next landing, and another beyond it. Neither was the beginning. The line continued towards the bend below Manikarnika, where three vertical spars stood under tarpaulin beside a service stair.

She did not follow it farther. One glance was already more than she could afford.

"Neeche landing. Tarpaulin. Teen khambe," she told Vedant.

He repeated the words back exactly, so she would not have to spend breath making certain he had heard.

At the cart, Veer's right hand began to close.

Her reflected curve thinned.

"Bas," she said.

He let go.

The reflection vanished at once.

The fitting discharged into its own half-torn bracket. Brass struck the empty step below. The note broke into a ragged cough, and the pressure on the crowded stair ceased.

Sitara moved the first waiting people through the white-cloth passage. Rudra stayed at its narrow turn, one hand against the wall, telling them where the next step was. He did not look at the heat under his sleeve. He looked at their feet.

Ira tried to rise.

The ghat tilted in a way that had nothing to do with second sight.

Vedant caught her elbow and put her hand against the stone lip. "Baithi raho."

"Wapas gaya," she said.

"Pehle saans."

"Mera dekhna. Wapas gaya."

She needed him to understand the cost before relief made them call it a clean victory. The instrument had broken. Its reading had left before it broke.

Her right hand trembled too hard to keep the fingers on the same patch of stone.

Vedant folded a clean cloth and gave it to her. "Aankh pe dabana nahi. Bas neeche rakho."

She held it beneath the wet left lid.

A red crescent appeared in the cloth.

Across the terrace Veer was still seated on the cart. His right elbow bled where it had hit the rail. He opened his hand once and found no curve left above it.

Their eyes met.

"Source band," he said, because it was a fact he could offer her without promising anything else.

Ira nodded.

The space beyond her palm was empty again. She could not call his shield back. She could not replace the portion of the stair hidden behind the blur.

She could hear the people getting through.

For now she let that be enough.

---

The aid-room attendant made Ira cover the left eye without pressing on it.

The cloth turned the world into a flat arrangement of edges. She could see with the right. Judging how far away a cup stood was another matter. Twice she reached short of it before Rukmini moved it into her hand.

"Abhi padhna nahi," the attendant said.

Ira nodded.

"Likho bhi mat."

She lowered the pencil.

The attendant made her practise finding the chair arm with the back of her hand before putting weight on it. On stairs she would need someone to name the edge aloud. Ira disliked the instruction enough to repeat it back exactly; if she was going to hate a limit, she would at least remember where it was.

Veer sat opposite her with a fresh dressing on his right elbow and the old sling still holding the left arm. Between them lay a blank sheet that neither could make useful by stubbornness.

"Main bolungi," Ira said. "Tum yaad rakhna."

"Isko treatment bolte hain ya punishment?"

She might have laughed if the change in pressure had not hurt behind her covered eye.

He leaned forward at once. "Sorry."

"Joke kharaab tha. Injury alag hai."

He settled back, reassured by the distinction.

She dictated what had happened in plain words. The reflected power had come from a live source. It had kept the source's size, weakness and interruption. She had changed its place by changing the return angle. When Veer stopped, the reflection had stopped, with nothing left to summon.

"Ek waqt pe ek," he said.

"Abhi utna hi pata hai. Zyada claim mat karna."

He repeated the observations until she was satisfied he had not improved them into a legend.

Then he added the part she had skipped.

"Tumhara reading chala gaya."

Ira sat very still.

"Haan."

He did not tell her it was worth it. The people had got through. The enemy had received something. Both belonged on the page.

Rukmini put the unopened food packet between them. This time Ira let Veer tear the paper while she held it down.

---

The second mark was found where Rukmini had been standing.

Not a wound. A narrow black stroke on the limewashed window recess, level with the knot on her wick bundle. The wood beside it was warm although the lamps had been moved away.

Ram brought the bundle into the light. One outer reed had been cut almost through. Rukmini had not felt it happen.

Rudra's face changed.

"Aapko—"

"Nahi," she said before he could finish. She showed him both hands, turned them, opened the fingers. Uninjured.

He looked anyway.

Vedant asked her to leave the bundle on the sill and move back. In the broken fitting below, a small sighting tongue shifted towards the empty space beside the aid-room door.

Rukmini stood there now.

They moved the bundle again. The tongue did not follow it.

Ram stepped between the niche and the door.

"Andar," he said.

This time Rukmini went at once.

They transferred the remaining patients to the room behind the grain store, one by one. It had no river-facing window. A watchman stayed at its street door, and the white-cloth route was moved to match the new exit.

Ira heard the work from her bench. She did not remove the eye dressing to confirm it herself. Other people could observe things. She had spent too much of her life behaving as though everything unobserved by her would disappear.

When Ram returned, she asked only, "Rukmini-ji theek?"

He nodded, then moved her cup precisely against her good hand before going back to the door.

At the far end of the bench Rudra rested his forehead briefly against the wall. His hand stayed open. No tapping.

---

By late afternoon the physical evidence lay on one table: roof-washer sketch, peeled drain tongue, gate splice, broken niche plate. The polished eye remained packed in wet sand.

Ira dictated from memory. Vedant drew the joins. He used different marks for what she had seen and what he inferred, and did not connect a broken line merely because it would make a more satisfying diagram.

The returns ran downriver towards the covered spars. Other lines fed them from the city side. The ordinary service stair above the landing had become a place through which too many separate measurements passed.

"Wahi main machine hai?" Rudra asked.

"Wahi ek jagah hai jahan hum dekhne jaayenge," Vedant said. "Dono ko ek mat samjho."

"Aaj?"

"Bina sight khole. Bina naam bulaaye. Pairon se."

He sent two experienced watchmen along the public lane, with instructions to come back rather than make an arrest. Ram would walk the upper route. There were still families at the ghat who could not be moved simply by writing CLOSED on a board.

Ira wanted to go. Her body made the refusal before anyone else had to. When she stood, the table edge slid away from her hand and she sat again too hard.

Sitara moved the chair closer to the wall.

"Aaj jo dekha woh sambhaal lo," she said. "Baaki hum dekh lenge."

Ira rested her good eye. Behind the covered one the stair still seemed to be there, with a dark aperture at its foot. She knew the image was not proof of continuing sight. She named five sounds in the room until the false depth receded.

Then she asked for her unfinished breakfast drawing.

Vedant brought it. She dictated a correction to the arrows: one line for the power received, one for the power returned, and a separate mark for what the instrument had learned about the receiver.

"Mera naam mat likhna," she said.

"Kyun?"

"Rule hai. Sabke liye."

He wrote RECEIVER beside the mark.

The diagram looked less like a portrait. That made it more frightening.

---

At evening the watchmen came back with dust on their knees.

There were three covered spars, as Ira had said. Between them, workmen had fitted an iron frame round an ordinary service winch. No public works order named the contractor. The old waterline went beneath it; fresh cables had been laid alongside, concealed under the stair's replacement boards.

The watchmen had not touched them.

"Kal tak nahi the," one said.

The other shook his head. "Cover tha. Humne kal dekha nahi. Alag baat."

Ira opened her good eye. "Wahi likhna."

Rudra listened to the report standing beside the new patient-room door. Beyond it, his mother was helping a woman change her child's dressing. The familiar low voice reached him clearly through the wood.

He stayed where he could hear it.

Far downriver, in a portable instrument case, a strip of paper advanced past a brass stylus. Three short traces crossed the strip. A fourth remained broken.

An attendant cut the paper, fed its measurements into a punch, and laid the card in the Right Hand's dispatch tray.

**LAUTNE KA PATA MILA.**

The return address has been found.

Beneath the line, a second reference mark had been added in pencil: not the eye, not the shield, not the roots.

A small lamp, beside a doorway.
