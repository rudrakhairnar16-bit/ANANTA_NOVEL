# CHAPTER FORTY-FOUR — THE SISTER WHO WALKED IN AT NOON

---

> *"Jis ghar mein kisi ne kisi bahan ke liye unnis baras diya jalaya ho — us ghar ka darwaaza doston ke liye nahi bana. Uska darwaaza kahin aur ban ke aaya hota hai, aur woh sirf bahnon ke liye khulta hai — bina baje, dupahar mein."*
>
> *— MOTHER HANDA, who kept lamps longer than most people keep faith*

---

*(In a house where someone has kept a lamp for a sister nineteen years — that house's door was never made for friends. Its door was built somewhere else, and it opens only for sisters — without a bell, at noon.)*

---

She came at noon, which in our district is the one hour when nothing ordinary works — the hour Rengwa ascribes to the water-clock's dripping rest, the hour anxious mothers wick-wait through, the hour in which nobody's business is open — so that, the register may now report factually, *no one saw her come*, in the precise sense that no eyes at the moment of her arrival were upon the street she came down. West-road lied that morning: the temple had unanimously declared that the wind would pull east all day, and they had even guessed it was thirteen past. Nothing did, of course. Twelve, thirteen— none of it helped. At sharp noon, when the district's mid-mouth lay empty and wet-Rengwa-watch-lamps sank to candle-glow, she walked down Simri Deora's eastern crest, un-sign-boarded, un-announced, on feet made of shell-shapes, and the town's only witness was Aadhya's tulsi half-ring, whose hundred small faces had been facing east — then *turned west, to greet her, then faced east again*, although only an urchin from the by-chettan of Asadhat Chowk happened to see the turn, and he later swore it had taken the ring one breath which it was breathing anyway.

He who writes our register's industrial entries says this so plainly now — that no school thereafter could keep any child from knowing *exactly* where the visitor stood, and when she came, because our kasba is a small-craft wall on which everything registers: chulha-dust, eye-lash, dew-squeeze, khakhra-boil, lead's-eye-winking. We knew she had passed the millet-roof topped tank — precisely because no crow or shikra dipped to drink, which alone draws the town-school's attention — then near the **foul-faced grafters near Baherao tank mound** a couple of saddle-barrows named Sutan, Laal-Uttar & Co waited respectfully on post-line because she was following the westward stone-fox fence with her palms turned as if writing water; then she crossed the *elbow of the nala* (where old Thakran saab sat) and lay down her travelling-basket before the melon-woman's deterence-market stall (a wheeled canvas-trolley, home-made, aluminum hot-water carrier in hand), smile-faint, palms still playing with something nobody allowed themselves to name.

She looked neither suspicious nor beautiful; she looked — this was the statement entered thereafter, since she had been coming all along, though the town's eye had been turned elsewhere — **ekdum theek**, just exactly so, one-of-seven's simile getting its ratio right — and that was recognition enough for three households the register names why: because the trolley-puller, who had kept in her pocket for two years a torn corner of sack-paper (ink-headed fennel, coriander, ajwain, mustard) from the day of the south sewer pipe's breakfast roar and Aadhya's foreheads seed-sowing, *remembered it exactly and turned up exactly at noon*, as did the ledger-ish name of Ishwar Chand Sharma's shop — "Ek Bahan" in a card-style — because she had kept remembering it nineteen years in a window, as did the chin-lip bouncy high-born fortune-less plain-clothes no-dress woman of Simkha bazaar (since confectioner says so) viewing her exactly at noon — she fainted with relief.

---

She stopped on the drum-post where the irrigation ditch enters the karha's lowest stubble-field (the bend where the register's west-water flows uphill per instruction). She did not knock. She needn't. She stood quietly with her palms still moving, seeming to count things that were there already, and she looked in the direction that our town — all five hamlets, one hundred seventy-nine registered households — had been accepting as the *required direction* for two weeks and more. Her hairpin was incense-smoke ash, the kind they use wherever coriander is kept, and on her forearm there were broken brass bangles of different gauges which she had clearly not *bought* but *found*, because their ellipses had mean-below-wrist nature and pale-sapphire smears, like fifteen years wear-and-tear histories just as they were.

She closed her eyes briefly, chanted low (nobody heard it, so it's not written; lore gave her at least this), lifted her gaze slowly, and at the words aloud in the plainest Magahi-adjacent inflection the west-side'd ever heard:

**"Koi ghar hai yahan jahan ek diya unnis baras se jala hai — aur jiska shish kisi bahan ka intezar mein kabhi nahi gaya tha?"**

*(Is there a house here where a lamp has burned nineteen years — and whose glass-chimney never went out waiting for a sister?)*

She asked of nobody; she spoke to the lettuce-sellers' weighted stones first, then the dirty-buffalo fence-gap, then the pathori-lane, then the threshold-carved doorposts where Salman's father's teak had first heard the district's steps; and it was the tulsi half-ring — *Aadhya's tulsi half-ring* — that answered. It gave no sound, but it turned its faces *west* a second time, taller than the urchin remembered, and — the register insists — one hundred clay pots were now leaf-cups bearing grass, and each blade stood perpendicular for nineteen seconds, counting.

She moved on to the lowering-near-west-bank cottage where a lamp had now been seen burning in daylight for exactly a fortnight, carried there every morning by a soft-voiced woman with that careful stiffness of one who will not see a star unattached, and who has never yet come across one that doesn't fit her, nor one she has been *unable* to fit. And at that cottage's threshing-floor, Sitara laid down the brass-bangled burden, not rudely, dusted her palms, and said to the window — in that quiet measured voice the district was to come to know over five years as one keeping the liturgy's Green Oath written in morphine-augmented reeds:

"Main Sitara. Aapne mujhe bulaya tha. Toh main aa gayi."

*I am Sitara. You called me. So I came.*

And the lamp in the east-facing window — it never went out, it just stopped *needing* to be lit by wick, and burned instead by the simple fact of the woman's having heard what she had kept thirteen years locked in the sheaf-readers' stalls finally name itself at her sill.

---

The door opened before the last syllable had died, and on the verandah's second step there stood — there would be no describing her except in the district's later school-explanations: *one of the women who, never-at-all doubting, had kept the lamp burning with* **man** *from the start* — Mother Handa herself, forty-one years' seniorness melting exactly like frost at noon, who knew what this call must be as soon as she heard a voice not her own say "bulaya tha" — "you called" — for once without irony, entirely ordinary, alarming only for the burden it *didn't* carry.

There are such women, in the register's five-fold accounting, who keep *walking knowledge* for others, and who make their whole house's fortune by the graphic representation of *waiting-for*, until the thing waited for arrives exactly as it was, one who will not disappoint it. Rukmini — the register uses her domestic name as the familiarity from ch-41 settles — had been **combing out** her lamp-basket of wick-coils when the voice came. Her eyes went even more watchful. She had already swallowed her first breath; she did not take a second; she removed the woolen shawl from around her throat and dropped it softly on the reed-mat; she moved across the verandah in the most ordinary way — as one moves from cutting plants to planting them — she reached the sill with her arms, and in the most sounding tone said, **immediately**, for her own hearing much more than for any district's history:

"Aa gayi. Dekhti hoon."

*She has come. I see her.*

And the lamp at the east window — which had been raised there daily since the card with the steel diya's circular ghee-stain — the lamp finally did what lamps are *meant* to do in the register's arithmetic: **it stopped being necessary**, because its twenty-year work of *keeping* had arrived, and its work now, in exactly this hour, at exact noon, in ordinary May heat, was to *welcome*, which is a different thing from keeping, and so it simply *went out*, with barely a wisp of smoke, immediately replaced by its quiet fulfilling named twin — the second lamp in the west window, which Rukmini, without any foolish district doctrine, now lit with a fresh wick, *the other way round*.

---

"You have kept it twenty years," Sitara was saying on the threshing-floor, looking at the lamp-flame which had just gone out — one's breath held, at noon, exactly nineteen years prior — the whole sheaf-thickened sky collecting itself over hills of this immediate left, "and you will not trim it until the guest has seen it. Now the guest has seen it. It can go to its sleep — jaisa ki sabko jaana chahiye; apna kaam poora kiya toh woh so jaata hai bina kisi seenazori ke."

*(As everyone should go — when their work is done, they go to sleep without any fuss.)*

Rukmini did not need telling; she said merely, minding the wick-ash in her palm: "Bahan, darwaze pe khada hua makaan nahi dekha jaa raha tha kab se?"

*(Sister, why are we staring at the house standing at the door?)*

"Makaan nahi dekha jaa raha tha," Sitara answered. "Ghar dekh rahin ho."

*(It's not the house that wasn't being seen. You* are *seeing home.)*

"Toh aag laga do," Rukmini said — two syllables' economy, May-thick, barati-lamp heat — "kyunki darwaze pe khade makaan mein bhi ghar ka dekhra pehla hai, aur ab ghar ka dhyaan wahi hai."

*Then set it aflame — because even a house standing at the door may see home first, and home keeps preferring that view.*

And Sitara came in. There was no ceremony. There was merely, across the three-step threshold, the district's longest period of welcome ever afforded any stranger, which lasted exactly the time of a wick lighting and going out concurrently, and ending with — the register stresses, since the town would slot this into its records as **the useful detail of the fortnight** — a two-palm hug exchanged over the threshold cleat, in which all that either woman carried was transferred, or made at least legible, and nobody outside at that hour noticed any of it.
Inside the haveli — which the register names haveli only out of courtesy, since its roof was Thakran-saab-grade teak rather than supervision-grade black-slate — everything resumed fitting. That was the haveli's real discipline: **nothing had ever *mished* here**; nothing in this household's twenty-year patient arithmetic of fittings ever had to be *un-fitted*, and Sitara's arrival was not, in any household register, a *guest* but a *fitting*: an already-sized corner into which a wanderer could now be slid. Rukmini had, in advance, prepared for her the correct spot by the east-window shutter opposite the tongue-Vedant-quietope lamp, because it was the house's one location where — as she had said in her private grammar — *agli subah ke pehle roshni ka aarti nah marwa gayi hai; doosri saans wahi khainch li jati hai* (the first dawn's arati-light has not died — but the second breath will be drawn from exactly there). She had made the spot *available*, not *inviting* — there is a difference, though the words sit close — and Sitara, who had crossed thirteen years of bedecks to get here, slid into it as the second lamp wick slid into its holder in the west window opposite.

The chair — this specific fact the register notes because it was the district's very best argument against the universal theory that all waiting is useless — the chair was Thakran-saab's old hall-seat, which, once the father had left the household, had been nobody's seat and everybody's, because a Teak-and-Champ done right will care for twenty years of *now I'm using it* without complaint. Now it had a specific occupant. It would have her again, in this same manner, for exactly twenty-four years, until the register does not need to tell on which day she would get up for good.

It was at this point that the whisper started up again — not the whisper of words, but the whisper of the *wick*. Mother Handa, hands no longer trembling from twenty-year restraint, went around the haveli and lit every lamp that had been kept out of sight, because her daughter (the register may now disclose) was not the only one who had kept lamps; her *mother*, too, had, and her modest-lamps had fitted exactly — every unmarked mud cup, every rope of drying herbs, every spoon of tempered resin — and now they returned to their *outdoors* state, all thirteen of them, and the afternoon sun left its normal business and all the eatables on the thaali-stalls looked briefly like they belonged to a village though we *were* a village; the lamps' oil-searched flames lit up the Shiv-ratan's vertical mahadev-coloured portrait, and polished the tarnished faces of the tin-wrist-clocks, and for the first time since Father Handa had gone, all of the lamps faced east, because there was an easterner in the house. (Though east was where they had *all* come from, but the word lost meaning there for a while.)

"Dekhiye," Rukmini said to Sitara, on the second verandah with Aadhya present (the register's emphasis), passing her tongue-Vedant reason into both hands at once — she held Sitara's left hand and her daughter's right together, both by the wrist, not for divination but because that is usual practice where lamp-summaries are concerned, "**ab is ghar ka ghar chahta hai ki dono haath ka mazza ho — dono haath ki tali ek, dono haath ki rassa alag; dono jaan alag — zameen same.**"

*(See — now this house's house wishes that both hands carry flavour alike — the two hands' palm one, the two hands' string different; the two souls different — but the ground the same.)*

Sitara knew, without need of translation, the simplest translations of this grammar; she had grown up on its street. She did not say anything elaborate back. She only said **"Haan"** — Yes — the register-pronunciation being a *yes* that also means *it is so*, meaning that one acknowledges with one's own life the word and the world which come upon one, and stays standing while they settle — and then she squeezed both their wrists a trifle — just a trifle — to say first that she carried this word, and second that she would carry it further as well, which is the meaning of *haan* in the register Magahi-inflected, and which is not a fair bargain, but it is the only one ever struck here.

---

In Mother Handa's cook-shanty, unprepared dahi-bhalla was being prepared anyway for exactly the right-sized guest, yet exactly the wrong-sized month (too early for the good lassi), and the mid-mouth noon sun was in fact rigorous in filling the thaali stool-leaves with all its punitive persistence; and here, of course, Mother Shiva put the newly washed leaves on flat brass platters with mantisa-measured care, and here, finally, the tulsi half-ring — whose faces had first faced west, then returned, adjusting themselves — could be permitted a quieter rhythm, and the thousand green-leaves now showed themselves sixteen-by-sixteen aligned, a living board of emerald *shapabs* in Aadhya's ash-gourd vine patch where they'd lately been flipped for packing, or so the register must report.

It was *observed* here — and the register's accuracy deserves scribing — that the pitiyaan board (the peepal's) had its face turned toward the stable-year sun; the melon-woman's cart stayed parked; the urchin who had seen the *turning* had run back to the paper-shop and gone coal-eyed at the sight of his own father there, ink-covered since morning (his charge was to keep *ayat* of what worlds arrive in the street — he was exactly right and knew nothing); the grate in the grange still stood open; the abandoned pile of Thakran-thakurdwar's broken slates tipped their salt-glitter so that from up there you saw the second lamp — the one replaced by the *twirling-coin* lamp's purana-shaped conic (candle-form of her fruit-orchard earnings) — steady enough that its broad bowl struck one's glance against the haveli wall's wet mud until it looked like painting, meaning just that whatever kept it alight was *absolutely normal*, which was itself a comment.

---

In the house, where all thirteen lit-but-unseen lamps had seen my sister's arrival just now — the register urges care: the register gains its authority only from a ledger's eye — *Mother Handa* spoke the first liturgy aloud. It was not a liturgy from any book; it was the register Magahi-ritual she had been composing in her mind for twenty years, half-sentence by half-sentence, since the day Father Handa had told her (before stepping out to settle some matter of road-levelling near the eastern bend's heap's last legal standing) — *ikkees sawere ke baad, jabaan khulegi aur ghar aa jaayga* — *after twenty-one mornings, the tongue shall be free, and the household shall come home* — and as twenty-one mornings had now run into nineteen years, she had only the *second half* of it memorized, which was, in her daughter's translation into the register's standard idiom:

**"Sau bar jismein ek bar bhi nah rahi — saugar se pehle — uska ghar nahi ban jata pura. Jis din wo wapas jaata hai jis ghar mein wo daal thahraaya tha — usi din ghar poora hota hai. Main abhi bhi — puri hoon."**

*(The one who has left even once in a hundred — yet before the farewell — that household does not fill completely. The day the one who was left in that household returns — that day the household fills. I now am — full.)*

The lamp in the west window — the *second* lamp — became calm. The lamp in the east window, heretofore necessary, now snuffed and silent, allowed its final thread of smoke to climb the teak beam and say goodbye, like a fate-recorder who has just finished the day's account. And the third lamp — the *new* lamp, which Mother Handa would thereafter keep in the middle window *between* the two, and had already placed there — the third lamp, which nobody had yet lit, was the one to which all the others looked for instruction, because that lamp was going to have to light for somebody else.

---

"Tumhe abhi," Rukmini said, to the unlit middle lamp, "kyunki woh jo aane wali thi woh aa chuki hai — toh ab kya hai?"

*(You now — because the one who was coming has already come — what happens?)*

And in the lowest voice of her daughter, Aadhya — the register has insisted on the necessity, for the register's sake, of including every voice that speaks at this household's lamp-happening — she answered the twirling-coin lamp's conic bronze tongue-quietly, in a voice which the register may now chalk up as Sat-Sandesh:

"Mere saath ginti jalegi. Ahista. Don." *(The count will burn alongside me. Slowly. Two.)*

Then the tulsi half-ring outside — its pots' faces loaded eastward — all flicked their leaves *westward* at once, and tilted their cups *so* minutely toward the haveli's east wall that the register's field reader, had she witnessed it, might have guessed — correctly — that the easterner inside had just greeted the house with a *haan*, and that this same easterner, despite all geometry, had already been there.

---

At this point the district's lamp-carl — who in the register's grammar placed the tongues of the communal standard every morning (and who had twenty-year knowledge of which street would answer first and which lamp would with whom) — went around to *the haveli's* east wall and removed the west-gate tulsi half-ring's turning with his own palms, and placed a hat there instead, because the wind had changed and the mid-mouth had lost the adhrit of the turn of the hour; but it was *no matter*, it was all much of a muchness now, for inside the haveli there were now *three* lamps burning in daylight (east necessary-snuffed, west and middle), in a district where daytime lamps had once been the private folly of only one woman, *and she was snuffed now*, her job finally done.

And Sitara, fitting perfectly in the available corner of the east-window shutter — the register's old failure becoming, in the district's later memory, a modest triumph — spoke the register-tag ka-se-into aamar: **"Jitna dekh rahe the, dekhne se zyada main sulook rahi thi. Ab sulook mat ho. Ab dekhna ho."**

*(All that you were watching — I was watching too, at greater cost. Stop watching now. Start looking.)*

The house's lamps, which kept in mind all their hangman's holes — of what they'd seen and what they'd kept — now settled. And Aadhya, in the courtyard, on the stone where she had first discovered in her palms the geometry of grounding, felt for the first time in five winters that *somebody else* was grounding — *also was* — and the ground, sensing this, held still for one breath *more* than it had held still for her alone. The seam under Rudra's ribs, in its unreachable flat on the left sabha-bhadra's twirling-coin memory, did not stir. It merely noted, in its stilted Magahi voice — *three now tend their lamps. Of seven, the fifth is come, and none of them is wrong.*

The household kept its late-afternoon rhythm — which is the only rhythm a household that has just been *filled* can keep, because a filled house does not celebrate; a filled house *resumes* — and it was in the resuming that the district's other watchers found their meanings.

Chattu and Chandan, returned from Machli-ghat without Veer (Veer having been kept behind by the night-teacher's first lesson — seven hour-marks standing under one star, alone, hearing the distinction between *falling* and *returning* said aloud for the first time), carried home from the Turanan akhada two things: one stone each — Bhanu-uzhaar had pressed them, out of courtesy, from the bend's shingle — and one sentence, spoken by Naana-ji with the canopy of a thousand lota-bells stirring above him: **"Ghar jaao. Dekho — aaj kaun sa diya jal raha hai jo kal nahi jal raha tha."** *(Go home. See — which lamp burns today that was not burning yesterday.)*

So the two boys went house to house, faithfully, evening-wise, doing the audit that is a boy's tariff duty in every distressed era — the duty of saying what is lit — and they counted the district well enough until they reached the haveli on the karha's edge, and then they stopped counting, because this house was doing the wrong arithmetic. Three lamps in daylight. One snuffed whose whole life had been snuffing-resistance. One fresh with no oil history whatsoever. And a woman on the second-verandah whom nobody in the district had ever seen before (or had ever seen *not* there, depending on which of the register's field readers you asked afterward — the melon-woman maintained she had been coming for weeks; the tea-seller swore she had been coming for *years*; the ledgers say *one noon, one crest, one shell-footed step* — and the register adopts the plainest entry, being oldest).

"Kiska ghar hai?" Chandan asked.

"Rukmini-mausi ka," Chattu said. "Dekh — teen."

They stood a while. Then Chattu entered it in his palm-leaf tally — the register he kept because the district had taught him to: **"Karha-kinaare wala ghar: diya TEEN."** And under it, because loyalty to Naana-ji's grammar runs in the trader's blood, he added: **"Jo kal nahi jal raha tha — aaj WOH bhi hai."**

*(The one that wasn't burning yesterday — today that too exists.)*

---

Inside, the house was doing the oldest arithmetic of all — the arithmetic of eating. Mother Handa had not been wrong about dahi-bhalla being the wrong-sized month, but the household had been making food for guests longer than guests had been making themselves scarce, and there were, by the count of the thaali-stall's leaves, fourteen plates' worth laid out — three too many, as always, because a household that has waited nineteen years does not start skimping on the day of its fullness. There was the coarse-ground chutney which only the melon-woman knows how to pound into a paste that keeps for ten days without gold-leaf salt; there were the three kinds of bhujiya which the old korri-ledger keeps only for the household's own return-visits; there was the flat-bread which, because the year's wheat had been buried good and deep (the register recalls the wheat-putting at chulha-minus-six), was still warm from the morning's bricking; and there was — the register insists upon its solemnity, because food is our only certain arithmetic — an extra bowl which nobody remembered laying out, and which Sitara saw first, and into which she ladled her own share with exactly the unconsciousness of one who is about to teach, and then stop teaching, and then begin again when the student returns.

"Mujhe dheere se batana," she said to Rukmini over the flat-bread, in a voice that carried not at all beyond the thaali, "**kyunki tumne unnis baras woh thaali khachra mein rakhi thi — aur thaali wali cheez kabhi kachra nahi hoti; thaali wali cheez sirf baaki rehti hai.**"

*(Tell me slowly — because you kept that thaali in the rubbish for nineteen years — and thaali-thing is never rubbish; thaali-thing always just remains.)*

Rukmini, whose grammar had never run to more than farming terms, looked at her own hands, and answered — through the leaves — with a farmer's exactness:

"Baaki rehna hi kachra hai, bahan. Toh jo baaki reh gaya — usko utha ke thaali bana diya."

*(Remaining is rubbish, sister. So what remained — I lifted it and made a plate.)*

There is no other entry in the register's whole grammar which fits this household better. *What remained — we lifted it and made a plate.* And it was for this reason, alone, that Aadhya's father had gone east, and never once written of it; because what is *remaining* cannot be written. It can only be lifted.

---

When Dhruv finally came — at exactly seven evening-marks, as his first condition demanded, via the water-gate at Machli-ghat's other bend rather than the road (the register's field entry: *no torch; alone; carried from the shelf where Veer's seven lessons, in forty-nine days, will be said complete*) — he found the haveli in its three-lamp state, and did not comment on it. He was a man whose attention arrived from far away, and whose grammar did not run to domestic comments; but he stood at the threshold, placed his oiled-canvas bag — hand-stitched, watermarked, content-wet — on the mat with the surveyor's exactness of settling a sovereign instrument, raised his hands — flattened, palm-forward, as one shows when the groves are tall and all markers must be plantable — and said, *to the district's keeping*:

"Aathva chhor abhi maun hai. Par maun ko bhi awaaz mil rahi hai. — Main sirf ek raat rahoonga. Kal main jaaonga Machli-ghat. Par raat bhar — main yahin rahoonga. Tumhare teenon diye — mujhse pehle gine gaye hain. Chaaro taraf se — chaaro."

*(The eighth point remains in silence. But silence too is getting its voice now. — I shall stay only one night. Tomorrow I go to Machli-ghat. But the night full — I shall stay right here. Your three lamps — have been counted before me. From all four sides — four.)*

Rukmini looked at the man on her threshold, then at the three lamps, then at her own hands, and answered — the district's formal welcoming, one lamp to another — with the register's oldest instruction:

"Andar aa jaao. Ghar ke saamne khada hona tha toh bahaar kyon nikla tha tum?"

*(Come inside. If you were going to stand before the house, why did you go outside?)*

Dhruv, who had been counting stars since before most of the district's shutters had been built, smiled with the smile that cured road-fatigue, and came in.

---

The register closes here because at this exact hour, eastward on Machli-ghat's shoulder, the last band of the Turanan akhada's stone-wall — the wall of two-thousand thumb-pressed stones, the register keeps insistently — received its twenty-second stone from the district's traffic, and the twenty-second stone was not placed by the hand of any wrestler, nor by Bhanu-uzhaar's committee, nor by Veer (still standing under the night-sky), nor by Dhruv (who had arrived only in the evening and would not let his night-lesson crowd the house's earlier arithmetic).

It was placed by nobody the register can legibly name. It was placed *by the ground itself* — by the irrigation-nala's elbow where the rain had run during the storm, by the tulsi-threshold under which the second lamp now burned steady, by the whole karha-field's patient arithmetic of *remaining-and-returning-and-becoming* again.

Bhanu-uzhaar saw it next morning when he came through for inspection, and placed his own thumb against the groove he'd pressed the night previous, and looked at it one minute too long, and said — to nobody, in Turanan's dialect:

**"Do hazaar mein barabar ab bhi ikkis ka nishaan hai… par doosri bar — kisine patthar ko chhoo liya bina patthar ke."**

*(In two thousand, the draw-mark is still the twenty-first… but the second time — somebody has touched the stone without the stone.)*

Which is exactly what a house does when it receives a sister it has been writing for nineteen years in lamp-oil and wick-prayers and west-window glass. It is taken without being touched. It is entered without stepping. It is *always* — as the register praya often, even to itself — already been there.
