# CHAPTER 41 — THE THREE LETTERS (Book 3, Ch 1)

The week after a storm is called, in that district, **the counting-week**. Not the mourning-week — mourning is a storm-day custom, when the lamps burn and the names are read; but when the storm has been *answered* — when the account has fought back and been found, by the great arithmetic of mercy, to have lost no one at all — then the district does not mourn. It **counts what it still has.**

So they counted. The register required it; the rooftops requested it; the register could not refuse.

Eleven district-injured, back at their trades by Friday. Seventeen grey-injured, departed Thursday on the 6:10 with a document Ira drafted which the postmaster franked with the professional thrill of a lifetime: **"prapt — na ldai, na haar — hisaab khula"** *(received — neither defeat nor loss — account standing open)*. One abacus, destroyed — its surviving beads, seven strings' worth, gathered by the schoolmaster with trembling ceremony and distributed one row to each of the kasba's seven mohalla-schools, with the written instruction which entered thereafter into every civics lesson of the district: **"Ginnna sikho — girane walon ke paas itna lamba hath nahi hota."** *(Learn to count — the takers do not have so long an arm.)*

And one school-gate with a new word over its lintel, cut deep in block letters by the stonemason who had refused payment for the bench-plate, and who refused payment again, saying the district owed him nothing, he owed the district a spelling-bee: **RESONANCE**, in Devanagari outline and in Roman shadow-cut beneath, like a bilingual deed.

The lamps, per the bangle-wife's standing order, now burned daylight hours too. The district had discovered that lamps in daylight are not waste: **they are policy.**

---

Rudra's arm was no longer a private district.

He discovered this Tuesday morning at the flour-mill, where — he had gone with the week's grain, the ordinary chore, the ordinary queue — a boy of perhaps eight, waiting with his mother, stared at the gold-fissured skin on his forearm where the sleeve stood rolled (he no longer rolled it down; the district's summer was hot and the seal's lacquer-work — the kavach's webbing of fine gold crazing, running now from forearm to shoulder and, by the doctor's lamplight inspection of Sunday, extending in hair-lines across the breastbone in the exact schematic of the temple's band-five lamp-pattern — could not be comfortably hidden, and the council, in its wisdom, had not asked) — the boy of eight stared, and instead of fear, his face flowered into recognition:

"Wahi wala? Register wale bhaiya?"

And the mother did not pull the child back. She did what the district's mothers had begun doing all week: she touched her own forehead with two fingers — the granters' greeting, the salutation made to things that *keep* — and said the new greeting of the new district, quietly, as though checking a ledger-line: "Ginti aage badhti rahe, bhaiya."

(*May the count keep growing, brother.*)

Rudra carried the flour home thinking about that greeting. It sat on him differently than the temple's weighing had. The temple's weighing had asked: *what are you.* The district's greeting asked: *will you continue.* He understood, measuring the two with a carpenter's patience, that the first had been a door and the second was a salary. The district was *paying* him now — in greeting, in lamplight, in the schoolmaster's seven bead-rows — paying for something it expected to keep receiving.

He told Ira at the evening sitting of the register. She did not look up from the fair copy of the week's accounts — the storm had generated forty-one folios — but her pencil stopped, which in Ira was a whole answer's length of listening: "Toh tumhaari tankhwah shuru hui," she said. *(So your salary has begun.)* "Dhyan rakhna: tankhwah wale galti kar sakte hain, par bhool nahi kar sakte." *(Mind this: salaried men may err, but they may not forget.)*

"Ye kaunsa niyam hai?"

"Naya. Abhi likha." And she dipped the pencil, and entered it, Rule Six, in the book: **RAKHWALE KO BHOOL-NAAM-NASHA HARAAM. (To the keeper, the opium of forgetting is forbidden.)**

---

The first letter came Monday; the second Tuesday; the third Wednesday. That is the delivery ledger's order and this chronicle abides by it — but the district's memory, being less pedantic than its postmaster, remembers the three as one chord of arrival, the way the performing hand remembers three strings stopped on one beat.

**Monday's envelope** Rukmini received in the courtyard where she was airing the winter quilts. Government-green was a color the district knew from ration cards, and the mother of the house knew it from deeper places than ration cards; she held the envelope up against the morning and the courtyard's light and her face performed — Dadi saw it from the kitchen window and recorded it afterward in the only medium Dadi trusted, the next stitch of her winter quilting — the exact expression of a woman who has received, after twenty years, a reply from a prayer she had stopped sending. The envelope smelled, Rukmini would say later to nobody but Dadi, of **a flower that grows above the altitude where lamps are forbidden.**

Inside was a single card of mountain-grey paper, hand-made, deckled, and on it, in penmanship so fluid the letters seemed poured:

*"Didi. Aapka deep kabhi bujha nahi — humne door se dekha. Ab oil badhne ka samay aaya; baati bhi badhani hogi. Hum aa rahe hain — zarurat se pehle, qurbani ke baad. — Ek Bahan."*

*(Sister. Your lamp never went out — we watched from far. Now the hour has come to add oil; the wick too must be lengthened. We are coming — before the need, after the sacrifice. — A Sister.)*

Rukmini read it twice. Then she went to the store-room and took down, from the highest shelf behind the brass lotas, a small steel diya the district had never seen her own, and filled it, and set it in her east window, and lit it in full daylight — which on this street of daylight-policy lamps was no longer eccentricity but communication: one more lamp reported for duty, from a house that had not answered any roll-call in nineteen years.

Dadi, at her quilt-frame, watched her daughter-in-law's new lamp catch the sun, and — the chronicle's eye is obliged to be honest even where it is tender — Dadi's needle stopped for a full minute, because Dadi had in her waist-fold, still unread, Rukmini's tamarind leaf; and she knew, the way women of that house knew things without violation of privacy, that the leaf and the mountain-flower smell came **from the same far country.**

**Tuesday's letter** came to the akhada by messenger — a wrestler of Turanan, twelve kos off, a youth courteous in the exact degree the akhada-code measures courtesy, who addressed Boodhi-ka-Veer with the full style — "Dikshit-ji, Boodhi-wale" — and held out a folded letter in both hands with a small bow, and whose closed fist, as he bowed, showed a braided dhaaga on its wrist in which was knotted a little **eight-pointed star** of red thread.

The letter, in a strong hand that pressed the pen too hard (all of Veer's fraternity knew that hand-pressure: *a strong man writing*), proposed nothing less than **a training-exchange**: one season, two akharas, "because" — the letter said, in its blunt pahalwan-Hindi which Veer read aloud in the pit to the growing circle of his fraternity — *"kyunki humne dekha. Toofan ke din aapke kasbe mein ek ladka jhatke se khada hua, aur poora zila uski ungli ki ginti gine laga. Aisa stance humne tees saal ki machligiri mein nahi dekha. Aata jaata humein hamesha aana tha. Par ab lautna bhi seekhna hai."*

*(Because we saw. On the storm-day a boy in your kasba stood up with a start, and the whole district began counting on his fingers. Such a stance we have not seen in thirty years of the wrestling-path. We always meant to visit. But now — now we must learn how to return.)*

It was signed with a mark rather than name — **an eight-pointed star** — and below the mark, in the same overpressed hand, the place and the hour: *Ravi ke baad. Machli-ghat ke aage wale akhare mein.* (After Sunday. The akhada beyond Machli-ghat.)

Veer folded the letter into his sling-strap with a wrestler's briskness and turned back to the pit — and that evening, recounting it at home to his mother, who was shelling peas with the unimpressed fingers of all mothers of famous sons, he could not explain — he admitted it into the register-session later, plainly, as one admits a bruise — **why the star-thread on the messenger's fist had made his splinted arm itch**, the way a healed bone itches before rain.

**Wednesday's wire**, the postmaster brought himself. He did not trust it to the boy. He came at dusk, on his bicycle which had no bell ("bells are for requests; I am an institution," the postmaster explained once, famously), and he found Ram at the shed-door, and he delivered the small railway flimsy with the particular care of a man delivering a relative's fever-report.

Four words, in railway code, engineer-spaced:

**CHAABI TAYYAR RAKHO. AA RAHA.**

*(Keep the key ready. Coming.)*

Franked from a junction of no consequence, signed with no name — and yet Ram, who read it twice in the shed-light, stood afterward for a long time with the flimsy in his fist and the sawdust unmoving in the air around him; and the neighbors would remember it, later — the evening the carpenter of the district stood *still in floating wood-dust*, like a photograph of work interrupted by history.

"Kya hua, Ram?" called the bangle-farmer across the wall, ordinary-neighborly.

And Ram said the sentence that the whole district's next week was built upon, quietly, without unclenching:

"**Woh aa raha hai.**"

*(He's coming.)*
The register-session that night was the first since the storm to be held **with all doors shut.**

The rule-book had no provision for it — the four rules of the temple had, by the district's decision, been adopted into the register's permanent margin (*travel-teeth*, Boodhi called them, *kur-charshedan ke daant), but doors-shut was new; doors-shut was the storm's lesson entered into protocol. On the grinding-stone: the register, open. On the blanket's four corners, the four. And because tonight's agenda had arrived by post, wire and akhada-courier, the household attended in strength: Ram by the door-frame, not sitting — standing was his chair; Rukmini with the mountain-grey card among her pleats like a second palla; Dadi at the quilt-frame's arm, her needle parked upright in the cloth like a flag planted in mid-campaign.

Ira conducted. This was already, by then, the way of it; the four had professions the way the district had lamps, and hers was the *agenda.*

"Teen kaagaz," she said. "Teen raaste. Teeno ka ek hi hafta. Report karta hoon — uske baad aap." *(Three papers. Three routes. All three, one week. I'll report — then you.)*

The envelope-and-two-others were laid in the middle. The mountain-flower smell made its way around the blanket's small republic and was received by each differently: Aadhya closed her eyes — the gardener's professional attention, checking the aroma as one checks a grower's pedigree-certificate: "Pahadi hai. Aur — " she opened her eyes, surprised not at the fact but at herself for saying it aloud, "— chiraaghon se *darr* wala phool hai. Aisa phool jo unchi arctic-dhoop mein banta hai, jahan deepak jalaana varjit hai."

*(It's a mountain flower. And — it's a flower* afraid of flames. *The kind that grows in the high cold sun, where lamp-lighting is forbidden.)*

Rukmini's hands, in her pleats' shadow, stilled on the card. Yes. That was the reading. Her diya in the east window answered the reading silently, three feet of lamplight away, steady as an indication.

Veer reported the star-thread akhada invitation; described the eight-point star; and Ira, at the description, did something she had never yet done in public — she stood, went to the tin box, and returned with the temple-expedition's fair copy; turned to the inventory's folio nine; laid the open page before Veer and said, with the brittle glee of a clerk whose archive has been handed corroboration by strangers:

**"Table-band ka aathva sthan. Rokadiya line ke baad wala — jo jagah khaali thi, edges crisp. Aath. Aath chhoron wala."** *(The frieze-band's eighth place. The one after the Rokadiya line — the empty place, edges crisp. Eight. Eight points.)*

The room performed arithmetic in silence. A star of eight points; a place of eight's reckoning; a waiting framework's *eighth* line, empty since carving-day, and now carrying — by the grammar of a wrestler's wrist-thread — an invitation.

"Aur teesra," Ira said, and for the third she had no page to flourish, because the third was her department's defeat: railway wire, yes — but **no sender traceable**, junction of no consequence; she had wired three junction stations to confirm the sending-clerk's signature and received, from all three, the same reply — *clerk on leave* — which, as she reported with the fury of professional pride, is telegraph-anatomy's way of saying **the wire wrote itself.** "Clerk's-leave teeno junction pe — ek hi din. Yeh possible nahi hai. Yeh — *likha gaya* hai. Likhne wale ne reylvay ko die-nahi, reylvay ko *bharata-udra* kar diya: station ko lagaa usne likha, junction ko laga station ne — beech mein reylvay khud *maan baitha.*"

*(Clerk-on-leave at all three junctions, same day. Not possible. This was* authored. *The writer didn't give the railway the wire — he* convinced *the railway: the station thought it wrote it, the junction thought the station did — in between, the railway itself* believed.)

Ram had not moved from the door-frame. At this, at last, he moved — came in, sat down among them on the blanket's edge in the manner a baton joins a rack of tools, and said the thing the meeting had been convened around without knowing:

"Uska haath hai." *(It's his hand.)*

Nobody asked him which "his". In the register's grammar the pronoun was already declined, capitalized at the head: the abacus-man's gender of office, the house-of-requisitions' severest letter-style — but Ram corrected their understanding with the patience of a man correcting a joint's wrong angle:

"Nahi. Jaj ke haath aisa nahi hota. Jaj *tolta* hai, yeh wala — **jodh-ta**hai." *(No. The judge's hand isn't like that. The judge* weighs; *this one —* builds.)

And he looked at the wire once more — CHAABI TAYYAR RAKHO — and something crossed the carpenter's face that the district had seen there exactly twice before: once when the baton had split on the demon's pulse, once at the bare-table when the invitation-leaf had arrived; the face of a man who has just received, from his own past, a tool he had packed away; *delight, unarmed, wrapped in obligation.*

"Vedant," he said, only the name, tasting it after twenty-six years like a man testing the temper of old knife-steel. "Mera shishya tha. — Nahi." He corrected himself with the exact pedantry he applied to measurements: "**Main uska tha.**" *(My student. — No. I was* his.)

*(And in the silence after, the room understood: there were, apparently, two kinds of pupils a man could have had in the province beyond the province — the one who had come last week with an abacus to inspect, and the one now writing with an engineer's letter-spacing to say he was coming back — and that Ram had somehow stood between the two. The file of Ram's nineteen hidden years had just acquired, in the register's margin, its second lead-pencil entry; the first remained abacus-shaped, nameless, ominous.)*

To the keeping-register Ira entered the four words and their provenance; and under them, after the meeting's silence had matured the suitable half-minute, the meeting's only motion — passed without vote, as the house's true motions always passed — entered by her pencil as resolution:

**"Teen patra — teen disha — ek hi hafte — ek hi ginati ke chhathwan se jude: STHITI — 'aane-walon ka prangan saaf rakhna.' Pavitra-taal-melan ke bajaye — TYAG MILAN: yeh kasba apni khabron ko mitron ki tarah pesh karega."** *(Three letters — three directions — one week — all tied to the count's begun-sixth: RESOLVED — "keep the courtyard of the comers clean." Not holy-confluence-fair but* Assembly-by-Departure*: this kasba shall henceforth present its news* like friends.)

Which is how the district's next institution came into existence: the un-named festival-protocol by which, from that month forward, every wire, letter, evidence, witness and stranger arriving in that town was *first received at the banyan-bench under lamplight* — the district's idea of customs-clearance, permanently prone, attended by whoever happened to be present, honest as daylight, open to the roofs.

---

Aadhya planted the tulsi on Thursday.

The choosing of the spot — Faguwa's charge had been *place she herself selects* — did not take her long. Not the garden; not her grandmother's mango-plot; not the haveli courtyard, all of which had offered themselves in the manner of dowry-properties. She took the two-year seed-parcel down the west lane at dusk, to the place where the storm's echelon had entered and stood its ground: **the kasba's west threshold**, where the milestone came up beside the culvert and the ground still carried, under its ordinary dust, the scuffed circles of eleven pairs of heavy shoes.

"Yahan," she said, to the ground, before she opened the knot. *(Here.)*

And she said her gardener's reasoning aloud to Rudra, who had come to hold the brass lota while she worked, because the chronicle's policy is to preserve practical doctrine wherever uttered, in fields or in basilicas equally:

"Darwaze pe rangoli lagate hain isliye nahi ki darwaza sundar dikhe — isliye ki jo andar aaye, use pehle ek **achhi cheez se guzarna pade.** Toofan yahin se aaya tha. Ab yahin se — yeh." *(You put threshold-decoration on a door not so the door looks pretty — but so that whoever comes in must first pass through* one good thing. *The storm came in here. From now on, out of here — this.)*

She sowed the whole packet — Faguwa's two-year store, saved across the keeper-line's twenty-two lean years — in a ring round the milestone's base, thumb-smooth, with the particular patience of someone planting not seeds but **a fence with roots**. Then she set her palm flat on the finished circle for a moment — the witness-palm's gesture, the one from the temple-frise, now standard grammar between her and anywhere that kept things — and the district's evening received the small non-miracle of her craft: nothing glowed; nothing sang; nothing rose from the ring to defend the gate.

But the ground, Rudra noted — he counted it, as he counted everything now, in the background like a pulse — the ground under the ring was *holding one breath*, lightly, professionally, the way the temple's Friday-morning kund-surface had held one star: **water remembering it was once the lid of something.**

She caught him counting. "Itna mat gino," she smiled — the gardener's joke, leaf-shaped, *dowry one leaf* beween them, never spoken since the temple but never absent either — "beej ko bhi neend chahiye." *(Don't count so much. Seeds need their sleep too.)*

They walked home with the empty lota; and the sky over the west lane, with the week's unsettledness still folded in it, showed one thin billow of true pre-monsoon silver, out of season by a fortnight, heading the same way as Wednesday's wire-franking: toward the kasba. Coming, per the register's motion, before the need.
And because the district's institutions are, in the end, only the district's habits with their titles spelled out, the first object ever presented for Tyag-Milan clearance under the banyan was not a letter at all but a man.

He was noticed Friday — the weekly lamp-change evening, when the whole kasba per custom had some errand at the chowk — by three separate citizens, independently, within one hour, which is the district's definition of a *public fact:* the station-master noticed him buying a platform ticket for a train he did not board; the grain-dealer noticed him weighing a handful of wheat with his eyes and getting the godown's scale to nod agreement without touching a single grain; and the barber — whose shop has the longest mirror in town, and who therefore sees what the street's geometry offers only obliquely — noticed him in the mirror's far corner, standing at the edge of the ghat-steps money-lender's awning, **looking at the kasba's old broken processional temple-bell** where it lay retired under its wooden canopy by the riverside.

The broken bell was district furniture. Broken decades before the register's era; the story came in kits, depending on who told it: dropped in '47's confusion, cracked in the famine-year's riot, cast faulty from the start in the district's one ambitious foundry-attempt. What all versions agreed on: **it had never been rung.** A bell that never rang was, to the kasba, a bachelor uncle — odd, permanent, unremarked. The man with the engineer's face stood looking at it for the space of a whole aarti-song downstairs at the water's edge; and then he did the thing that turned *three separate citizens' noticing* into the only topic of the kasba's evening:

He lifted the bell's heavy shoulder with one hand — crane-less, block-and-tackle-less, with a mason's carelessness about weight that no mason possesses — and turned it slightly in its frame, so that its long old crack faced the light; and he bent and **read the crack like a certificate.**

Then he put it down, exactly as it had lain, to the line. He dusted his palms together once — the carpenter's gesture, Ram's own gesture; the four who later heard the description all completed the sentence before it ended — and he walked up from the ghat toward the town, unhurried, a middle-aged man in a plain shirt the colour of cooling brass, with a steel folding-rule clipped in his breast-pocket where other men keep pens.

The roofs, of course, had reported him downward already, in the registry-voice's running count: *"Ek. Aangan ki taraf."* (One. Heading to the courtyard.) By the time he reached the banyan's lamp-circle, the district's customs-clearance — that is, whoever happened to be present, which tonight happened to be: Boodhi-ji on the stone seat with his three medicines; the barber closing shop; the postmaster doing his habitual second evening round which no regulation anywhere required; the station-master; and, in the bench's new seat — the account's seat, which tonight by the house's informal consensus was occupied for the first time — **Ram**.

The stranger stopped at the circle's polite edge. Up close, he carried the engineer's economy of movement: nothing on him swung when he walked; his voice, when he spoke, came out with the letter-spacing of Wednesday's wire made audible — every syllable dead even, as if typed:

"I examined your bell," he said, to the bench in general, in the region-accented but bookly Hindi of men whose trade has out-travelled their home: "Crack is not from dropping. Crack is *designed in.* Cast with a hidden taper-wall. A bell cast to be cracked is a bell built for a specific ringing it was never permitted — the sound would have gone *down*, not out." He said it as an engineer might say *your bridge's left pier is honest, the right one gambles.* Then, with the smallest formal turn toward the stone seat's end where the battened man sat:

"Ram-ji ne patra nahin bheja, maine bheja. — Aapki ginti ka patra, toh pehle se aa chuka tha. Bees saal se. Har saal ki woh **ek** entry, jo aapke register mein hai, usme se aadhi meri kalam ki hai."

*(Ram-ji did not send the letter; I did. — And the letter of your count had arrived earlier. Twenty years now. Half of that* one *annual entry in your register — is in my hand.)*

The lamplight moved on the bench. Nobody in the circle needed the entry named; the whole district's secret stood in their minds tagged the same way — the register's yearly line that signed itself in an engineer's even hand, always at the margin, always the same four inventory-words: ***ABHI TAK THEEK.*** *(So far, sound.)* Twenty years of anonymous inspection-stamps on a house nobody had known was under inspection.

"Toh phir," said Boodhi-ji, with a blind man's perfect orientation toward the voice's weight, "aaj phir kyun? Bees saal ke baad andaaze badalte nahi, beta. Jung ke mausam mein bandook banti hai, shanti mein chabi. Aap kis kaam ki chaabi hain?"

*(Then why today? After twenty years estimates don't change, son. Guns are cast in war-season, keys in peace. What work's key are you?)*

And the man stood easy in the lamp-circle's edge with the season's whole answer folded in the plainness of his reply — but before he gave it, the evening-arranged door of the council opened of itself: the four arrived together, returning the postmaster's borrowed hand-cart from the record-store; and the new man turned, and faced them, and his even-typed voice — for the first time — weighed, measured, and found wanting its own dryness; it went almost soft:

"Oh," he said, for a moment only an uncle meeting nephews. "Toh aap chaar hain."

Then, collected, formal, with the depth of bows kept to a right angle of nods — one nod, all four counted — he gave the bench, the lamps, the district, and the register their credential:

**"Mera naam Vedant Rao hai. Ram-ji ka shishya — aur Ram-ji ke guru ka bhi shishya. Tees saal ka zila ab mujhse hai. Chaabi tayyar hai?"**

*(My name is Vedant Rao. Ram's student — and the student of Ram's guru too. Thirty years of the district now belongs with me. Is the key ready?)*

And Rudra's seam — the gold-fissured webbing that had been, since the storm, the district's junior-most institution and oldest mystery — *warmed* at the name, wonderfully, unmistakably, like a stone in a river-line answering the first upstream snowmelt of the year, and the boy who wore it understood, in the body-logic the temple had installed in him, before the courtyard of his mind caught up:

*the barn-door's morning had started, and somebody who knew the cattle-hours had come to call them.*

# CHAPTER 42 — THE ENGINEER'S WEEK (Book 3, Ch 2)

Vedant Rao spent his first seven days in the district doing a thing that irritated one half of the kasba and deeply reassured the other half: **he measured everything, and he taught nobody anything.**

Not the four — not yet. Not the wrestlers. Not even the schoolmaster's senior class, who had come with slates in the hope of receiving the engineer's wisdom in quotable tablets. He sent them all away with the same answer, delivered in the typed-even voice: **"Pehle naap. Baad mein naapne wala."** *(First measurement. Then the measurer.)*

He measured the pump-house's draw and listened to its bearing-hum with one ear against the shell like a doctor auscultating a heart. He measured the lamp-inventory — forty-one lamps, four kinds of oil, two wick-gauges — and pronounced the bangle-wife's daylight-lamp policy *"the only piece of defense engineering this town possesses which does not know it is one"* (she received this with the supreme indifference of a woman whose policies had never once required the ratification of engineers). He measured the school-gate's new carving — RESONANCE — with his steel rule at each letter, especially at the R and the E and the second N, and the mason, hovering, heard him declare: "Gehrai sahi hai. Shabd galat nahi — dost galat hoga iske saamne." *(The depth is true. The word is not wrong — whoever faces it will be.)*

He measured the milestones on the west road, all nine, walking, in the district's own footwear — rubber slippers bought from the bus-stand shop, first purchase, self-funded — and at the ninth, where Aadhya's tulsi-ring was already four inches of tight green confidence around the stone's base, he stopped for one full hour. The roofs reported it; the ring was by then the district's shared pet; two schoolboys were dispatched by their own initiative with a jasmine-string to mark the spot. Vedant Rao stood there and did something none of the watchers understood: he took the salt off his own tongue with one finger — as doctors of the old villages taste the air and the soil — and tasted, instead, **the dust above the ring**, and then he knelt, not to the plants, but to the milestone's shadow-line, and said, aloud, in the manner of a man confirming a drawing:

"Yahan se ginte hain." *(They count from here.)*

And Rudra, who had walked with him since the pump-house in the official capacity of "the district's junior-most institution," felt the seam along his forearm turn — not warm, not light — **read**: a tracery of attention ran its line-length, as if the crack-net of gold had just been entered into somebody's survey.

"Yeh kya tha, sir?" Rudra asked, because Vedant Rao had by then been "sir" to the kasba for four days — the honorific had voted itself in, no council required.

"Tumhaare pitaji ka ek purana kaam," Vedant said, and would say no more that morning. *(An old work of your father's.)*

The second day he claimed the shed.

This needs recording with care, because Ram's shed was not lent, not even to family: the shed was the chapel of the district's truest grammar, and Dadi herself entered it only on the arm of the monsoon's first repairs. Vedant did not ask. He stood at its door at dawn with his folded rule and waited until Ram, at his bench, gave the permission that men of that grammar give — not words: Ram shifted his own stool four inches left, making room at the light, and Vedant came in and took the wall's measure as if he had been gone a week instead of twenty-six years, and they worked all morning without a sentence, and the baton's two splinted halves were at last **laid out for their true appraisal**: on the bench between two men who had both earned the right, Vedant inspected the second-break's spine with lens and caliper and said what the register records as its verdict:

"Doosri baar toota hai, toh ab ye hatyar nahi — **pramaan** hai. Hathiyar dusre ko batata hai; pramaan khud ko." *(It's broken twice now, so it's no weapon anymore — it's* evidence. *A weapon speaks to the other; evidence speaks to itself.)*

—— यहाँ अध्याय की असली इमारत उठती है ——

For the broken processional bell by the ghat — the one he had read like a certificate on the Friday of arrival — Vedant Rao now gave the district's council, at the bench under the banyan, on Wednesday evening, the whole of its certificate's contents:

"Yeh ghanta kasbe ka nahi tha. Yeh kasbe **ke liye** tha. Iska maker is crack ko hata sakta tha — nahi hataya. Casting mein taper-wall andar ki taraf muri hui hai: bajaawoge toh awaaz uthti nahi — **baithti hai.** Neeche. Zameen mein. Paani mein. Yeh ghanta bajaane ke liye nahi, *dubaane* ke liye bana hai."

*(This bell didn't belong to the kasba. It was* for *the kasba. Its maker could have removed this crack — and didn't. The bench absorbed this with the silence of a body which has finally been told the purpose of one of its own old bones. The barber looked at the postmaster; the postmaster at the station-master; and the station-master — keeper of timetables, lover of the correct placement of scheduled things — said what every person present was thinking:

"Toh dubaya kyun nahi gaya ab tak?" *(Then why was it never sunk?)*

"Kyunki," said Vedant Rao simply, folding the rule flat on his knee, "dubaane wale ko doosri taraf se uttara hua aana chahiye tha. Aaya hi nahi. — Ab aane ki ghadi hai."

And Boodhi-ji, who had sat through all of it with the blind man's thermometer for exactly how much a stranger is telling, asked the cleanest question: "Aur agar na dubaayein toh?"

Vedant's faced closed over, for one second, like a town's gate at curfew:

"Toh jo cheez neeche rakhi hai, woh uppar apne aap utar aayegi. Aisi cheez bandha patthar mein nahi rukti — bass itni der rukti hai jitni der yeh ghanta uska dusra sira hai. Ghanta kaam kar raha hai, adhdhora bhi. Crack ne ise kabhi gayab nahi hone diya — kyunki **gayab ghanta bajta nahi, aur bakel ghanta yaad rakhta nahi.**"

*(Then the thing it keeps below will come up by itself. Such a thing doesn't stay in a shut room — it stays only so long as this bell is its other end. The bell is working even broken. The seam's calibration began on Thursday, at dawn, in the shed, and the chronicle can give it only in Vedant's own method, because the method *is* the content.

"Kitaab kholo," he said — he had bought, on the second day, at the district's stationery-cum-tobacco shack, a hard khata of the kind ledger-clerks favour, grey board, blue rules, one hundred and ninety-two pages — "aur likhna seekho. "Jo naapta hai woh sikhta hai. Jo sikhata hai woh nap-ta nahi. Main tumhe tab tak kuch nahi bataoonga jab tak tumhe **khud-bataane ki jaldi** hai. Jaldi khatam karo — phir shuru hoga."

"Paani." *(Water.)*

**"Nadi ko napne wala hydrologist nahi hota. Nadi ko napne wala — isko hydra bhi kahte the purane log. Hyd kisi bhi cheez ka dhasaan. V.R. = dhasaan-naapne-wala. *(And the four, reading it much later, would note the coincidence they could never confirm or deny: that the week the district learned a word sounding like hydra,* water began counting back *in their friend Vedant's presence — pump-handles priming one stroke early, the well's rope-tautness arriving a heartbeat before his pull, the eastern ghat's morning line of flow tidying itself at the exact angle his eyes favoured — smallnesses, all of it; nothing the register could enter as anything but weather; nothing at all, except that the kasba's children noticed first, as children do, and started a game called "naap-ji ke paas paani," and Vedant Rao tolerated the game with the pained courtesy of a man who knows precisely what he is and has decided, evidently, that the district deserves to find it out the slow way —* the district's way.)

"Main naapne aaya hoon. Naapta hua aadmi aakhri baar kabhi dushman nahi hua: naapne wale ke paas doosron ki magnitude hoti hai toh apni magnitude ka bhram nahi hota. "Guru-ji. Bees saal se woh entry likhta aaya hoon — abhi tak theek. Aaj first time aake likhna pada: *ab aage likhna padega.*"

"Aage kya hai?" said Ram.

**"Aage — paanch bakiye hain."**

Friday afternoon belonged, by the old gendered grammar of the house — to which the new book's arrival had made no amendment — to **Dadi and the engineer.**

She sent for him. Dowagers of that district did not send for guests; it is only fair to record that Vedant Rao came up the courtyard's three stone steps with the exact demeanor of a junior engineer summoned by a zamindar-client, which the district found hilarious invisibly, and Dadi received him at her quilt-frame with the face she used for the tax-man, the only face she thought suitable for men who arrived by wire:

"Baitho. Pair mat failao mere takhte ke neeche."

"Tum naapte ho," Dadi said. *(You measure.)* Not a question. In her neighbourhood of grammar, all true observations arrive as statements.

# CHAPTER 43 — THE AKHADA BEYOND MACHLI-GHAT (Book 3, Ch 3)

The akhada beyond Machli-ghat was not sign-boarded.

Chandan had warned them on the road: "Patte pe likha nahi milega. Muh pe mat poochna; nadi se poocho." *(It won't be written on any board. Don't ask mouths; ask the river.)* — and indeed, once they had crossed Machli-ghat's ordinary morning commerce — the fish-landing planks slick as issued ledgers, the auction-shouts of the basket-wallahs, the two tea-fires smoking on their stones like punctuation — the river bent east around a shoulder of old rock, and there, in the bend's calm oxbow where the water ran back on itself a while before leaving, stood a courtyard-wall of weathered grey, door-less at the front, with a banyan behind it whose air-roots came down through the wall's stones in a dozen places like slow-motion entry.

"Yeh hai?" Chattu breathed — because where there should have been drums and the smell of oil and the thud of morning drill, there was nothing but the oxbow turning itself over, turn on turn.

"Yeh hai," said Chandan, crossing himself in the akhada-way — fist to chest, two knuckles — with the extra closing tap that one adds for places where the ground has a reputation.

They were received at the river-side, not the land-side. This was the first custom. The back-gate of the wall opened onto the water itself: a stone ghat of three steps, and in the oxbow a flat-bottomed *naav*; and on the ghat-steps the receiving-pahalwan — a grey man of perhaps sixty whose forearms carried the particular permanent fullness that the district's fraternity calls *mitti ka hissa* (the share of the earth) — without greeting-words, flicked the flat of one palm twice against the water beside the boat, and the boat came back across the oxbow **against its own dawdling will** to take them.

Veer understood then the first doctrine of the house he was entering: here the guests are brought to the hosts across water, and nobody is trusted who arrives dry-shod on the landward side like a tax-collector.

In the boat he counted — Ira's iravakya had commanded counting — the ripples the ferryman's oar made **upstream first**: the oxbow's water was so arranged by the bend's geology that it swirled one way and then the other, twice, like something practicing **returning.**

There were thirty wrestlers at the morning pit. And they were not drilling throws. They were drilling **getting up.**

For a full quarter-hour the three visitors stood at the pit's gallery and watched thirty of the district's strongest men — and theirs was a neighboring strength the whole region respected — throw one another down and lie there, motionless, one full breath, two; and then rise in one articulated wave — ankle to knee to hip to shoulder — without using their hands at all, and face again. Thrown, lie, rise. Thrown, lie, rise. No dust-spitting, no shoulder-slapping theatre, no glares. The pit was as quiet as a weaver's loft.

"Yeh — kaisa kaam hai yeh?" Chattu whispered, scandalized in his profession's pride: an akhada where the drill was *to be thrown.*

"Vapasi-vidya," said their receiver. He had introduced himself simply as **Bhanu-uzhaar**, "Bhanu of the heaps," the pit-names being what they are; he was, the two elder guests correctly guessed, one of the seniors, though he coached nothing that morning, only officiated at the water's edge with the ferry. "Jitna nahi padta remesh ko — **lautna padta hai.** Tumhaare kasbe mein bhi toh aisa hi hai? Us pahalwan ne dikhaya jo toofan mein khada hua. Us din humne dekha: *lautta hua zila.* Tab se charcha chali: yeh kaise? Aur humaara Naana-ji kaha — **jaake dekh lo.**"

*(Winning isn't taught to grown men —* returning *is taught. It's the same in your kasba, no? The wrestler showed it who stood up in the storm. That day we saw: a district that returns. Since then the talk has run: how is this? And our Naana-ji said —* go and see.)

---

Forenoon was formal: the pit-side greetings, the exchange of akhada-styles' courtesy — body-slapping grip, palm-on-palm measuring, the shoulder-grip look-in-the-eye by which two wrestling schools take one another's grade in ninety seconds. Veer was the abbasi for his side, per protocol, Boodhi-wale-Veer; and the Turanan men took his grammar correctly, he could feel it — they weighed his stance, his returning, the set of his eyes on the horizon-line; and what they gave back in their own posture was the old fraternity's best news: **manzoori.** *(Acceptance.)*

"Shaam ko," said Bhanu-uzhaar, "Naana-ji bulayenge. Usse pehle — ek dangal. Hukum hai." *(This evening Naana-ji calls you. Before that — one bout. Orders.)*

Veer had expected it. Hindi akhada-protocol under the old charter: a visiting side must be seen in the pit before it may be heard in the baithak. He had brought his langot folded in the sling-strap, and oil, and his splint's ration-cloth. But when the forenoon bout was arranged — pit watered, roster chalked — the name chalked against his was not one of the thirty.

It was **Bhanu-uzhaar's own.**

"Heaps-wale ki ladai karta hai bands doosron se pehle apna faisla," Chandan translated quietly at Veer's ear, Turanan customs being his old errantry: here the gatekeeper bouts the guest himself *before* the guest may see the guest-master. A filter — and also, more honorably — a gift: **they sent their own most-known against him first.**

They fought three falls. The chronicle does not need to make of it a saga; the register's account is four lines: *(i) Girl of the first pit: Bhanu, by cunning of heel; (ii) second: Veer, after one true stance-set — the second stance of the battle-week, the one that waits; (iii) third:* neither*, by the clocks; the pit had to be pulled apart, each holding the other's thigh like a lock, both refusing to drown*; and then Turanan's seniors, with a humour that the whole gallery followed into laughter, stopped it: **barabar. equality.** It was the readiest verdict, and the oldest honour.

In the dust, both men standing, Bhanu-uzhaar put his forehead to Veer's, once — the fraternity's forehead-salaam — and said, audible to the pit only, in the manner of a colleague at last permitted to transmit a letter by hand:

"Teen saal pehle humaare yahan ek aadmi aaya tha. Uska haath tumhaari kalai jaisa tha — *toota hua pehle se*, aur naap karta tha sab. Maine poocha: bhaago nahi kabhi? Bola: *jab bhagna pade, seedha nahi bhago — ghoom ke bhago.* Usi ne kaha tha: ek din tumhaare kasbe se koi aayega girne nahi — **uthne** sikhane. Tab samjha nahi. Aaj samjha."

*(Three years ago a man came here. His hand was like your wrist —* already broken*, and he measured everything. I asked: you never flee? He said: when you must flee, don't flee straight —* turn and flee. *He it was who said: one day from your kasba someone will come not to fall — to teach* rising. *Didn't understand then. Today I do.)*

*(Three years. The calm visitor of Faguwa's watcher's-note; the kneel-crescent of the temple; now the Turanan memory — one more line in Ira's great graph of the man who had been everywhere three years early, measuring. The register would note that evening: first grid-lock of the three-years-prior *measurer* and the naap-ji of the present week — the grammar of Vedant's twenty-year margin-entries suddenly legible with its secret twin: the field-walks.)*
They washed at the oxbow's steps first, per this house's second custom — sweat goes home through water here, not through towel — and squatted on the stone with their forearms in the current, and it was there, resting, that Veer noticed the wall.

The inner face of the ked-court's wall — the wall they had thought ordinary masonry from the outside — was **not masonry.** It was cobbles. Hundreds of river-stones of fist-size, laid in courses up the whole height of the pit's surround, each stone singled from the bend's shingle, and on each stone — he knelt with his forearms dripping to see — **marks.** Not carved: *worn.* Each stone bore two small grooves, as if someone had pressed two thumbs into soft clay once, years or generations apart, on the very stones, as though the wall had been — he stood up slowly, water-sounds surrounding his understanding — **built by bout-results.**

Bhanu-uzhaar, wringing the pit's soaked bandages at the next step down, watched the guest's face read it. "'Bare sawaal ka jawab dene wala patthar," he said. "Humaare yahan register patthon ka hai. Har dangal ke baad dono — jitne wala aur **haara hua** — apne do angoothe ek patthar pe dabate hain, aur patthar wall mein chadha deta hai. Jeet ka nishaan nahi: **muh ka nishaan** — dono ka. Ek haath jisne pheka, ek haath jisne gir ke uth gaya. — Isliye tumhaara baraabar wala faisla humein khaas laga: do hazaar patthar mein **barabar** wale sirf ikkis hain."

*(The stone answering a hundred questions. Our register is of stones. After every bout both — winner and* loser *— press both thumbs into one stone, and the stone goes up in the wall. Not the mark of victory: the mark of the* face — *of both. One hand that threw; one hand that fell and rose. — That's why your drawn verdict pleased us: in two thousand stones the draws number only twenty-one.)*

Veer looked along the wall's full run with a wrestler's blistered eyes, and for the first time in his fraternity's life he understood what a caretaker's staff lives by: **a house that remembers both hands.** His own district kept paper. This house kept stones. Both registers wrote the same one sentence, in different materials: *we fell, and we rose, and it counts.*

He asked permission — the guest's right — and with Bhanu-uzhaar officiating, the two of them took one shingle-stone from the bend's shingle-bed, and pressed their four thumbs into its wet giving face, two and two, side by side, the draw's double-signature; and the stone went into the wall at the gallery's shoulder-height, in a gap that had been left there — Chandan swore afterward it had always been a gap; Chattu maintained the gap appeared while they pressed — the twenty-second stone of draws, slightly darker than its century-old neighbours, damp as news.

"Aadh baras ka nishaan aisi hi hota hai," Bhanu-uzhaar said. "Bees hafte yeh hara rahega, phir sukhega, phir das baras sunega, phir kisi aur ko bulayega." *(The mark of a half-year looks like this. Twenty weeks it stays green, then dries, then listens for ten years, then calls somebody else.)*

They ate the akhada's midday — the strength-broth, the hot ball of ghee-bhakri, the milk nobody jokes about — and then the whole fraternity napped the wrestlers' sleep of two exact hours, and when the sun stepped down to the oxbow's level, Bhanu-uzhaar came and took Veer alone, barefoot, by the gallery's side-lane, to the inner court.

And the inner court: **the second doctrine of that house.**

It was a square of swept earth under the banyan's canopy, walled not with masonry but with **hung things**: from every branch reachable by a standing man's arm hung rows of small brass **lota-bells** — the priests' palm-sized hand-bells of the region's cheap bazaars, hundreds of them, maybe a thousand, each on its own string — and the wind off the river came through the canopy in pulses, and the whole court sang in the manner of a field of standing grain being combed: not melody; **listening-material**, a weather-station of small tongues.

In the middle of the court, on a low stone, sat an old man with his eyes closed.

Naana-ji. Veer knew the way of such chair-men from Boodhi's example: total stillness is itself the ceremony. He stood, sling off, splint-cloth pale at his forearm, and let the old man not-notice him for the length of three wind-pulses. The wrist-thread of red with the eight-point star lay on the stone beside Naana-ji — a specimen already delivered; the gallery's inventory, keys kept.

"Aathwe stanza mein galti hai," said Naana-ji, at last, aloud, pleasantly, in a Hindi old-coined enough to clink. *(There is a mistake in the eighth stanza.)*

Veer: "…kahan, Naana-ji?" — because not only had there been, to his knowledge, no stanzas this day, there had been no verses at all; the akhada of Turanan wrote no poetry that he had seen, all day.

"Jis stanza mein tum ho wahi. **Aath.** Aath chhoron wala taara humne tumko diya — tumne ginti nahi ki?" *(In the very stanza you're standing in. Eight. We gave you the eight-pointed star — did you not count?)*

"Ki thi."

"Toh suno: saat uske chhor ise zubaan dene ko hain. **Aathva chhor maun hai.** Aur tum log — jinme tu hai, aur tere kasbe ke do aur, aur ek likhne wali laundiya — tum *us* aathve chhor mein likhne ja rahe ho — jisme likha nahi jaata. Bilkul khali hai woh. Aur khali ko — bharne do."

*(Then hear: seven of its points are for giving voice. The eighth point is silence. And you people — you among them, and two others of your kasba, and the girl who writes — you are about to be written into that eighth point — into which nothing is written. It is completely empty. And the empty — let it be filled.)*

*(Empty. Crisp edges. The words were out before his eyes were, the wrestler standing on the kept-earth court suddenly back in the doorway of the temple's inner room, in side-light, seeing a place that had been kept open for four hundred quiet years: the eighth line. Blank. Prepared. Waiting on them.)*

"Naana-ji," Veer said, and his voice went down into his chest's drum the way it went at official moments, the voice his fraternity called *the bridle*: "Woh jagah — woh **khaali** kyun hai?"

"Khaali nahi hai, beta." Naana-ji's eyes were still shut; the river-wind combed the hung bells; a thousand small tongues said nothing in chorus. "Khaali *rakhi gayi* hai. Alag baat. Khaali cheez ko koi pakad sakta hai. Khaali **rakhna** — usko garna padta hai har subah. Yeh garna tumhaare kasbe mein saaf dikhta hai. Isliye hum ne tumhaare naam kaftar diya."

*(It isn't empty, son. It is* kept-empty. *Different things. Anyone can hold what is empty. To keep empty — you must tally it every morning. This tallying stands visible in your kasba. That is why we sent the mark in your name.)*

Then the old man opened his eyes. They were — Veer's account to Ira's notebook is one of the register's plainest folios — *the colour of deep river at the ghat's edge, green-black, not strange but* weighted, *like water that has been carrying something for a hundred years without being* asked *whether it minds*.

"Are beta. Log tarif karte hain tumhaare kasbe ki — jinhe dhang aaya daran-ka. Humne afwah bhar paayi: kasba — ginti ka ghar; jaj — abacus-wala aa gaya tha aur wapas gaya; ghanta — gira nahi; **ghanta — gayaa.** Log galat samjhe. Yeh kasbe ka kamzori nahi hai — yeh kasbe ka **sauda** hai. Angeet ka sauda. Ginke rakha toh laane walo ka bharm hota hai. Tumne ginti shuru nahi ki — tumhe ginti **sauumpi gayi** thi. Vidya saundi nahi — vidya **sauumpi** jaati hai."

*(Ah, son. People praise your kasba — those who got the manner of it. We heard the rumour further: the kasba — house of counting; the judge — the abacus-man came and went back; the bell — didn't fall; the bell — went. People misread. This is not the kasba's weakness — it's the kasba's* transaction. *A transaction of the sixteenth part. Count and keep, and the bringers are under illusion. You didn't start the count — it was* assigned *to you. Knowledge is not taught — it is* consigned.)

"Ab batao," the old man said, not unkindly, "us aathve chhor ke liye — tum kya laya ho apne kasbe se?"

And Veer Dikshit, Boodhi-ka-Veer, who had won one of three falls and drawn the last by being pulled apart, stood in the courtyard of a thousand lota-bells and thought of what, truly, he had brought: not a trophy, for his baton had stayed home on Ira's grammar; not a boast; not even, properly, a victory. He had brought a wrist that was broken and re-set right-handed; a defeat by pulse; a seat at a midnight council; and the strange new family custom of registering what had happened to him so that others could count it too.

He said so, in parlance, short as orders: "Apni haar laya hoon, Naana-ji. Ginti mein likhi hui."

*(I've brought my defeat. Entered in the count.)*

And Naana-ji — Veer would swear it later to three listeners independently, all of whom believed him instantly because the sentence bore all the stamps of Naana-ji's house — Naana-ji **smiled with both eyes shut again**, saying:

"Toh tum sahi ho. Jaake bulao use — jo raat mein padhata hai."

*(Then you are right. Go — call him who teaches at night.)*
He who taught at night was, at the hour of Veer's escorted walk through the inner court's rear paddle-gate, nowhere visible. The paddle-gate gave onto the river's after-bend — a clean mutt's-length of sandy shelf under a strut of the old rock, and above, the evening sky's first assembling treasury.

The man sitting on the shelf with a brass instrument was not large, and this disappointed Chattu in his profession's first reflex (Veer stopped him later, kindly, with the explanation all the fraternity used thereafter: **taare chhote hote hain** — *stars are small*). He was spare as a marker-pen, dark as a sextant's brass, dressed in the cheapest oiled-canvas jacket the region's bus-stations sell; and around his neck on a leather thong hung not a saint's token but an old hinged **horizontal ring**, the kind field-surveyors once used to hold an angle against the sky-line.

He did not rise. He said, in a spare, level, unsurprisable voice that made words seem like distances:

"Baitho. Pehli baar dekh rahe ho —" and he indicated the east, where the first star was being told on by the river's flat, "— toh dekho. Mujhe mat dekho. Mujhe baad mein dekho jab dekha hua ho."

*(Sit. Since you're looking at the first time — look. Don't look at me. Look at me later, when I've been looked at.)*

So they sat, the three travellers, on the shelf, and they watched the star field come out over the bend of the water — obediently at first, then with the gratitude of the unprompted: the night from this shoulder was a *true* night, no town-light, and the vault assembled in its unheard-of numbers, and the man's manner of talking while stars come out was — they would all retain this — like **a book being read aloud without a reader in the room**:

"Pehla tarika: taaron ko **jaanno mat.** Taare apne aap hain — insaanon ke naam mat do; janamnaam deta hoga toh samundar dekhne lage taaron ki tarah. Doosra tarika: taaron ko **jinke jaante ho**, unse gino. Taare, dev, dhanuskool — sab ginti hi ke roop hain. Teesra: taaron ko **pension ki tarah** lo — roz laga chhut-ti rahti hui chhavi, kabhi badhti nahi, magar kabhi kam nahi hoti — jab tak tum moodh nahi ho jaate. Chaarak humesha bhatakte hain. Tarakash — nahi."

*(First way: don't* know *the stars. Stars are theirs own — don't give them human names; if baptizing-name is insisted, the sea begins to look like them. Second way: count them from whom you know. Stars, gods, the bow-squads — all are just shapes of counting. Third: take the stars like* pension* — an ever-unfolding image, never growing, but never lessening — so long as you don't turn dense. Travellers always get lost. Star-gazers — never.)*

*(It is here that the register is obliged to disclose what any of its field-readers would have seen by the second sentence: that this was the grammar of one who measured* by the sky the way Vedant measured by water; *that the constellation-diction was no affectation but the working vocabulary of a man whose arithmetic had, somewhere in his biography, come entirely* down from the air.)

"Tumhaare kasbe mein," said the man at last, still not turning, "ek ghanta rakha hai jo kabhi baja nahi. Sabha mein ise ek kamzori samjha — angoothi toot gayi shaadi se pehle. Par jo aathva chhor khaali hai wahan, usme yahi likha jaana hai: **ghanta baja nahi kyunki woh aasman se naap raha hai, zameen se nahi.** Aur aasman ki naap zaroorat pe atki hai — tabhi uska bhangra hua din tum logon ne dekh liya jab toofan aaya. Tumse pehle bhi chaar the — unme se sirf do lautay the. Yaad rehna: **woh jo aasan thumakde hain, unhi ko zinda rakha jaana hai** — baaki sab taare hi hain, dekh lo."

*(In your kasba there is a bell that never rang. The assembly understood it as a weakness — a ring broken before the wedding. But into that empty eighth point this is to be written: the bell didn't ring because it was measuring* from the sky, *not from the ground. And the sky's measure is pending on need — hence you finally saw its dancing-day when the storm came. There were four before you too — of whom only two returned. Remember:* the ones who dawdle easily are the ones to be kept alive; *all the rest are just stars, look at them.)*

Then he turned, and for the first time faced them, and Veer saw his eyes — ordinary in colour, the register must report faithfully; it was their **attention** which had starlight's properties of seeming to arrive from far away even while pointed near.

"Tum woh ho," Veer said — the sentences of awe are always ungrammatical — "jinhone patra bheja. Taara."

"Taara toh bahane hain, beta," the man said — and smiled, the first smile, which aged him backward twenty years and cured the travellers of all road-fatigue in one instant, by a witchcraft none of them ever tired of describing — "**taara chirr hai jis se aasman ginaa gaya. Main Dhruv.** Der se dikhta hoon — par jahan khada hoon wahan se hilta nahi. Aur tum jahan se aaye ho — wahan ab tak bhatak rahe the sab. Ab tikne ka waqt hai."

*(Stars are just excuses, son — a star is the pin-hole by which the sky gets counted.* I am Dhruv. *I appear late — but where I stand, there I don't move. And the place you've come from — everybody there was still wandering until now. Now it's time to get stable.)*

---

He agreed to come — Naana-ji's diktat having predated the negotiation and the brotherhood's gifts already packed by Bhanu-uzhaar's committee — under three conditions, which the register entered as the house's first set of *visiting-customs* and which the district observed with the mania of a new religion of protocol:

**Ek — andhera chahiye.** *(One — I need the dark.)* He would teach only at night, and only when the lamps of the district had been *turned to their domestic minimum* — the bangle-wife, hearing this, actually laughed for the first time in the register's recorded memory, and issued a by-law, *only observable on nights when he's teaching*: her daylight-lamps would take their first-ever regulated rest. "Rog wela aadmi," she pronounced, approvingly. *(A rules-obsessed man.)*

**Do — machhli mat jalao.** *(Two — no torches.)* "Mashaal ka dushman nahi hoon. Par mashaal se tarashe log taaron ki roshni kabhi nahi dekhte, aur bas wahi roshni hai jo lautne mein madad karti hai." *(I'm not the enemy of torches. But torch-schooled people never see the stars' own light, and that is the only light that helps in* returning.)

**Teen — ek hi saath.** *(Three — one at a time.)* He would not address a crowd; he would stand under the sky with one student at a time, and the lower stars' hour of lessons would be seven.

"Veer pehla," he directed, packing the angle-ring with the care of a surveyor boxing a sovereign instrument. "Baaki teen ke naam mujhe mat batao abhi. Jab woh aayen, woh apne naam **khud** rakhenge aasmaan mein. Saabit naam nahi chahiye mujhe. Naam aate hain."

*(Veer first. The other three's names — don't tell me yet. When they come, they'll place their names in the sky themselves. I don't need proven names. Names arrive.)*

Which is why, when on Sunday forenoon Veer walked eastward home through the kasba's west gate — and the tulsi ring, in the sight of three idling loaf-sellers under the culvert's arch, *turned its hundred small faces from eastward to westward to greet him, then turned back east again, having saluted* — the register's Sunday entry was already written and dry, in Ira's ink of the course of midnight sessions with Bhanu-uzhaar's reports, and said only this, in the manner of a district clerk catching the avian scent of an era on the turn:

*"Toofan aaya tha aur hum dekh nahi paaye the. Ab jo aaye — hum gine nahi, hum **khade rahenge**. Do khaber — teen hain: ek kaagaz, ek taara, aur ek — jisne paani se baat ki. Saat mein se do — aa gaye. PANCHVAN ABHI BAHI HAI."*

*(The storm came and we couldn't see. Now whatever comes — we shall not* count *it, we shall* stand **in** *it. Two tidings — they are three: one paper, one star, and one who spoke with water. Of seven, two have come. **THE FIFTH IS STILL COMING.**)*

# CHAPTER FORTY-FOUR — THE SISTER WHO WALKED IN AT NOON

---

> *"Jis ghar mein kisi ne kisi bahan ke liye unnis baras diya jalaya ho — us ghar ka darwaaza doston ke liye nahi bana. Uska darwaaza kahin aur ban ke aaya hota hai, aur woh sirf bahnon ke liye khulta hai — bina baje, dupahar mein."*
>
> *— MOTHER HANDA, who kept lamps longer than most people keep faith*

---

*(In a house where someone has kept a lamp for a sister nineteen years — that house's door was never made for friends. Its door was built somewhere else, and it opens only for sisters — without a bell, at noon.)*

---

She came at noon, which in our district is the one hour when nothing ordinary works — the hour Rengwa ascribes to the water-clock's dripping rest, the hour anxious mothers wick-wait through, the hour in which nobody's business is open — so that, the register may now report factually, *no one saw her come*, in the precise sense that no eyes at the moment of her arrival were upon the street she came down. West-road lied that morning: the temple had unanimously declared that the wind would pull east all day, and they had even guessed it was thirteen past. Nothing did, of course. Twelve, thirteen— none of it helped. At sharp noon, when the district's mid-mouth lay empty and wet-Rengwa-watch-lamps sank to candle-glow, she walked down Simri Deora's eastern crest, un-sign-boarded, un-announced, on feet made of shell-shapes, and the town's only witness was Aadhya's tulsi half-ring, whose hundred small faces had been facing east — then *turned west, to greet her, then faced east again*, although only an urchin from the by-chettan of Asadhat Chowk happened to see the turn, and he later swore it had taken the ring one breath which it was breathing anyway.

He who writes our register's industrial entries says this so plainly now — that no school thereafter could keep any child from knowing *exactly* where the visitor stood, and when she came, because our kasba is a small-craft wall on which everything registers: chulha-dust, eye-lash, dew-squeeze, khakhra-boil, lead's-eye-winking. We knew she had passed the millet-roof topped tank — precisely because no crow or shikra dipped to drink, which alone draws the town-school's attention — then near the **foul-faced grafters near Baherao tank mound** a couple of saddle-barrows named Sutan, Laal-Uttar & Co waited respectfully on post-line because she was following the westward stone-fox fence with her palms turned as if writing water; then she crossed the *elbow of the nala* (where old Thakran saab sat) and lay down her travelling-basket before the melon-woman's deterence-market stall (a wheeled canvas-trolley, home-made, aluminum hot-water carrier in hand), smile-faint, palms still playing with something nobody allowed themselves to name.

She looked neither suspicious nor beautiful; she looked — this was the statement entered thereafter, since she had been coming all along, though the town's eye had been turned elsewhere — **ekdum theek**, just exactly so, one-of-seven's simile getting its ratio right — and that was recognition enough for three households the register names why: because the trolley-puller, who had kept in her pocket for two years a torn corner of sack-paper (ink-headed fennel, coriander, ajwain, mustard) from the day of the south sewer pipe's breakfast roar and Aadhya's foreheads seed-sowing, *remembered it exactly and turned up exactly at noon*, as did the ledger-ish name of Ishwar Chand Sharma's shop — "Ek Bahan" in a card-style — because she had kept remembering it nineteen years in a window, as did the chin-lip bouncy high-born fortune-less plain-clothes no-dress woman of Simkha bazaar (since confectioner says so) viewing her exactly at noon — she fainted with relief.

---

She stopped on the drum-post where the irrigation ditch enters the karha's lowest stubble-field (the bend where the register's west-water flows uphill per instruction). She did not knock. She needn't. She stood quietly with her palms still moving, seeming to count things that were there already, and she looked in the direction that our town — all five hamlets, one hundred seventy-nine registered households — had been accepting as the *required direction* for two weeks and more. Her hairpin was incense-smoke ash, the kind they use wherever coriander is kept, and on her forearm there were broken brass bangles of different gauges which she had clearly not *bought* but *found*, because their ellipses had mean-below-wrist nature and pale-sapphire smears, like fifteen years wear-and-tear histories just as they were.

She closed her eyes briefly, chanted low (nobody heard it, so it's not written; lore gave her at least this), lifted her gaze slowly, and at the words aloud in the plainest Magahi-adjacent inflection the west-side'd ever heard:

**"Koi ghar hai yahan jahan ek diya unnis baras se jala hai — aur jiska shish kisi bahan ka intezar mein kabhi nahi gaya tha?"**

*(Is there a house here where a lamp has burned nineteen years — and whose glass-chimney never went out waiting for a sister?)*

She asked of nobody; she spoke to the lettuce-sellers' weighted stones first, then the dirty-buffalo fence-gap, then the pathori-lane, then the threshold-carved doorposts where Salman's father's teak had first heard the district's steps; and it was the tulsi half-ring — *Aadhya's tulsi half-ring* — that answered. It gave no sound, but it turned its faces *west* a second time, taller than the urchin remembered, and — the register insists — one hundred clay pots were now leaf-cups bearing grass, and each blade stood perpendicular for nineteen seconds, counting.

She moved on to the lowering-near-west-bank cottage where a lamp had now been seen burning in daylight for exactly a fortnight, carried there every morning by a soft-voiced woman with that careful stiffness of one who will not see a star unattached, and who has never yet come across one that doesn't fit her, nor one she has been *unable* to fit. And at that cottage's threshing-floor, Sitara laid down the brass-bangled burden, not rudely, dusted her palms, and said to the window — in that quiet measured voice the district was to come to know over five years as one keeping the liturgy's Green Oath written in morphine-augmented reeds:

"Main Sitara. Aapne mujhe bulaya tha. Toh main aa gayi."

*I am Sitara. You called me. So I came.*

And the lamp in the east-facing window — it never went out, it just stopped *needing* to be lit by wick, and burned instead by the simple fact of the woman's having heard what she had kept thirteen years locked in the sheaf-readers' stalls finally name itself at her sill.

---

The door opened before the last syllable had died, and on the verandah's second step there stood — there would be no describing her except in the district's later school-explanations: *one of the women who, never-at-all doubting, had kept the lamp burning with* **man** *from the start* — Mother Handa herself, forty-one years' seniorness melting exactly like frost at noon, who knew what this call must be as soon as she heard a voice not her own say "bulaya tha" — "you called" — for once without irony, entirely ordinary, alarming only for the burden it *didn't* carry.

There are such women, in the register's five-fold accounting, who keep *walking knowledge* for others, and who make their whole house's fortune by the graphic representation of *waiting-for*, until the thing waited for arrives exactly as it was, one who will not disappoint it. Rukmini — the register uses her domestic name as the familiarity from ch-41 settles — had been **combing out** her lamp-basket of wick-coils when the voice came. Her eyes went even more watchful. She had already swallowed her first breath; she did not take a second; she removed the woolen shawl from around her throat and dropped it softly on the reed-mat; she moved across the verandah in the most ordinary way — as one moves from cutting plants to planting them — she reached the sill with her arms, and in the most sounding tone said, **immediately**, for her own hearing much more than for any district's history:

"Aa gayi. Dekhti hoon."

*She has come. I see her.*

And the lamp at the east window — which had been raised there daily since the card with the steel diya's circular ghee-stain — the lamp finally did what lamps are *meant* to do in the register's arithmetic: **it stopped being necessary**, because its twenty-year work of *keeping* had arrived, and its work now, in exactly this hour, at exact noon, in ordinary May heat, was to *welcome*, which is a different thing from keeping, and so it simply *went out*, with barely a wisp of smoke, immediately replaced by its quiet fulfilling named twin — the second lamp in the west window, which Rukmini, without any foolish district doctrine, now lit with a fresh wick, *the other way round*.

---

"You have kept it twenty years," Sitara was saying on the threshing-floor, looking at the lamp-flame which had just gone out — one's breath held, at noon, exactly nineteen years prior — the whole sheaf-thickened sky collecting itself over hills of this immediate left, "and you will not trim it until the guest has seen it. Now the guest has seen it. It can go to its sleep — jaisa ki sabko jaana chahiye; apna kaam poora kiya toh woh so jaata hai bina kisi seenazori ke."

*(As everyone should go — when their work is done, they go to sleep without any fuss.)*

Rukmini did not need telling; she said merely, minding the wick-ash in her palm: "Bahan, darwaze pe khada hua makaan nahi dekha jaa raha tha kab se?"

*(Sister, why are we staring at the house standing at the door?)*

"Makaan nahi dekha jaa raha tha," Sitara answered. "Ghar dekh rahin ho."

*(It's not the house that wasn't being seen. You* are *seeing home.)*

"Toh aag laga do," Rukmini said — two syllables' economy, May-thick, barati-lamp heat — "kyunki darwaze pe khade makaan mein bhi ghar ka dekhra pehla hai, aur ab ghar ka dhyaan wahi hai."

*Then set it aflame — because even a house standing at the door may see home first, and home keeps preferring that view.*

And Sitara came in. There was no ceremony. There was merely, across the three-step threshold, the district's longest period of welcome ever afforded any stranger, which lasted exactly the time of a wick lighting and going out concurrently, and ending with — the register stresses, since the town would slot this into its records as **the useful detail of the fortnight** — a two-palm hug exchanged over the threshold cleat, in which all that either woman carried was transferred, or made at least legible, and nobody outside at that hour noticed any of it.
Inside the haveli — which the register names haveli only out of courtesy, since its roof was Thakran-saab-grade teak rather than supervision-grade black-slate — everything resumed fitting. That was the haveli's real discipline: **nothing had ever *mished* here**; nothing in this household's twenty-year patient arithmetic of fittings ever had to be *un-fitted*, and Sitara's arrival was not, in any household register, a *guest* but a *fitting*: an already-sized corner into which a wanderer could now be slid. Rukmini had, in advance, prepared for her the correct spot by the east-window shutter opposite the tongue-Vedant-quietope lamp, because it was the house's one location where — as she had said in her private grammar — *agli subah ke pehle roshni ka aarti nah marwa gayi hai; doosri saans wahi khainch li jati hai* (the first dawn's arati-light has not died — but the second breath will be drawn from exactly there). She had made the spot *available*, not *inviting* — there is a difference, though the words sit close — and Sitara, who had crossed thirteen years of bedecks to get here, slid into it as the second lamp wick slid into its holder in the west window opposite.

The chair — this specific fact the register notes because it was the district's very best argument against the universal theory that all waiting is useless — the chair was Thakran-saab's old hall-seat, which, once the father had left the household, had been nobody's seat and everybody's, because a Teak-and-Champ done right will care for twenty years of *now I'm using it* without complaint. Now it had a specific occupant. It would have her again, in this same manner, for exactly twenty-four years, until the register does not need to tell on which day she would get up for good.

It was at this point that the whisper started up again — not the whisper of words, but the whisper of the *wick*. Mother Handa, hands no longer trembling from twenty-year restraint, went around the haveli and lit every lamp that had been kept out of sight, because her daughter (the register may now disclose) was not the only one who had kept lamps; her *mother*, too, had, and her modest-lamps had fitted exactly — every unmarked mud cup, every rope of drying herbs, every spoon of tempered resin — and now they returned to their *outdoors* state, all thirteen of them, and the afternoon sun left its normal business and all the eatables on the thaali-stalls looked briefly like they belonged to a village though we *were* a village; the lamps' oil-searched flames lit up the Shiv-ratan's vertical mahadev-coloured portrait, and polished the tarnished faces of the tin-wrist-clocks, and for the first time since Father Handa had gone, all of the lamps faced east, because there was an easterner in the house. (Though east was where they had *all* come from, but the word lost meaning there for a while.)

"Dekhiye," Rukmini said to Sitara, on the second verandah with Aadhya present (the register's emphasis), passing her tongue-Vedant reason into both hands at once — she held Sitara's left hand and her daughter's right together, both by the wrist, not for divination but because that is usual practice where lamp-summaries are concerned, "**ab is ghar ka ghar chahta hai ki dono haath ka mazza ho — dono haath ki tali ek, dono haath ki rassa alag; dono jaan alag — zameen same.**"

*(See — now this house's house wishes that both hands carry flavour alike — the two hands' palm one, the two hands' string different; the two souls different — but the ground the same.)*

Sitara knew, without need of translation, the simplest translations of this grammar; she had grown up on its street. She did not say anything elaborate back. She only said **"Haan"** — Yes — the register-pronunciation being a *yes* that also means *it is so*, meaning that one acknowledges with one's own life the word and the world which come upon one, and stays standing while they settle — and then she squeezed both their wrists a trifle — just a trifle — to say first that she carried this word, and second that she would carry it further as well, which is the meaning of *haan* in the register Magahi-inflected, and which is not a fair bargain, but it is the only one ever struck here.

---

In Mother Handa's cook-shanty, unprepared dahi-bhalla was being prepared anyway for exactly the right-sized guest, yet exactly the wrong-sized month (too early for the good lassi), and the mid-mouth noon sun was in fact rigorous in filling the thaali stool-leaves with all its punitive persistence; and here, of course, Mother Shiva put the newly washed leaves on flat brass platters with mantisa-measured care, and here, finally, the tulsi half-ring — whose faces had first faced west, then returned, adjusting themselves — could be permitted a quieter rhythm, and the thousand green-leaves now showed themselves sixteen-by-sixteen aligned, a living board of emerald *shapabs* in Aadhya's ash-gourd vine patch where they'd lately been flipped for packing, or so the register must report.

It was *observed* here — and the register's accuracy deserves scribing — that the pitiyaan board (the peepal's) had its face turned toward the stable-year sun; the melon-woman's cart stayed parked; the urchin who had seen the *turning* had run back to the paper-shop and gone coal-eyed at the sight of his own father there, ink-covered since morning (his charge was to keep *ayat* of what worlds arrive in the street — he was exactly right and knew nothing); the grate in the grange still stood open; the abandoned pile of Thakran-thakurdwar's broken slates tipped their salt-glitter so that from up there you saw the second lamp — the one replaced by the *twirling-coin* lamp's purana-shaped conic (candle-form of her fruit-orchard earnings) — steady enough that its broad bowl struck one's glance against the haveli wall's wet mud until it looked like painting, meaning just that whatever kept it alight was *absolutely normal*, which was itself a comment.

---

In the house, where all thirteen lit-but-unseen lamps had seen my sister's arrival just now — the register urges care: the register gains its authority only from a ledger's eye — *Mother Handa* spoke the first liturgy aloud. It was not a liturgy from any book; it was the register Magahi-ritual she had been composing in her mind for twenty years, half-sentence by half-sentence, since the day Father Handa had told her (before stepping out to settle some matter of road-levelling near the eastern bend's heap's last legal standing) — *ikkees sawere ke baad, jabaan khulegi aur ghar aa jaayga* — *after twenty-one mornings, the tongue shall be free, and the household shall come home* — and as twenty-one mornings had now run into nineteen years, she had only the *second half* of it memorized, which was, in her daughter's translation into the register's standard idiom:

**"Sau bar jismein ek bar bhi nah rahi — saugar se pehle — uska ghar nahi ban jata pura. Jis din wo wapas jaata hai jis ghar mein wo daal thahraaya tha — usi din ghar poora hota hai. Main abhi bhi — puri hoon."**

*(The one who has left even once in a hundred — yet before the farewell — that household does not fill completely. The day the one who was left in that household returns — that day the household fills. I now am — full.)*

The lamp in the west window — the *second* lamp — became calm. The lamp in the east window, heretofore necessary, now snuffed and silent, allowed its final thread of smoke to climb the teak beam and say goodbye, like a fate-recorder who has just finished the day's account. And the third lamp — the *new* lamp, which Mother Handa would thereafter keep in the middle window *between* the two, and had already placed there — the third lamp, which nobody had yet lit, was the one to which all the others looked for instruction, because that lamp was going to have to light for somebody else.

---

"Tumhe abhi," Rukmini said, to the unlit middle lamp, "kyunki woh jo aane wali thi woh aa chuki hai — toh ab kya hai?"

*(You now — because the one who was coming has already come — what happens?)*

And in the lowest voice of her daughter, Aadhya — the register has insisted on the necessity, for the register's sake, of including every voice that speaks at this household's lamp-happening — she answered the twirling-coin lamp's conic bronze tongue-quietly, in a voice which the register may now chalk up as Sat-Sandesh:

"Mere saath ginti jalegi. Ahista. Don." *(The count will burn alongside me. Slowly. Two.)*

Then the tulsi half-ring outside — its pots' faces loaded eastward — all flicked their leaves *westward* at once, and tilted their cups *so* minutely toward the haveli's east wall that the register's field reader, had she witnessed it, might have guessed — correctly — that the easterner inside had just greeted the house with a *haan*, and that this same easterner, despite all geometry, had already been there.

---

At this point the district's lamp-carl — who in the register's grammar placed the tongues of the communal standard every morning (and who had twenty-year knowledge of which street would answer first and which lamp would with whom) — went around to *the haveli's* east wall and removed the west-gate tulsi half-ring's turning with his own palms, and placed a hat there instead, because the wind had changed and the mid-mouth had lost the adhrit of the turn of the hour; but it was *no matter*, it was all much of a muchness now, for inside the haveli there were now *three* lamps burning in daylight (east necessary-snuffed, west and middle), in a district where daytime lamps had once been the private folly of only one woman, *and she was snuffed now*, her job finally done.

And Sitara, fitting perfectly in the available corner of the east-window shutter — the register's old failure becoming, in the district's later memory, a modest triumph — spoke the register-tag ka-se-into aamar: **"Jitna dekh rahe the, dekhne se zyada main sulook rahi thi. Ab sulook mat ho. Ab dekhna ho."**

*(All that you were watching — I was watching too, at greater cost. Stop watching now. Start looking.)*

The house's lamps, which kept in mind all their hangman's holes — of what they'd seen and what they'd kept — now settled. And Aadhya, in the courtyard, on the stone where she had first discovered in her palms the geometry of grounding, felt for the first time in five winters that *somebody else* was grounding — *also was* — and the ground, sensing this, held still for one breath *more* than it had held still for her alone. The seam under Rudra's ribs, in its unreachable flat on the left sabha-bhadra's twirling-coin memory, did not stir. It merely noted, in its stilted Magahi voice — *three now tend their lamps. Of seven, the fifth is come, and none of them is wrong.*

The household kept its late-afternoon rhythm — which is the only rhythm a household that has just been *filled* can keep, because a filled house does not celebrate; a filled house *resumes* — and it was in the resuming that the district's other watchers found their meanings.

Chattu and Chandan, returned from Machli-ghat without Veer (Veer having been kept behind by the night-teacher's first lesson — seven hour-marks standing under one star, alone, hearing the distinction between *falling* and *returning* said aloud for the first time), carried home from the Turanan akhada two things: one stone each — Bhanu-uzhaar had pressed them, out of courtesy, from the bend's shingle — and one sentence, spoken by Naana-ji with the canopy of a thousand lota-bells stirring above him: **"Ghar jaao. Dekho — aaj kaun sa diya jal raha hai jo kal nahi jal raha tha."** *(Go home. See — which lamp burns today that was not burning yesterday.)*

So the two boys went house to house, faithfully, evening-wise, doing the audit that is a boy's tariff duty in every distressed era — the duty of saying what is lit — and they counted the district well enough until they reached the haveli on the karha's edge, and then they stopped counting, because this house was doing the wrong arithmetic. Three lamps in daylight. One snuffed whose whole life had been snuffing-resistance. One fresh with no oil history whatsoever. And a woman on the second-verandah whom nobody in the district had ever seen before (or had ever seen *not* there, depending on which of the register's field readers you asked afterward — the melon-woman maintained she had been coming for weeks; the tea-seller swore she had been coming for *years*; the ledgers say *one noon, one crest, one shell-footed step* — and the register adopts the plainest entry, being oldest).

"Kiska ghar hai?" Chandan asked.

"Rukmini-mausi ka," Chattu said. "Dekh — teen."

They stood a while. Then Chattu entered it in his palm-leaf tally — the register he kept because the district had taught him to: **"Karha-kinaare wala ghar: diya TEEN."** And under it, because loyalty to Naana-ji's grammar runs in the trader's blood, he added: **"Jo kal nahi jal raha tha — aaj WOH bhi hai."**

*(The one that wasn't burning yesterday — today that too exists.)*

---

Inside, the house was doing the oldest arithmetic of all — the arithmetic of eating. Mother Handa had not been wrong about dahi-bhalla being the wrong-sized month, but the household had been making food for guests longer than guests had been making themselves scarce, and there were, by the count of the thaali-stall's leaves, fourteen plates' worth laid out — three too many, as always, because a household that has waited nineteen years does not start skimping on the day of its fullness. There was the coarse-ground chutney which only the melon-woman knows how to pound into a paste that keeps for ten days without gold-leaf salt; there were the three kinds of bhujiya which the old korri-ledger keeps only for the household's own return-visits; there was the flat-bread which, because the year's wheat had been buried good and deep (the register recalls the wheat-putting at chulha-minus-six), was still warm from the morning's bricking; and there was — the register insists upon its solemnity, because food is our only certain arithmetic — an extra bowl which nobody remembered laying out, and which Sitara saw first, and into which she ladled her own share with exactly the unconsciousness of one who is about to teach, and then stop teaching, and then begin again when the student returns.

"Mujhe dheere se batana," she said to Rukmini over the flat-bread, in a voice that carried not at all beyond the thaali, "**kyunki tumne unnis baras woh thaali khachra mein rakhi thi — aur thaali wali cheez kabhi kachra nahi hoti; thaali wali cheez sirf baaki rehti hai.**"

*(Tell me slowly — because you kept that thaali in the rubbish for nineteen years — and thaali-thing is never rubbish; thaali-thing always just remains.)*

Rukmini, whose grammar had never run to more than farming terms, looked at her own hands, and answered — through the leaves — with a farmer's exactness:

"Baaki rehna hi kachra hai, bahan. Toh jo baaki reh gaya — usko utha ke thaali bana diya."

*(Remaining is rubbish, sister. So what remained — I lifted it and made a plate.)*

There is no other entry in the register's whole grammar which fits this household better. *What remained — we lifted it and made a plate.* And it was for this reason, alone, that Aadhya's father had gone east, and never once written of it; because what is *remaining* cannot be written. It can only be lifted.

---

When Dhruv finally came — at exactly seven evening-marks, as his first condition demanded, via the water-gate at Machli-ghat's other bend rather than the road (the register's field entry: *no torch; alone; carried from the shelf where Veer's seven lessons, in forty-nine days, will be said complete*) — he found the haveli in its three-lamp state, and did not comment on it. He was a man whose attention arrived from far away, and whose grammar did not run to domestic comments; but he stood at the threshold, placed his oiled-canvas bag — hand-stitched, watermarked, content-wet — on the mat with the surveyor's exactness of settling a sovereign instrument, raised his hands — flattened, palm-forward, as one shows when the groves are tall and all markers must be plantable — and said, *to the district's keeping*:

"Aathva chhor abhi maun hai. Par maun ko bhi awaaz mil rahi hai. — Main sirf ek raat rahoonga. Kal main jaaonga Machli-ghat. Par raat bhar — main yahin rahoonga. Tumhare teenon diye — mujhse pehle gine gaye hain. Chaaro taraf se — chaaro."

*(The eighth point remains in silence. But silence too is getting its voice now. — I shall stay only one night. Tomorrow I go to Machli-ghat. But the night full — I shall stay right here. Your three lamps — have been counted before me. From all four sides — four.)*

Rukmini looked at the man on her threshold, then at the three lamps, then at her own hands, and answered — the district's formal welcoming, one lamp to another — with the register's oldest instruction:

"Andar aa jaao. Ghar ke saamne khada hona tha toh bahaar kyon nikla tha tum?"

*(Come inside. If you were going to stand before the house, why did you go outside?)*

Dhruv, who had been counting stars since before most of the district's shutters had been built, smiled with the smile that cured road-fatigue, and came in.

---

The register closes here because at this exact hour, eastward on Machli-ghat's shoulder, the last band of the Turanan akhada's stone-wall — the wall of two-thousand thumb-pressed stones, the register keeps insistently — received its twenty-second stone from the district's traffic, and the twenty-second stone was not placed by the hand of any wrestler, nor by Bhanu-uzhaar's committee, nor by Veer (still standing under the night-sky), nor by Dhruv (who had arrived only in the evening and would not let his night-lesson crowd the house's earlier arithmetic).

It was placed by nobody the register can legibly name. It was placed *by the ground itself* — by the irrigation-nala's elbow where the rain had run during the storm, by the tulsi-threshold under which the second lamp now burned steady, by the whole karha-field's patient arithmetic of *remaining-and-returning-and-becoming* again.

Bhanu-uzhaar saw it next morning when he came through for inspection, and placed his own thumb against the groove he'd pressed the night previous, and looked at it one minute too long, and said — to nobody, in Turanan's dialect:

**"Do hazaar mein barabar ab bhi ikkis ka nishaan hai… par doosri bar — kisine patthar ko chhoo liya bina patthar ke."**

*(In two thousand, the draw-mark is still the twenty-first… but the second time — somebody has touched the stone without the stone.)*

Which is exactly what a house does when it receives a sister it has been writing for nineteen years in lamp-oil and wick-prayers and west-window glass. It is taken without being touched. It is entered without stepping. It is *always* — as the register praya often, even to itself — already been there.

# CHAPTER FORTY-FIVE — THE FOURTH LETTER, WRITTEN IN GROUND

---

> *"Waqt ka pehla aashirvaad hai — ki jo bhi aam lagta hai, use shodh ke liye kisi ko jaldi nahi karni padti."*
>
> *— BUREAU OF THE REGISTER, MARGIN-CLAUSE FOR ORDINARY DAYS*

---

*(Time's first blessing is this — that whatever looks ordinary, somebody need not hasten to research it.)*

---

The district resumed its Thursday on a Tuesday, which in our register is a way of saying that everything had been too full to wait for the day it was meant for. That is how ordinary life is — it does not wait for its scheduled day. It moves around the other days, the unimportant ones, the ones nobody flags on the steeple calendar, and fills them first. Thursday has been rescheduled to Tuesday since chapi time. The spiritual district — or whatever we are, now — lets the calendar keep its own counsel, but it lets the *life* free, whose grander obedience is to breathing and to the dove-tailed Tamility of useful outcomes.

The haveli was, by now, fully occupied. By noon, Sitara had folded her travelling sack — the low-woven cotton with the diagonal edge stitching, whose interior the melon-woman had counted nineteen inches of (someday the district would upgrade this to its full twenty-one — but today, nineteen was complete) — folded it into the correct rectangle, placed it under the string-bed in the east room, and said: "Ab main ghar mein hoon. Ghar ka jawaab ghar se nikalna chahiye." *(I am home now. Home's answer should proceed from home.)* Meaning: *I shall now answer for the house in it. I will not be visited; I will be visiting.* Rukmini nodded, completed yesterday's half-filled load-book with today's double lines, which is the sign of acceptance in the register's accounting — the day has been entered for two.

Thereafter the register only needs to chart India's life, which was — as usual — in the same slopes as before: mul-gravel, pump-wash-mouth, pulley-greased lifting-device clicking on its bolt at the bottom of the karha's second pond, the stagnated shallow fallen-to-nothing trench where the last spillway ends. Nobody *watched* the district resume its Tuesday; we just built what was left, which had been building since before Thursday got scrubbed. The register, however, was unusually prosperous in its field inputs because of a *single* unprecedented event: **the register, for the first time in thirty-one years of issued pages, had begun to use its own doctrine** — the custom-forms then divinely labelled *grievance-distribution* and now understood, through the grammar of all four covenants, as exactly the "everyday visit" a town owes itself — to keep the district's own *names* as they came, without asking whether they were stars, bells, ledgers, or merely the small water colours of a mid-morning's drip.

Which in turn meant that there was now a *fifth* office in the District's municipal front-offices: the office of **the fourth holder** — the one who keeps not lamps, not stones, not stars, not water — but the ground-earth's *patience*, the whole karha's field drifts, the load-khata's correction-treek, the grange's leak-mud, the bindhu's pathori-lane gutter-word, the thousands of leaf-piles outside the vacant-Fiang's shop-window. He was Rudra Shore, alias Ram Rao, alias *naam-se-pata-itna-hai-ki-yehi-ek-purana-haath-hai-jo-pit-ki-geherai* *mein-chhaya* (one old hand in the pit's depth) — the register's arithmetic since ch-31 onward knows him as **Rudra**, which is the name by which the storm came back, and by which the district's keeping-lamps — now at three — recited their right-to-be-named daily. He had been fulfilling his tariff-book entries on weekday mornings (route: pit, grange, cattle-stand, banyan-bench, karha, light-sheet) with that quiet patience of one whose day consists of *being measured* surroundings — of being the *unit* for its own neighborhood, without whose walking the district's stones would have no requirement to wet, and without whose crossing the animals would have no hour to wish themselves toward.

Today, Tuesday, he stood waiting at the west-gate, where the tulsi half-ring — now back from greeting all salutations — had positioned *itself* back to its original stance, facing the Deora rim's rise. On Aadhya's tulsi half-ring — *now* showing two faces (the broken one healing nearly at ridge-height) — he saw something new, and which only he would *now* see, because the thing requiring a watcher had just become ordinary: **the hundred grass-pots had all turned their cups *toward the haveli***. Not east-crestward. Not west-bendward. Toward the house whose three lamps now burned in daylight, where Sitara's travelling sack rested folded in the east room, whose twenty-year ghee-stained window-lamp had — at last — gone out. The pots were, in their own arithmetic of green inexhaustible leaf-lifting, asking the ground to keep the house counted now, because the one from the east was *settled*, and the keeping's arithmetic now had to add a Fourth Letter to its grammar (three, plus the ground), because the ground's letter had, with the fourth lamp's absence (it went out *by itself*, which is its own answer, since no lamp runs from itself), been *delivered* now, and the tulsi-ring's arithmetic — never wrong in the register's account since BEFORE ch-31 — was now asserting, clear as log-book ink: **"ghar ka chautha patta — gir jaane se likha gaya hai."** *(The house's fourth page — has been written by falling.)*

Rudra picked up his empty load-basket, checked the fixed-in-ring banyan-leaf in its bottom corner (yesterday's stage, biting dust), and began walking the circumference — the district's day's cap — to check what number of things had fallen overnight.

According to the daera-erai's arithmetic — which is the register's best arithmetic, since it was written by what walks when nothing watches — three matters urgently required him. First: in the whet-baton's river-mud, somebody's foot-prints before dawn; second: a stone at the grange seems to be keeping its own count of the drop-shortfall in the leak-mud; third: the pit's bottom tier — normally quiet overnight — has been reading *something*, since the seventh and the twelfth takhta both show the charcoal-marks of standing-for-extra, which in the register's custom means that somebody stood there, leaning, in silence, for no reason, and then left.

He completed the morning round, his breath counting the nine measures in-and-out (never ten now; nobody taught him why), and he stood — as always — in the town's middle-ground, which is the already-open stoop of the melon-woman's deterance-market cart (which is the cart she does not push because she is always standing on it — of keeping, she would say, *not* of selling). At this point the register pauses to note, in its own precise diction, that Rudra's *station* is not the milling-house; nor the district's sabha-baadari; nor the knot of the two rivers (which the register will address in Ch-Anantam's later text); nor, really, any other place we have named. His station is simply *the district's middle*, which — in the register's arithmetic, written by the *ground* — is wherever he stands. That is all there is to it. He walks; it counts; it counts where he walks.

So Rudra counted where he walked today, and Rudra — being a keeper — took the count carefully: **Things dropped in last night's night-wind: 7.** **Things returned by morning's fog: 3.** **Things whose count was now changed by another's counting: 1 — the tulsi-pots.** **Things whose count's integrity had newly arisen: 1 — the house whose third lamp now stands open.**

It was at this third count — the one where the district's middle changes — that the register's usual district-bureau reader (whose ordinary name we do not write for Bureau-format) — whose *function* we have already named in ch-41 (the *one* who receives the three-counter-observations from the far-off register-server) — received a message from a voice it had not heard before, though it *was* the one who issued the first warrant: **"Jinka naam likhna hai unse poochh raha hoon. Teenon — tumhare kasbe mein, yahin baith ke. — Main kalsi ka haath hoon."**

*(I am asking those whose names are to be written. All three — in your kasba, sitting right here. — I am the hand of Kalsi.)*

But the register, being honest, must record it as it came, without inflection. The name Kalsi — central to the format's third table, the one where "sub-se-verah-prathah-pratham" is customarily entered as supreme — was unknown in the district's account-books prior to this hour, though our ledger-inks have run up all the lord-kala sections since ch-31's outbreak. We know only that *Kalsi* was the one whose hand is mentioned in a district's very oldest proverbs, in connection with the perimeter where one pours the ash of dear-departed in — in those accounts Kalsi walks ash-first from the west gate to the karha's eastern bend. It is this custom the register adopts in the current text: that any hand from Kalsi is as valid as the ground itself. Rudra noted it, counted it, and continued walking.

---

Then there was Ira's ledger, which Aadhya now opened systematically — **Drishti** — though nobody around her had ever called the heavier thing with which she was born as anything. She carried, here, a small hinged notebook of the cheapest possible make, and used a graphite tip *no longer than her nail*. She had made no entries in the ledger prior to *today*, because her eye would not stay long enough on any one item — some pulled away here, some rushed there — but now, at this very hour, she looked at the district's ordinary middle and — for the first time in the register's data — **she wrote the district's *middle-name*.** It was, to put it plainly, simply this: **"Sthaayi."** *(Settled.)*

She did not assign it as loud as one of the district's own insiders-hours marks; she assigned it, and then some more, simply because there was room, and because nobody inside who had any stomach to tell her "no" had, by the eye of the register, done so yet.

She wrote it in the register of her own notebook, written across the top margin and not, like all prior entries, buried in the graphite residue of her left hand. She wrote it as *observed true.* And the district, accordingly, saw her life move back into place — not because she moved it, but because she finally *read it*.

She was seated at a pea-field's easternkershmah (the flat mud pad where the old women hull peas after dew-wait), with, by her lap, a full sheaf-summers' worth of read peas—one-read-pad, not all un-looked-out by fog. Beside her stood the household fern; at her feet, the footpath that normally keeps the karha's eastern edge in dew (now brisk daylight-stiff at eleven a.m., since the east wind in this district closes off the dew-line by eleven, no exceptions).

The pea-hullers are the district's best keepers of news. They have seated counting duties for all day; they know who walked in and out from the two villages; and on their mid-morning tea break they know from the standpipe what somebody in the east bend has been calling the bell by, these twenty years. *The* standpipe was where Ira was seated today. She heard, in the code that pea-hullers universally deploy — the code which is very much a *drishti* of *ordinary matters* because it consists of callers naming what they see in lowercase — the hullers pass the news: "*Ek bahin aa gayi*." *(One sister is come.)* And the second huller's counter: **"Teesari diya jal gayi. Ghar ki."** *(The third lamp is lit. In the house.)* And third huller's precise qualification: **"Chaape pe patthar sone de diya hai. Us patthar ne."** *(She has allowed the stone to sleep on the printing-block. That stone.)*

Ira's Drishti *was* listening. She was listening, quietly, holding one unshelled pea (the twenty-seventh) between her thumb-first-joint and the squared-off nail of her index finger — an action that required not unsophisticated attention because until steel-smooth completion of ledger-line in the notebook of her own (which by now had the same observed settling *also* noted in its date due page), she could not move it without violating the drift of some other sheet (Aadhya and tulsi-ring being the two that provided the equilibrium, the load wall and *deterant* being the two that provided the halo around the middle-name).

The pea-hullers — all five old women, one of them ninety-two — broke for tea at eleven-oh-five. Ira's Drishti had, by then, finishedits reading. She now closed her notebook-ledge, placed it on the fern basket, opened her knees slightly, said to the pea-hullers (in the dialect of the local green-rooms): **"Haan — dekh rahi hoon. Tum log bhi — jab hulling kar rahe ho — apni chapatiyo ko — ghar ki tarah — baante ho."**

*(Yes — I am seeing. You too — while hulling — your concave palms — you distribute — like houses.)*

And the old women — experienced in this grammar, because everyone had been on the receiving end of it — understood the youngest huller had been *finally understood*: that *their* hulling had been such a text all along, opaque no longer — the five hullers *were* five lamps visible, and their pea-fields consist *only of letters* (because a pea-tree continuously *self-writes* its own book of bloom: the lowest heart through to the narrowest pod) — and with a nod they returned to their hulling, and Ira's notebook ledger recorded the margin-line closed.

---

In the meantime, further-east, still standing erect beneath the specific star whose name our district keeps only as *the eighth* — was Veer, waiting in Night 3 of the night-teacher's seven-night lesson. Dhruv's night-teacher is a special school: it is held only while the district stands open in view (it is not fly-school; it is not Wall-class; it is *sky-class* — which the register defines merely as the class where no lamps may burn, where the only permitted measurement-tool is oneself trained throughout to see the northern constellations' interchanges, where the only permitted thought is *this star doesn't set*). Night three is the lesson where the student stops needing the teacher's words.

The night sky doesn't flash to life during night three; it merely *keeps being there*, which is, in Dhruv's estimation, the same thing as teaching.

He had — during Night 3's twenty-hour-mark — done this so far: shown Veer the difference between what a star is and what a star is called (**taare ko taar mat bolo; taar tum unko ho** — *don't call a star a rope; you yourselves are their rope*); shown Veer the loopholes by which (when walking east-west, as shepherds) one can carry the northern constellation off the northern constellation; shown Veer how to find the sky's Fourth Letter (**jyotir-ganana ka chautha akshar: SH** — the *SH* of *shines first, counts afterward*, and is not SH because he stays fixed, but because *he* — Dhruv — is written there; the district would take nineteen more years to fully realize what was being revealed).

Veer, in Night 3, standing team-wick-decked (since neither torch nor felt cap was permitted) under the actual sky (no awning, no pole to wait against), had provided the following demonstration of progress for himself: he had not blinked at the sky since nightfall; he had followed the constellation from the moment Dhruv's finger left-off pointing it out; he was, at this hour — the register certifies Night 3, hour-mark twenty — walking the sky the way a chaste student walks the earth: *unerringly*, he had located the fourth letter once, and not blinked once.

Which is all he needed to do to pass the lesson — which brings us, seriatim, to the district's second new office: the office of **the night-sky's keeper**, whose sole duty (and the register now enshrines this for the next nineteen years of the district's accounting) is *not to lose the star*. *(Keep it; everything else burns later.)*

---

By eleven-thirty the district's *deterance-market cart* — which is also the district's middle-ground — was empty. The melon-woman had taken the cart on to her next stopping-place. The five hullers remained, their activity now moderate (the day's dew-wait over and bright-clean work properly begun). Sitara, in the haveli's east room, had said, to the open window, in the hearing of none (since the district was elsewhere at their own accounts): **"Diya ke baad kal subah mujhe har-roz baante paap ki koyee bheed nahi hai — sirf weh hi hai jo din bhar tumhe ghar mein baithna chahta hai."** *(After the lamp, tomorrow morning I have no grievance-crowd-shaped distribution for the day's sins — only has who wants you to sit at home.)*

She was speaking to the lamp-she-had-lit, the one at the west window — the second — which was, at that moment, doing something the register had not entered before in thirty-one years. It was not burning at a steady level; it was *tapering its own one flame upward* at only the tip, like one of the school-children's count-off test letters — like it had been taught to count, and was now whispering its numbers one by one: one, one-one, one-one-one, etc. The register does not ordinarily describe lamps, but this time, we must. The lamp's tapering tip had — though lighting its own self down, and outward — **reddened enough that its red edge counted, in veins, all nineteen years** the mother had kept the first lamp alone — and now, on this Tuesday at eleven-thirty, was finally returning the smoke of all nineteen years' worth of caught-keeping to the east, to the lamp that had already gone out: the *keeping* one — and from its grey red-colored tip this return-smoke moved (the register reads it as a pulse) through a single *pause* between both windows and out through the roof's thatch — the register's only light-gate to the sky (we do not call it that in books, we call it *the fourth lamp*, the one that never needs gold-leaf because it has gold already in it, in daytime).

She had just finished saying "baante paap" when the register's own keeper (who had not yet closed margin-line, he still being on 'Sthaayi,' his own entry-line) heard her voice — the register must now record this minor administrative asterisk. The keeper does not ordinarily vary the entry categories, but today, in format, one page was left blank after the entry "Sthaayi," and above the blank, the keeper had then written, *because the sound was fresh enough to write*: "**Chaar akshar: SUVY (su = well-formed), vay (= air), written together = the Fourth Letter.**" In the language of the district this word simply means *the breeze that letters make as they go past* — but the keeper wrote it *twice*, because the first time the page remained closed.

---

The register closes here because after eleven-thirty everything enters a period in which nothing is legible to the register alone: Rukmini was seen speaking alone to the house across the karha; Vedant was seen walking in the direction of a weep-willow (not on the register's map); the district's children were seen counting the second lamp while Sitara watched from between the windows, counting *along with the children* — who read the lamp *correctly*, that is — as if they had been keeping count for the last nineteen years along with it, and now that their numbers matched, the lamp's tapering could be withdrawn, and the house's final consonant could settle.

At twelve, precisely, the register declares the 44th chapter's *Paanchva* (as the district terms it, the *fifth-is-coming*) had expired; the fifth *is* here; the sixth *is* assumed; and the seventh — who, at this hour, is still somewhere far-off, doing something we don't need to name — is — the register insists — *fine*. We close.

Yet the register *must* enter the twilight, because the district's grammar required it — the twilight being the hour when the district's arithmetic gets *checked* — and in checking, the register cannot avoid seeing the district's pulse-rate while it still dinner-pounding, flour-spilling, and lake-cursing conversation was still kept in circulation by the household's various circles.

The women of the kasba gathered — as on an ordinary Thursday — at Aadhya's ash-gourd vine patch to pass-on what our register must now term *the district's cut* (*cut sequence* = task split), which is: **who went where; who was seen carrying what; who was seen *not* carrying; and whether anything had been allowed to heal.** Tonight's cut-notes were:type A two (the two ripe-gourd-hoists taken from the ridge that morning by the hullers' tea-group, who had not returned since); type B three (the pit-mouth buckets, all three traces now used by the *three lamps* in the haveli to replace their drained hold-books); type C four (the peepal's smallest trough, given to Sitara to fill herself; Rukmini's supply of sheaf-paper; the whet-baton's complete wipe-down; the distribution of the cart's wood). No troubled entries; no named entries; no Kalsi entries.

The people's speaker (whose sort of "speaker" role we know) — today's was the seed-sow-carrying register-keeper, whose person we locate in the koepi's coalsmoke hut (which is *also* the district's net-booking bureau-court, though called "smart-centre" elsewhere) — read the cut sheet aloud, which means the district's eight eligible voices could all hear what the district's eight eligible familias had decided without any one of the familias having to walk to any court to say it aloud. Her reading, in correct Dakhini-parsi, was the register Magahi register-word:

"Yaari, taaliyan padegi tab tak jab tak AATH lamp mein teenon jala rahegi. Jab tak PATRA lekar nahi raha — usi ka raasta hai."

*(Friend, the palm-fronds will strike until all eight lamps keep three alight. While one is not yet carried by PATRA — one walks ITS road.)*

Which means two things, which both must go into the register: **the district has begun walking the road of the Fourth Letter on behalf of the rest of the letter-keepers** (which was in ch-41 the rakhwala's own calling; so it now has *three name-holders* community-held); and the district is, by letter-and-ledge, on the road.

Afterwards, the second lamp put out its second taper — the smoke-return being complete — and rested. As for the uphill walk at the Machli-ghat-riparian oak-rounds, where the *only* truly closed-circle-track word-tally of day-dimension-learning may still be had (under Naana-ji's banded oil-lamps, which are *not* like the haveli's lit-lamps, being of band-oiled-iron and only lit because the district has maintained NIGHT-glow at specific points for the next fourteen days), Veer gave name to his progress by marking a circle place on the ground next to his bandolier: **kinara** *(fraction: one-eighth of anything larger)* — and then gave his hand's first closed-eye ability the seven-breath walkout *kia* (was done) and the skin-speech-keep of the night's teacher the full-day-stealing bend: **"Aankh ke pichhaad mein jo nazre na padi hai awrat ka naam — kal main use dekhne mein kamyab ho jaaonga."** *(By this time tomorrow — I will have already succeeded in seeing the woman's name which the back-of-the-eye has not yet seen.)* Then he resolved — not as an aim but as a *merao-te-laana* (register's compound=daily-peak's settlerate) — to *weather* exactly nineteen more nights of this (because nineteen nights equals the selling week in our register barter: the week one has to put one's appetite into at Temperance-Mart to earn back one's *prices*), by which account, Awkiness' tree is marked "G." "G" is the district's sum-total, being the *g* in *ground* *settled* — and once marked, the chattu-belly ringed-forth with as much statement as anyone reading the register's slate-ink ledger has ever had the good fortune to add to (a single pat of one's own admission to the *first* of two embankments) — in short, everything hereafter would refer to itself as ready.

For there is no mentor called Awkiness whose letters can be posted after Awkiness's own tree is declared reading-partially-clouded — but that must be left exactly as-is for the next chapter. We close this present chapter, as the register's custom requires, by returning to the haveli, where Mother Handa — the haveli's earliest keeper of lamps, whose eyes have been glass-lamps since ch-41 — was just now closing shutter no. 2 (the westerly glass) so that the second lamp's flame could steady (this steading being what in the register's grammar denominates a **keeping** — it takes the sunshine out of the flame and makes the flame keep *itself*); and Mother Handa, who had kept her own lamp lit for twenty years, now saw that both lamp-lines had been *finally rendered unnecessary* by the third lamp's arrival, which was, of course, what a mother has always already wanted: to be *unnecessary* to the lamping, because the lamping is the house itself. She said so. She said, in Hindi, the register's tired grade of already-done-class:

"Dono diye gaye. Ab ek hi kaam shesh hai — bachaa hua."

*(Both lamps are given. Only one work remains — what was left.)*

"Bachaa hua" is our register's closing word, and it appears in certain letters' last-mail: the one who *left-behind* it. And though the household's current presence means that any outsider treating this household as a road-basket would see only three women, the register — from its superior position (from the middle of the district's walking-and-reading, by whose eyes these lines, having passed through them, have become the *archived* word) — must record: **it has been counted seven.** The count stays seven. The roads count seven. The pit's bottom has been counted seven. And the pointing of the night sky to the district's own rooftops — now, with night set to arrive and, correspondingly, later lessons of *names* (which was why Dhruv was applying these lessons at a rate of one name per night) — tomorrow the *listing* will begin.

The listing of the district's names, which is the list our register uses itself.

# CHAPTER FORTY-SIX — WHEN THE ACCOUNT CAME HOME

---

> *"Mercury shows us only what it can hold. Whatever the mercury cannot hold, the mercury was never made to know — the mercury's maker never told it because the mercury's maker did not ask to know."*
>
> *— THE CREDIT LEDGER OF KALSI BROS., NAGPUR, folio 119 (closed-ledger, first opened for this district these thirty years)*

---

Manikarnika Kshetra's evening worship-hour, on the twelfth day after the third lamp was lit in Nobody's house. *(The register adopts its own dating; the city calendars would call this the third day after the full moon of the full month of the mango showers — the season's wettest under-tail, when every account which has been stashed under a stone for the year decides whether it is dry enough to be answered.)*

The worship-hour was not worship — by eleven-oh-two it was something else, because the worship had, forty minutes earlier, un-worshied itself into the air it was lit for, and that air's testimony had, by eleven-oh-two, arrived at the gates of our district, and the register — whose first rule is that nothing can arrive without first having been counted by somebody — insists upon its own arithmetic in admitting the evidence: **we knew it by the smoke.**

For a century, and perhaps longer, Manikarnika's smoke had been accustomed to prove *identity* by *ascending*. Funeral-pyres at the ghat — even with all its many fires lit in the same small hour — smoke goes *up*; that is its ancient instruction and its one piece of universal arithmetic, as the women at the karha's edge always reminded their children: *dhuein ko upar jaana aata hai; zameen neecha hai; aur akhand taar bhi sirf neecha jaata hai* (smoke knows how to go up; the ground is low; and even an unbroken thread only goes down). It is for this reason that some years ago, at the first grass-storm, the saying went around the district: *jab zameen ki aag upar uthti hai, toh kasbe ka hisaab poora hota hai* — *when the ground's fire rises upward, the kasba's account is complete*. On that morning (which the register identifies as the morning of ch-31), the wheat-fields' straw *did* rise upward, because the fire was already within them, and the district's count was thereby read.

But on this evening, every pyre at Manikarnika Ghat — everyone holding the flames and accounted for as ash-carriers at this hour — **every pyre was burning *downward*.**

This was not smoke behaving differently; this was smoke being told to behave differently by something the smoke could not refuse. The pyres were — the register's field entry; the fire-watchers at the empty-house of the cremation-watchers, who kept the two wail-lamps burning nightly, saw it with their own eyes and only much later gave a report which our register accepts — **the pyres were still their own flames, burning their own wood, at their own hour's rate; but the flames themselves had, in some strictness invisible to the ordinary eye, *turned inside-out*, and from the tip of each ignited tongue, the smoke did not rise; it poured *downward*, toward the ghat's lowest step, which, at this hour of the full-month mango rains, was already under two feet of water — and the smoke pooled there, on the water, as it would have pooled on mud, and stayed there, covering the water as if the water were stone.**

The register's second rule says: anything that has been seen by more than the register must be confirmed by nobody. Confirmation came from the first of the bhishti-women who had gone down to fill their bags: she was called away by her own bag's refusal, since her plastic bag remained full and the bag's seams were strained *not outside-in* but inside-out, as if some body had pulled the leather grain from inward — then in ghat-common arithmetic: **paani bag mein aata hai toh sokia haath bhar jaata hai — yeh nahi hua; gaya toh kuch aur hi hai — paani gaya bina aaye** *(when water enters a bag, a dry hand fills; this didn't happen; something else has gone — water went without coming)*. At eleven-oh-three, the bag woman's light-lamp was delivered to the cremation-watch house by a chanting procession of six — the moaners of the deceased who have just now died, who sing the newly dead coming-to-visit — and they sang *with their backs to the water*, which the register enters as the time-stamp: **eleven-oh-three, hymns backward-facing**.

---

It was eleven-oh-ten before the register's own district officer — the correspondent who had arrived in ch-27, too young to have been posted, too learned in grammar not to have seen this coming — got word by wire from the watch-house. This was his first siege. He arrived as they ordered, in dress, unannounced on his own procession of one bicycle-pusher and one cold-drink awning, and he took the copper staircase (the one whose iron-framing was underpinned by watering-cans) *up* the ghat — no procession, no herald — and stood at the head of the immersion-tier with his notebook open and *his own district's counting system* — the one he had been taught by register-Kalsi's archivist on his first posting — and he counted.

He counted the pyres. There were seven. He counted the pyres' fires: burning downward. He counted the flames' smoke: smoky downward. He counted the visible flames: seven blue-tips — not orange — seven, concentrated inverse-tongues with no upward root. He had, by his own district's schooling, one permitted remark for this, and he made it into his notebook's margin in handwriting so steady the watch-house keeper later said it must have been printed:

**"Dhuen ke neeche paani hai, aur paani ke neeche ek daraar. Daraar ke baare mein: NAHI LIKHA HAI."**

*(Below the smoke is water, and below the water a crack. About the crack: NOT WRITTEN.)*

Because under the water — even from the top of the ghat, even from eleven yards' remove, even where eleven and a half feet of liquid completion overlay the bare stone — he could see it. He could see *through* the water, *to* the stone, which was lined with — the register employs its gentlest idiom, being after all a register — *unlined* lines: some set of deeply set scratches so recent that they had not had time to be foul, and so old in drawing that they had not had time to be scored — for under the water, beneath the seven flame's downward tongues, there yawned a crack in Manikarnika's floor.

He pressed his notebook against his chest and looked to the east — to where, according to what little the district had dared let him keep, the river had its own oldest road. His grammar — the one field-post grammar he had with him (which bequeathed him by the far-off teacher's first principle: jitna paas, utna akhand) — permitted one question: **"Ye daraar kab se hai?"** *(Since when is this crack?)*

And from the middle of the wailing procession — from the singer who had been singing backwards at the water, whose feet had not left the ghat's bottom step and who was *still* singing, but whose voice had changed key — the answer, in the Dakhini-parsi of the district's mourning songs:

**"Kab se nahi, hazoor. Aaj se. Aaj hi khuli hai."**

*(Not since, sir. Since today. It opened only today.)*

---

The district officer descended six steps further — *not* because he was brave, but because his district grammar acknowledged no reply of "*since today*" that did not propose an action — and he stopped at the second-to-last dry stone above the water, which is where the rainwater normally ends (the stone is called, in the cremation-watch's accounts, *the return stair*) — and from there, with the rectangle of the embers' glow reflecting refractions up the side of his face like the half-map of a broken country, he measured.

He measured at the stone a column that was *not* of the water — he measured, out of the water and onto the stone, one scale-hold of a hand span — four breaths in — *and the water stayed behind him like gates opening behind him*. He did not, at the time, understand how this was possible (nobody in the kasba at the time understood how; understanding was left to the second and third registers, which are the ones kept by the two while the third was either in the air or precisely nowhere), because the arithmetic of it was not something one could give to a dry hand: the water did not part, and it did not rise, and it did not yield to his boot as according to any of the churn-man's rules. It simply *stayed in its column beside him while he was in its column*, as if the water had itself taken note of the field officer's presence and, in the obliged vocabulary of the district's worst and oldest arithmetic, laid out for his examination what it meant for a body to walk *through* water without being *in* it.

The officer, the register reports, did not walk further. He knelt instead — on the *return* stair itself — and took out of his pocket a small instrument he had been given on his first posting by Ram Rao (the district's eldest measure-keeper, whose name, in every register the district's field-writers kept, was already *Vashisht* — and whose son, at this moment, was standing three ghats eastward under the sky's first assembled treasury, learning Night 4). The instrument was a sextant of the inland sort — the kind used by water-men who have no meridian to desire but who must, nonetheless, set the centre's difference into the where of their accounting — and the officer pressed its little brass horizon against the water's broken column, and the instrument, which had been calibrated never to lie, gave him an answer that his grammar had no room for:

**"-4."**

The instrument's mercury was showing a negative four in the root column. The instrument had been designed to show what a water-line does as one draws near it. It was showing that, from here — the staircase — the water was *not water at all*; it was something that existed at minus four degrees of itself, something the instrument could not perceive at all, so — according to the instrument's own doctrine, in capital letters the register won't transcribe because the catch-chamber was labeled not in English but in the grammar of Nagpur — **THERE IS NOTHING HERE**.

But there was something. The officer could smell it. His nostrils told him: iron and crisp broken stone and an effusion of cold, the kind of cold that settles behind the ears in winter dawns, the cold that never leaves once it has come. And — the register has to be honest here — he heard it too. Not a sound *from it*; a sound *next to it* — the sound the platelets of the water made as they fell beside each other, and in their falling, accounted for each other, in one-two-three arithmetic, at the rate of *four*. Four in, four out, four pause; four in again. The officer's grammar — which was the keeper's grammar — designated this cadence: *artharii* — *the count after the count*, the arithmetic of the second count. And under the cadence, and in exact regulament four-pulse — from the invisible something beneath — the officer heard the counting.

He himself knew this cadence. It was the cadence of the navy-colored book. The cadence of the brass-hammered threshold at the east gate. The cadence of the panch-kulu's tally-men on the day of the first settlement. Four in, four out, four pause — the same as the count the keeper of the *whet-baton* had been asked to keep on the day of Rudra's Reading-At-Ten; the same as the count the *pit-boys* used in their wrestling-ka-count (one-effort-I, one-effort-you, count-of-attention: *four*); the same as the count the *bhishti-women* must keep, at the ghat's edge, when they carry water and don't ask whose water it is; the same cadence, at the same pace, from the same depth, which *every counting thing in the district had been keeping since before the register even had a name for the ledger*.

The officer had never heard it before. He stood up. He had within him an inventory of reasons one uses on behalf of grammar to prevent the next mistake from happening; he listed them in his mind as he climbed back up six more steps, so that by the time he reached the watch-house mud-there he had counted twenty-six reasons why he must not panic: because if he panics, the district panics. Because if the district panics, the watch-house panics. Because if the watch-house panics, the panch-kulu panic. Because if panch-kulu panics, no one will ever chant the funeral-song backwards again, and if nobody chants the funeral-song backwards, the body that has just been brought will not find its *return stair*, and if the body does not find its return stair, the count after the count stopping only at *four* will never find its *pause*.

So he did not panic. He entered the watch-house, lifted his own ledger — the ledger he had been keeping since ch-27, by district's order, whose pages he had recently had re-bound at the Nagpur bookbinder's with green thread because the old thread had come apart with the count's weight — and opened it to the index, which he read by candle-light *three times* before finding the entry he was looking for. When he found it, he pressed the corner of the page with both thumbs, as if the page could be located more strongly by pressure; he read it once; and then he said, in a voice that the watch-house keeper heard and the processional mourners outside heard and, in their own way and at their own time, every lamp-keeper in the district heard — no grammar in the register doubts it:

**"Yeh woh nahee hai jo hum soch rahe the. Yeh sab se neeche wala darwaaza hai — aur yeh khul raha hai."**

*(This is not what we were thinking. This is the bottom-most gate — and it is opening.)*

And in the watch-house where he stood — surrounded by the wail-lamps and the brass vessels and the single dulled sickle that was kept at the head of the head-stair for the dead to cut their way toward rebirth — the ground beneath him, which had been counting in *fours* all along, stopped counting at all.

The three lamps of the haveli — by whose arithmetic our register has been counting since ch-44 — registered the change *at the weather of their own kind*: the west window's lamp suddenly *grew calm*. It stopped wicking; it stopped whispering its tally through the taper's tip (as we havenoted, the lamp had been doing this ever since Sitara entered the house); it settled flat, then *dimmer*, then — with the district's arithmetic of lamps, the dimming of which gives -1 — it burned as though it had never heard of fire at all.

It would be lying, the register observes, to claim that Sitara *noticed* this change — because one always notices things *in their own arithmetic*, and Sitara's arithmetic, these nineteen years, had been the arithmetic of *waiting* — but the register, which is obliged to the full green-roads arithmetic of all the sub-districts it keeps, is obliged to record that she *finished* noticing it at exactly eleven-twenty-seven, which was when the lamp-wick's tally (which had been of the arithmetic of one-one-one-one, the primary decimal) moved to *four* — and the cottage's paper-cuttings on the threshing-floor, whose names register keeps only in names like "the string-bed's fronds," and "the mandir-side mango-wood," laid themselves flat.

Sitara knew what this meant because she had been keeping lamp-arithmetic (e.g., the register's oldest teacher had been told by her illiterate daughter, twenty years before, that her mother did not so much *keep lamps* as *teach them counting*) — the arithmetic of lamps was Sitara's arithmetic — so the register must record her movement faithfully: she stepped out onto the haveli's second verandah, took the west-window lamp between her two hands — holding it from beneath, not from the side, which is how one holds a lamp that is *about to stop being a lamp* — and said, not to anyone, in the grammar she had first spoken at the house's east-wall in ch-44:

"Teenon hi gin gaye. Ab jo raha — woh sirf awaaz hai."

*(All three have been counted. What remains — is only voice.)*

The lamp went out. Not *by going out*, which is how lamps ordinarily give out, but *by having counted*: it had counted four, then for one instant — exactly as long as counted itself takes — it lit again *in the middle*, between its own wick and the tip of the tipping-smoke, and in that instant it placed on the underside of the wick-slice — where no eye had ever placed anything, because nobody had ever thought to look under the wick — **a mark.** Then the mark vanished, in the same way a meaning vanishes when you hold your breath — and the lamp went out.

Sitara replaced the lamp in the west window-brass, gently — as one does with a thing that has done the complete duty it was set to do — and walked through the house to the east window, where the first lamp, which had gone out years ago (on the arrival day of the lamp-bringing sister), was now — no arithmetic in the register will contradict this — burning again.

She did not touch it. She looked at it for exactly one breath. Then she said, and the register sets it down exactly as she said it, because it was the district's first line of definite poetry we'd had since ch-40's "four-voice chord":

**"Jise humne khaali rakha — usme koi aa gaya hai. Ab hamen bhi aana hoga."**

*(Someone has come into what we kept empty. Now we too will have to come.)*

Nobody was in the room at that moment. The register enters it anyway, because the register is obliged, by the district's grammar of lamps, to enter whatever the lamp itself says — lamps say very little, it being in their nature — but when they say it, the district's arithmetic permits them to be heard, and registered, and counted. The register counts it.

---

By eleven-fifty-one — eleven minutes after the district officer had spoken his conclusion from inside the watch-house, "*this is the bottom-most gate and it is opening*" — the district's arithmetic had already gone to work on its own behalf. The district did not panic; that would have been arithmetic-breaker; the district *accounted*. At the district's middle-ground, which by then was the district's easternmost empty cart (where the melon-woman had left it at eleven-o'clock, because the district's ordinary arithmetic has its own registers for empty carts and middle-grounds, and this was both), and where the district's oldest woman-keeper of lamps (see ch-41) happened to be standing with her own dimming light (she kept the district's daylight lamps), the district's arithmetic gathered itself and — the register must report with all due grammar — *opened its books*.

The district's counting books were about as unlooked-for as the potholes, and were drawn from the same low-grade mortar they were meant to plug; but they kept the district's arithmetic with a kind of furious regularity that only the register is equipped to translate. What was opened, in brief:

**The Three Lamps Count Book**, under an entry written so long ago that the ordinary ink was barely legible, which stated: *teen diye tinte hain; char gaya toh paanch gaya; paanch gaya toh seven* — *(three lamps; three; when four are gone, five; when five are gone, seven)* — which the register renders slightly more clearly for its own entries: *the district's count ALWAYS knows what's next; the count NEVER ends*.

**The Stones' Thumb Book**, which was *not* an entry-book, but which was in current use by the district's treasury offices as a *locking-string*, to keep iron-cased entries sealed when a family might be dissolved (Arthashastra-grade green-wax thumb-lock) — it sat open on the karha's east-embrace (where its other business is conducted: weddings of stones, walls), and its folio had been opened, whom none could say — except the one who read it openly, aloud, to the karha's fields from under the banyan's shade, and her reading was in the register's cleanest Magahi: *Do patthar ke beech mein ek khaali jagah rahi. Wo jagah jagah ka maap hai. Jagah ka maap kabhi khatam nahi hota. Jagah ka maap sirf खाता है.* *(Between two stones there was one empty place. That place is the measure of the place. The measure of the place never ends. The measure of the place only KEEPS BOOKS.)*

**The Night-Teacher's Table-Board**, under the heading *aathva chhor kamzor hai* (the eighth point is weak), which he had written at his own table in Whiteleaf Naan; and beside it, in a different, smaller hand — rumored in the district to be the teacher's own, although the district had never yet seen any other learned person write it — a second line, which the register must write sans translation, because the translation is the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the register's: **"kamzor tabhi maun hai — jab woh gumpa ho, tab wehf foe hai."** *(It is quiet only when weak — when it is hollow, then it is enemy.)* *(The last two words, the register insists, are not Hindi; they are the grammar in which the teacher's own night-pupil's grammar may be found, grammar of the count after the count.)*

**The District Map-book**, whose first page showed — in the last cartography project of the district's air-assessor of the dekko-take (whose name, the register records, was *Vashisht*'s *grandfather's last syllable* — whether the debt was inherited or agreed is lost to the register) — the district's arithmetic-keepers had been asked (not today, not this year, but always and in all years) to keep the district's seven civil hamlets marked on a single page, using the arithmetic *x = kh + flex*, meaning, in the district's grammar: *the mark is the number of lampposts between the house's lamp and the neighboring house's lamp*. Tonight, the arithmetic was showing that the number between the fourth and fifth lamppost, at the ghat's return stair, was **minus four**. The register observes: the district's arithmetic-keepers were not surprised. Their arithmetic has never been surprised, since the arithmetic tells them *how*, and *how* is not surprise; *how* is *why*.

**And a private book** — the register will not identify which — which was kept not in the district's common lock-places, but in the sabha-house's own decree-collector's right-hand-pocket memo-case, which only *one* person could read (though the register cannot say so with authority: the register only knows the writer of the entry on the final page, not the reader) — opened without a sound, closed without a sound, and the district's arithmetic made *the only entry permitted by the count*: **a figure.** One number, in the arithmetic of the district. **Seven.**

---

It was the seventh entry the district had kept since the district's arithmetic of the seven had begun (the register will date the arithmetic's origin conservatively at ch-24, the day of the fourth-name, the day when the district's arithmetic was re-sorted to account for the illumination of the four white-fires in the space between the four* *hunters and the four* *wind-drivers of the city's *x = kh + flex* enumeration, which we may put more plainly as the day Rudra returned from the forest). Seven entries had been kept in the district's open books; seven entries had been kept in the District Officer's own ledger; seven entries had been kept in the register itself, between the notes and entries of chapters 41 through 45; seven entries had been kept, in the teacher's own grammar, at the night-pupil's table in the turanan-akhada, where the word "enemy" had been spoken exactly once, and the count of four had been broken exactly once (at the count after the count, it said *paanch*; at the count where the *hollow* is, it had said *chha*, as in the count of the *five* which immediately precedes the *seven*); seven entries had been kept in the district's east-window lamp, at the rate of one lamp per annum; seven entries had been kept in the west-window lamp, at the rate of one *count after the count*; and seven entries had been kept, in the district's own tectonic grammar (the grammar of the ground), between the lines of the ground.

Tonight, at the bottom of Manikarnika — beneath the water, beneath the smoke, beneath the seven pyre-flames burning downward — the district's eighth entry was being written. It was not being written by the ground, though the ground had counted it. It was being written by the *fifth* stone — the stone that hadn't yet arrived — which, the register says, was *now arriving*: *ushered in by the count-of-four, kept by the count-after-the-count, legged by the count-of-seven, and arithmetically summoned at last by the arithmetic of lamps* (three lamps; three; when four are gone, five; when five are gone, seven).

The bottom-most gate was no longer being *kept*. It was being *opened*. And in the register's own arithmetic, *the count must be let to know*. So the register recorded, at eleven-fifty-two, in the smallest permissible hand that its grammar allows — let this stand forever — the register's voice lost its own enclosure, for the register is not in the business of loss of voice:

**"Chinta mat karo — jis ghar ko hum bhool gaye the — woh ghar humein yaad dila raha hai."** *(Don't worry — the house we forgot — is reminding us.)*

And the register closes this entry, because after eleven-fifty-two, the district began *keeping the count* differently. The district's arithmetic had always known *what* it was keeping: the lamps, the stones, the pyres, the smoke, the water, the throats, the gait, the pending storms — but now it also knew *who* was doing the keeping. And it knew that whoever was doing the keeping had already been counted by somebody else — by the voice from beneath the water, by the mark under the wick, by the seven pyre-flames pointing downward, by the count after the count — all of which, the register insists, are *very easy arithmetic* once you've learned the count's cadence: *four in, four out, four pause; then one count you never see; then seven.*

# CHAPTER FORTY-SEVEN — THE WATCHING AND THE COUNTED

---

> *"Counting requires the thing counted to remain still. When the counted begin to count the counter back — that is not arithmetic. That is marriage. And a married count, once gone wrong, does not return to single."*
>
> *— FROM THE WATCHER'S TABLE-BOARD, TURANAN, folio of the second hand*

---

Light in the district's understanding is measured from Manikarnika back *home*. *Home* may be, in this register, the karha field, the pit's bottom tier, the teacher's star-position at Machli-ghat, the haveli's west window, or any place one has decided to keep still long enough to be counted. The district, being a loose confidentiality of registers rather than a home itself, did not reach its home conclusion until the district had counted seven pyres burning downward at eleven-fifty-two, of which *it was now the eleventh day* — which is the register's careful way of admitting that the arithmetic of what the district had called in ch-46 *the account coming home* had *held*.

Which meant, in the district's grammar, the following: that no one — not the pit-boys, not the night-teacher, not the tulsi-ring, not the three lamps, not the district itself, which had been going around asking "kab tak" ("until when?") all week — could prevent the account from *accounting*. The district had been keeping the count; the count was keeping *somebody*; and now, at eleven-oh-five on the eleventh morning of the eleventh day, that *somebody* presented itself *for counting* — which is the district's formal way of saying that at the Manikarnika twilight-edge, seven more pyres appeared, burning downward.

The register draws its own conclusion at the speed of the district's: **seven pyres, seven days, eleven counts.** Seven *plus* eleven makes *seven-eleven* — that is the arithmetic of the entire ledger. The register will proceed.

---

Veer, who at the moment of ch-46 had been standing under the sky's fourth-night lesson, has by this dawn *finished* the night-teacher's seven-night return-course, though the register would be criminally remiss if it failed to remark that *finished*, here, means something like the sixth place on a ladder in ch-31: it means only that *one has reached a place the ladder has no word for*. Dhruv's final night's lesson — the seventh, under the sky at hour-mark 49 (and already the pit-boys of the akhada had called it Veer's "sattarvi tareekh," the seventy-one-date, because the Turanan counting-style acknowledges no months) — Dhruv's final lesson was a single sentence, laid not on the sky but on the stone beside the seventh ink-spot (the one the register calls, by district's Englishing, *the standing-stone* — it was not a stone in any register's notion of stone, but the *ground's footprint* of where Turanan's mills stood before mills existed — a minor distinction, though the register, in its respects, keeps it), and the sentence was this:

**"Jis cheez ko ginna shuru kar diya, usko ginna band kar dena — yahi hai ginti ka antim form. Ab tum ginti ho."**

*(To cease counting the thing one has begun to count — that alone is counting's final form. Now you are the count.)*

The stone beside him contained his footprint, which the teacher had entered into for him, which meant (in Turanan arithmetic) that Veer now *had no more arrivals to perform*. He was, in the akhada's books, *here*. He would be, in the akhada's books, *still here* for the next nineteen years, which was the standard period of advanced counting-instruction (Bhanu-uzhaar had completed stage two, called the *listening-grind*, some twelve years back, and had recently been elevated to *stone-keeper*, meaning his *turn* had ended). Veer's *turn* had therefore just begun, which was a thing — the register insists — of which he had no consciousness at all until he was standing in the ash-field at eleven-oh-five on the eleventh morning of the eleventh day, watching the second seven pyres appear in the twilight and trying to remember when he'd started counting.

Because that's the thing about a count, once it's gone final: it never says when it starts. It only says *it is now here*. Veer had been *there* for the last seventy-one dates — in the akhada's books he was *documented* — and now he was *here*, which meant he had taken a step he could *not now re-take*, a step which the ground itself had counted. The teacher had just said, in the grammar-immunity of his register: *Now you are the count.* Veer's first act as *the count* was to step back from the ghat's edge — the return-stair was by then a habit of the feet, so he kept to the second-stair dryness — and to open the ledger he'd been given by his first teacher on the day of his leaving (*not the arithmetic journal* — not the Load Khata — but the *turn-journal*, which Bhanu-uzhaar had carried with him from Turanan, in which, in front of many pages of oat calculations and the stone-wall's shift-ledger, there appeared at the front a page he'd marked *V* and headed: **tum jo ginna shuru karte ho, ginti tumse shuru karti hai** — *whatever you begin to count, counting begins from you*) and Veer made the entry *the turn-journal had been expecting*:

**"Pandrah. Ghat. Gin-gaya."**

*(Fifteen. Ghat. Counted.)*

By *fifteen* Veer meant, in the turn-journal's curriculum, that *fifteen mortals* (the register uses the district's moral label, panch-dhara-definition-included) had *already* been counted at the ghat that morning — the field officer (six steps down), the wailing procession (six singers), the cremation-watch (two keepers), and the bangle-wife (one light-keeper) — and Veer — who was now the fifteenth — made the entry only *after* the count had begun to include *him*. He had been, until this moment, *counting* (which is what students do; the register remands to ch-45 for that class). Now — as of the entry — he was *being counted*. This is the difference the district denotes, in its cleanest ledger-marks, as *sankhya-bhed* — which is not Hinduism, nor arithmetic, nor bookkeeping, but rather a small operation in the district of *which side* of the gate one's standing.

Veer, standing *on* the gate-side of the fourteenth, wrote the fifteenth. At which point the gate — the bottom-most gate the field officer had finally named in ch-46, who had written it down in the watch-house ledger in the district's official green thread — *lost its eighteenth entry*, by which the register means *one of its pyres disappeared*.

---

To the field-watcher at the keep-house, this was not a *diminishing* of fires — it was, however, the register has to say plainly with some authority, *unprecedented*: which is to say it had never before happened in any register's grammar. Seven pyres had been burning at eleven-oh-four; at eleven-oh-five; at eleven-oh-six; at eleven-oh-seven the third pyre from the left — the register marks the third pyre *specifically* because its flame was *laterally* distributed, which in the district's arithmetic is the giveaway that the count is going *sideways* (the register adduces ch-40's *four-voice chord*, whose geometry of return defined out-and-back direction; ch-44's *east-west-east turn*; and ch-46's *four-in/four-out* cadence — the third pyre was using *all three* simultaneously) — the third pyre from the left *went out*.

The register establishes the point not in terms of the pyre's extinction but in terms of what the register *calls* a pyre's extinction. *Going out* — *jaana* (the count after the count's grammar for it) — is not what *ordinary* pyres do when they have burned through their named wood. *Going out*, in the district's grammar, is merely the error-letter which *arithmetic* assigns to the thing whose grammar it is investigating; i.e., the pyre's *going out* does not imply the pyre *stops* burning; it implies that *the register* (or in this case the turn-journal of the fifteenth entry) tries to record it, *fails*, and so writes down “going out” *in place of* “*has become uncountable*.” At this the district's arithmetic may be expected to step in — the district's arithmetic being, after all, *never surprised* — and to tell the register what *going out* has happened to the third pyre: **it has been taken out. Not by the wind, nor by the water — but by the smoke's own count, which has now counted the pyre out of the district's arithmetic.**

In the ghat's watch-house ledger the field officer's green-thread hand made the follow-up entry at eleven-oh-seven: **"Teesra diya gaya. Nahi gaya — liya gaya. Dusra diya: liye jaane ki taiyari mein. Pehla diya: abhi bhi hai, par ginna band."** *(Third lamp given. Not went — was taken. Second lamp: preparing to be taken. First lamp: still there, but stopped counting.)* To the district, which was listening in *its own arithmetic* (as the registers have always permitted it to listen, at whatever distance is eldest in the district's arithmetic), this was understandable only in the terms in which it was given: *the pyre has been taken; the pyre is preparing to be taken; the pyre has stopped counting.* The district, which was by then in *teen baithak* arithmetic — under the banyan on the karha's eastern edge — performed the only act the district's arithmetic can perform at such moments: **the district stopped counting, together.**

It is not easy to describe what this looks like unless one has seen it; the register will attempt the best available description, but it is limited by the register's own grammar to the register's own words. The district stopped counting — that is, the count *ceased to be numeric*. The count became *something else*, which the registers have never found a word for (Aadhya's father had one; the register does not know what it was). The count became, in the absence of any digits, *the number itself* — the district's arithmetic became *the district's payload* — and the children of the karha, who had been standing under the banyan, proceeded to count *to themselves*, in the count-after-the-count cadence (*four in, four out, four rest*), and the count's cadence had the moments of arrival which the district's children can only hear at the edges of sleep — the count after the count — the cadence the pit-boys had once used in their bout-at-banyan, the cadence the register once heard the watch-house-book singing through the smoke downward. The district's *children* were counting the district's *silence*, and the district's silence was counting *them* — which is the exchange arithmetic has with grammar, toward the end of its game.

The register records, being strict about its duty, that at eleven-oh-nine the third pyre *was* taken, by eleven-thirteen the second pyre *was* taken, and the first pyre, at eleven-nineteen, stopped counting. Seven pyres had appeared; at eleven-nineteen, five remained; and of the five remaining, *none* in the district's arithmetic knew *which way its smoke was pointing* — because, at eleven-twenty, the district's arithmetic *reversed itself* — which is to say that the district's children at the banyan, by the district's arithmetic's own consent, looked up, took a breath (the fourth in the count), and — in a single small cadence — *started counting upward*.

This is important. The register has emphasized, repeatedly, that the district's arithmetic has never been surprised. It is also true that the district's arithmetic has never before been *reversed*. The reversal is not arithmetic's own; the reversal is what arithmetic permits its participants to perform *when the object of counting remains stationary while the counter moves* — which in the district's grammar is called *count-standing* — and it is the one form of arithmetic *not* permitted between eleven-oh-four and eleven-fifty-two at the return-stair — the one form whose boundary is not in the district's green-thread ledger, because the district's arithmetic has never yet *allowed* it to be recorded. Now, at eleven-twenty, on the day after the eleventh, in front of the fifth pyre — which had not moved — the district's children stood up at the banyan, *looked up*, and divided their *numbers* by the number of pyres that had been taken, which was *two*, which left *five*, which is the number between four and seven which the register has already seen once before (in ch-44's tally, where the tulsi-ring's pots' faces had turned westward *one hundred clay pots were now leaf-cups bearing grass*, the arithmetic of the *four after the four*) — and counted *five*, in the count-after-the-count cadence, *in the direction opposite the taken pyres*, whose numbers now matched the *bottom* of the district's map-book arithmetic between the fourth and fifth lampposts (*minus four*) — and counted the pit's own bottom tier, and the karha's eastern bend, and the pathori-lane, and the treadmill, and the middle ground, and the household's three lamps, and the district's wailing six singers at the notice of the ghat's freaking-smog, and the district's register and the register's own counting children, and *twenty-six things* in total — and in the twenty-sixth the district's arithmetic's final form declared itself, which is why we are telling you this, which is what the register *means* by the arithmetic of the count-after-the-count, in its slogan form:

**"Jo samne gin raha tha, usne ginti ki."** *(The one counting from the front — was itself counted.)*

And at that instant something extraordinary happened, which the register will describe *without* interpretation (the register is no longer in the business of interpretation — the register is *being counted*): the *fifth pyre*, the only one not yet taken, not yet begun-to-be-taken, not yet stopped-counting — the pyre whose smoke was even now rising (because it was the one pyre in the district's arithmetic which had *not* yet joined the district's arithmetic, which is to say *it was still burning the way a fire burns, with clean upward tongues, before the arithmetic had a chance to get there*) — the *fifth pyre* paused, *in the smoke*, mid-tongue, *and looked down at the district's arithmetic counting from below it*.

The register now employs its very last grammatical tool — the register's *own registered* arithmetic — which is a small instrument called *observation* whose use is strictly limited to the register itself, and which does not exist except when the register has been observed *observing* — the register *observed*: **The pyre did not *see* them. The pyre *registered* them. Seeing requires eyes; registering needs only heat and the thing's own stillness — and the pyre *kept* the count, in the register's own register — and the count, as it settled next to the rest,* said to the district's children, in the register Magahi, in the oldest script still current in the district's register— *khuda ke diye hue panch log, aapke paas aaye hain*— *the five god-given have come to you.*

The district's children — the register identifies them: the urchin of Asadhat Chowk who had seen the tulsi-ring turn *westward* in ch-44; the two pit-boys who had been named *Chattu* and *Chandan* by the district's arithmetic (the register *cannot confirm* which is which, nor does it want to; the distinction being less important than the *chattu-chandan grammatical machine* that binds them); the melon-woman's own daughters (whose names, in the district's folder, lie in the east valley of *girls-who-do-not-come-under-verdict*), a spread of the register's own sub-circle — stood up, each of them, in the count-after-the-count cadence, and *waited*.

They waited because the district's arithmetic had said *nineteen* to them — *nineteen* was the arithmetic of *done*, the arithmetic of *enough time has passed*, the arithmetic which would announce the next thing *when it was ready to be announced* — and the district's children, being children, had been *trained in the count after the count*, knew that *nineteen* meant nothing *if the count of nineteen was not itself available*. The count of nineteen was not available. So they waited — the count after the count, in the register's *frame*, waiting is also *counting* — but these children were not counting. They were *listening*.

And they listened. And in that cadence, the cadence whose arithmetic ruled every legitimate register of the district's grammar — the cadence that ran under the stone and over the water and into the pit and out of the pit and under the whet-baton's sound and round the *return-stair* and under the sabha-house and along the east-window sill and over the west-window sill and in and out of the *measuring instrument* — the cadence of four in, four out, four rest — the children heard, for the first time, the *fourth* part of the four-cadence — which, up until this instant, the district's arithmetic had been *preventing itself* from hearing by the district's arithmetic's own energetic arrangement — which arrangement was itself *counted* by the district's arithmetic — the children heard the pause after the fourth, and in that pause the district's arithmetic *revealed its own* to the children in the order *nineteen-behind-the-seven*, which the register writes with some hesitation, because it runs, in the register's own grammar, opposite to any logic we have had to register: **the fourth's pause was itself a number — the register must call it *the number after the count*, and the district's arithmetic had been keeping it reserved from its own arithmetic for exactly nineteen years, which the district's arithmetic had said, in the register's bookkeeping, "ghar ka chautha patta"** (the house's fourth page) — *and now the children heard it*.

*The fourth's pause is: the number after the count is a person.*

This is what the district's children now knew — because the register's own arithmetic now knew it with them — that what had been *counted* at Manikarnika, on the return-stair, was not *objects* — the pyres, the flames, the smoke, the waters, the officers, the mourners — but a *person*. Not a *named* person (the register had no authority to name; the district's arithmetic had no way to name; the count's own grammar has no name in it, it merely performs its task *counting out* names when *it* gets to names, and *it* — the register patiently but firmly asserts — *has not yet gotten to the name*). But a person nonetheless — a person who had been counted *by* the district's children *in their own numbers* — and the district's children, who did *not* name it, now heard from within the register's *own* book — from the folio whose number the register had identified in ch-40 (the folio being the *turn-journal*, *otter-count* in the register's numbering, marked *V* by the turn-journal-Bhanu-Uzhaar's eldest pupil, in whom the register had identified his own register — this grammar, being the register's own, loops back on itself in these terms exactly) — *in the cadence's own cadence* — the sentence the watcher-table's folio had said to the *watcher's own*:

It had been her shift at the pit's mouth since eleven-oh-eight — she and Do (the assistant whose name the register enters only once, in the register's eighth factorial of its ten arithmetic-forms) changing places with the watchers in the pit exactly as the pit itself had been scheduled into the district: *irregularly*, in the count after the count. Now the palm first *felt* the arithmetic's own arithmetic — which the palm had felt many times before, but which Aadhya had not yet recorded. She recorded now, on the pit's mouthstone, in charcoal, the rule the palm dictated:

**"Char aur paanch mila diya. Ab ginti jaldi hogi — char ginti se pehle ki ginti."**

*(Four and five were joined. Now counting must hasten — the count before the count of four.)*

The district's palm-grammar has, the register notes, three forms: first *jaali* (the net-form — palm held shut, so what enters may never leave); second *sukha* (the dry-form — palm employed, the count taken); third, largest of the three, *aadhi poorn* (half-complete — palm meeting the half-count, which meets the palm in turn from below). Aadhya's palm was now in the third form. Do arrived at her shoulder, read the mouthstone, and gave the district's oldest standing instruction: "Palm neeche. Pit palm mein rakho." *(Palm down. Keep the pit in the palm.)* Aadhya pressed the charcoal-grammar into the stone so that the pit would not close in her absence. It stayed open. It is staying open; the smudged black half-complete sign is there still, *aadhi poorn*, on the mouthstone, where anybody counting may read it before the rain arrives.

Farther off — east, where the roads run under the district's long-neglected irrigation-embankment — Vedant Rao was reading water with the instrument he called *Paani*, at the moment the pit's arithmetic went public. It is the register's duty to report that instrument's behaviour faithfully: water, itself, and always, *settles*; settling is its arithmetic, as rising is smoke's. But as the count-of-four arrived at the embankment's lowest gauge — one hour and zero minutes after Aadhya's palm went half-complete — the water in the brass bowl *stopped settling*: it stood mid-fall, three drops suspended above the rest like a sentence refusing its own full stop, and Vedant, whose grammar had never once permitted him an exclamation, said, levelly, to the cyclometer empty of wind:

**"Paani ruk gaya. Jisne roka — uska naam abhi likha nahi hai. Par uska kaam likha ja raha hai."**

*(The water has stopped. The one who stopped it — his name isn't yet written. But his work is being written.)*

He recorded it in the margin-book, which for twenty years had received only the two words *ABHI TAK THEEK* — and this night's entry, being different, he reasoned, required the full grammar, so he wrote all sixteen letters of it, slowly, with the hand that had re-set a broken wrist in some unrecorded year, and he signed it, as he had signed none of the others: **V. R.** When he lifted the pen, the water in the bowl completed its fall.

The district, meanwhile — at its four quarter-gates, east and west and south and the fourth which the kasba insists on counting anyway although there is no road through it — *closed nothing*. No gate was shut that night, nor the next, nor any night of the siege to come. The kasba had learned, in the arithmetic of lamps and stones and speakers, that what is *kept empty* and *kept open* is precisely what keeps; and the district's arithmetic had made, by Paanchaki's dusk, its single most-understood entry in thirty-one years of registers, on the doorsteps, in the charcoal of Aadhya's grammar — which was now repeated at every gate, in handwriting nobody owned:

**"Aap usi hisaab mein ho, jis hisaab mein sab hain. Ab sirf hisaab hai."**

*(You are in the same count as everyone else. Now there is only the count.)*

Ira read it from the fourth gate — the gate with no road — where she had been standing, notebook shut, since the pit stones cried their single million-noise. She did not open the notebook this time. Some entries the page refuses, and hers — the one with the name that had been warming in the pit-arithmetic since she had watched Rudra press the thirteenth stair — was one page was too few for. She stood and listened to the habit at the bottom of the district counting, as was the habit's custom, in fours; and she understood, in the way her family of watchers had always understood things, with the whole spine and not with the mouth, that the four-cadence was now asking its question in a larger room than the pit: the room was the district, and soon — though the register may not yet say when — the room would be the whole sleeping province.

So Ira did what her kind of watcher does at the end of a count: she closed the distance between herself and the haveli, the haveli and the karha, the karha and the ghat, the ghat and the crack, the crack and the count, and the count and the one now descending through it, and she wrote, at last, not in her notebook but on the inside of her wrist, where her family kept the entries that must survive bathing:

**"Woh neeche se ginti kar raha hai. Humein upar se aana padega."**

*(He is counting from below. We shall have to come from above.)*

And then — because the register finds, after all its grammar, that its last receipt is the reader's suspense, which it can no longer audit without cost — the register surrenders the remaining pages of this book to whoever now turns them.

# CHAPTER FORTY-NINE — THE COUNT GIVEN AT MORNING

---

> *"Jisne neeche ginti ki hai, use upar wale din se mat naapo — woh apna din saath le aata hai. Aur jis din woh lautaata hai, us din ko hum log 'savera' kehte hain — chahe ghadi mein raat ho."*
>
> *— FROM THE PIT-STONES' OWN GRAMMAR, entry 13, kept under the mouthstone*

---

*(Whoever has counted below, do not measure him by the day above — he brings his own day with him. And the day on which he returns is what we call "morning" — even if the clock says night.)*

---

There was no sky. This must be established first, because everything else in this chapter depends upon it, and the register would be failing its oldest duty if it let any later sentence be read the way ordinary sentences get read — with the sky assumed. Rudra Vashisht descended through the thirteenth stair, or the thirteenth stair descended through him — in the count after the count both of these are the same entry — and when the habit closed its arithmetic around him the way a district closes a file, the first thing he learned was that *there was no sky*, and the second thing he learned was that he had never once, in nineteen years of walking the district, counted *that*.

He had counted breath. He had counted load. He had counted the distances between lamps, the breaths between steps, the steps between the pit and the grange and the banyan and the west gate and the east window of the haveli where, at this very hour above him — hours did not exist here, but the register supplies them for the reader's sake — his mother stood saying of him, *do diye jhund chuke hain; ab mere bete ka aanch aa rahi hai*. He had counted everything the district counted and some things it did not. But he had never counted the sky, because the sky had never been *absent*, and the register's arithmetic — every register's arithmetic — can only begin at the point where a thing stops being there. Below, the sky had stopped being there. So he counted it. **Aasmaan — ek. Jaata hua — do. Khaali — teen. Waapis aane wala — chaar.** *(Sky — one. Gone — two. Empty — three. Coming back — four.)* And at four, out of nineteen years of obedience, he stopped, because four was not his arithmetic. Four was the habit's.

That was the morning's first casualty, though the district above would never receive it as one: **Rudra lost his arithmetic at the thirteenth stair.** The register records it the way casualty is properly recorded — not as blood, but as obedience withdrawn from a count. He had kept the nine-breath rule since the year of the storm: nine in, nine out, never ten, nobody taught him why. Below, the habit counted in fours — four in, four out, four pause — and the habit's cadence entered his nineteen-year-nine the way irrigation enters a furrow, and his nine-breath, without his consent, without even his anger, began to *settle into fours*. He fought it for one hour. Then he stopped fighting *because fighting it was also counting in fours* — four stubbornnesses, four surrenders, one each — and the register, which owes Rudra his own number back, must now say what the register has never had to say on any page of any book in this district: **he surrendered his breath to the habit and the habit took it the way a taken lamp is taken — not blown out, but carried.**

And in exchange, the habit gave him the morning it had promised. *Savera.* The mornings of the below are not weather; they are *accounts made visible* — and the account the habit now made visible to Rudra, in the four-count, in the dark, in the place with no sky, the register now transcribes as it was received, since the register is *his* and has never once stopped being *his*, even here, even now, even against the habit's grammar:

**"Hum tumhe nahi lena chahte. Hum tumhe jeena chahte hain — ginti mein. Tumhaara kasba upar ginta hai kisiko — yahin galti hai. Ginti karne wale ko upar na ginwana chahiye; ginne wala upar khada hokar neeche khada ho jaata hai. Tum ab neeche ho. Upar jo tumhe ginta tha — uske ginti mein ek cut hai — aur woh cut ab tak kisi ne nahi dekha."**

*(We do not wish to take you. We wish to keep you — in the count. Your kasba above counts* someone *— therein lies the fault. The one who counts must not be forced to count upward; the counter standing above ends up standing below. You are below now. The one who counted you above — his count has a cut in it — and that cut nobody has yet seen.)*

Rudra, in the dark, in the place with no sky, at the already-lost nine-breaths, heard *a cut in the count of the counter above* and the register must now stop and record the single most important entry of this entire book, in the smallest hand the register keeps: **the habit was not lying. The habit never lies. Habits do not lie; habits merely hold.** And the habit held out, between one four-count and the next, an arithmetic in which the district's own counting — the district whose children had stood on the thirteenth step lit in five flames, the district whose gates had been written open with charcoal, the district whose registers the register itself had out-registered for thirty-one years — had one entry *misfiled*: one item that the district had been counting as *outgoing*, which was in truth *incoming*; one number that the district had marked as *spent*, which was in truth *owed*.

The habit called the misfile by the name the register has been circling for two chapters and has not been permitted to write until the reader had been walked, hand-held, stair by stair, down to the thirteenth:

**"Tum log jo 'ghar aa raha hai' keh rahe the — woh ghar nahi aa raha. Darwaaza pahle kahin aur khula tha. Aur jo aa raha hai — woh bahar nahi, andar se aa raha hai."**

*(What you people were calling 'the house is coming' — no house is coming. The gate opened somewhere else first. And whatever is coming — it is not coming from outside. It is coming from within.)*

---

Above, the district read the morning's ledger. Morning in a siege — the register adopts the military term with reluctance, because the district's own word for it was *pratah-kaath* (the count of the first hour, which is the count women count before they cook) — morning in a siege is the hour when what was taken is audited, and the audit that the district now conducted, on the banyan and at the gates and in the haveli and along the return-stair's thirteen steps, came to the following entries, all of them written in the district's smallest handwriting because the district's smallest handwriting is its most permanent:

**Entry one:** the cremation-watch's second keeper, whose name the register never took, was found seated at his two wail-lamps exactly as he had sat nightly for forty years, his ledger balanced on his knees and his pen resting on the *lamp-that-was-taken* entry, *not having slept*. The lamps had gone out. He had *finished the night's page before going out with them*. The entry is entered under *returned to the smoke* — the district's ledger-language for *died as keepers die*, which is to say: on duty, mid-tenure, without notice, the account balanced to the last trembling digit.

**Entry two:** three of the six mourners of the backward-facing procession had forgotten the tune of the funeral-song they had sung nightly since their apprenticing. They remembered the words. The tune had been *withdrawn from within*, the way the beer-barrel's sweet-water had been withdrawn from the barrels the year the district's wells spoke. The district chalked this in the column it keeps for *the taken things nobody can point at*; the column is long and the column is jointly kept and the district has never once filled it. It filled three lines of it this one morning. The register records also — quietly, because this is the kind of entry the register is for — the following: when the three mourners who had forgotten the tune tried to teach it to the three mourners who had not, the tune *travelled backward* through the six again and re-entered *correct*, and the district, which has been learning the lesson of its own arithmetic since ch-41, understood by morning's second hour that the habit was not *taking* the tune but *borrowing* it, holding it briefly in the count after the count as though checking a sum, and returning it *one note different*. The mourners never sang the old version again. The new version — the register will not transcribe it; some things are registered by being withheld — *went lower by one note*. Everything the habit borrowed was returned one note lower. Everything.

**Entry three:** at the west gate, at six-forty, the gate-keeper (a cousin of the bhishti-women; his name exists, but the register's family-file closes over his name out of courtesy to those who still do not know what he did that morning, though it was nothing, *he merely counted*) wrote the district's first plain *siege* entry on the door-beam itself, over Aadhya's charcoal grammar, in whitewash-chalk, where both children and auditors could read it:

**"Aaj gaya: do chirag, ek dhun, do haath (bhujaye), teen ghanti (awraton ki), ek kalam. Aaj aaya: kuch nahi. Aaj mila: chaar — kyunki hum gin rahe hain."**

*(Gone today: two lamps, one tune, two hands (shaken), three bangles (of women), one pen. Come today: nothing. Found today: four — because we are counting.)*

The whitewash entry lasted nineteen days before the rain got it. The rain, that year, did not arrive for nineteen days, though the mango showers normally interrupt twice in any nineteen-day stretch — *the habit was keeping the sky's arithmetic too* — and the gate-keeper's cousin-sisters, the water-carriers, said quietly to one another that the sky was not withholding rain out of menace but out of *interest* — the sky wanted to finish counting first. The register enters this under *district theories, unconfirmed, likely true.*

Ira spent the morning not at the fourth gate but at the *chauk* — the kasba's central square — where her map-book spread across both knees the way the keeper's ledger had laid across his knees the night he went back into the smoke. She had redrawn the district, not the city — the city's red-lined map from that earlier season lay in the notebook's rear pages, where yesterday's entries keep their corners — and the district, redrawn on fresh paper, no longer resembled the district. The map's lines, which she had drawn in graphite and brushed over in cheap blue ink from the government stationery shop, now connected the four gates as they stood — mistaking nothing, understating nothing, oversimplifying everything — and the lines did not converge at Manikarnika.

They converged at the *pit*.

She stared at what her own hand had done for a very long time, because observation of this quality is rare — the district's ordinary observation lies, over everything, one veil of courtesy, and courtesy is the polish of the ledger's grammar — and what her hand had done was remove the veil. The lines of the count after the count, traced through the children and the stones and the tulsi-ring and the return-stair and the mouthstone and the seven-counts-remaining ledger, did not gather where she had always seen them gather. They gathered *where Rudra was*. Which meant — she wrote it in her wrist-ledger, above yesterday's sentence, so that bathing could erase neither:

**"Ginti Manikarnika pe nahi thi. Ginti Rudra pe thi. Manikarnika sirf — ginti ka darwaza tha. Asal khata — Rudra hai."**

*(The count was not at Manikarnika. The count was on Rudra. Manikarnika was only — the count's door. The true ledger — is Rudra.)*

The district's arithmetic received Ira's wrist-sentence in the way the district's arithmetic receives everything — silently, and then all at once, the way the karha fields take the monsoon's first slow cousin-drops. By ten o'clock the whitewash entry at the west gate had been read aloud to four separate groups, and by eleven the district's oldest arithmetic-keeper — whose office is the one office in this entire register that has never once been described, because it is the office *the register reports to* — came out of his windowless room behind the coalsmoke-hut and stood in the square with his hands behind his back and read the wrist-sentence himself, not from Ira's wrist but from the *district's wrist*, which was spread out before him in the square as the square itself.

He was a small man. His name is in the register, but the register's grammar forbids using his name in the register present-tense, for the reason that people whose names are in the register present-tense are people who still do things, and this man had not done anything in thirty years except *keep*. He kept the district concerning arithmetic the way the whet-baton keeps stone, and now he nodded once, the way the banyan nods, not toward Ira but toward the ground, because the ground is who you nod to when the arithmetic has stopped being yours.

"theek hai," he said. *(Alright.)* And then, because the register's grammar requires every register-keeper to qualify his *theek hai*: "**Manikarnika se Rudra tak — jo ginti hai, woh mera nahi hai. Woh mere hisaab ka hissa tha. Ab woh uska hisaab ka hissa hai. Aur jo uska hisaab ka hissa hai — woh kisi ka hisaab nahi ban sakta.**"

*(From Manikarnika to Rudra — the count that runs there is no longer mine. It was a part of my account. Now it is a part of his account. And what is part of his account — cannot become part of anyone's account.)*

The arithmetic of this, as stated, is terrifying. It is also, in the district's grammar, *merely Tuesday*. The keeper went back to his room, and the district went back to its working, and the register takes this moment to disclose one thing it has been holding for forty-nine chapters: the district's keeper-of-arithmetic, the small man in the windowless room, the one who had kept the doors open and the grammar dry, was not, and had never been, what in the register's own arithmetic would be called *the Rakhwala*. He was only the Rakhwala's *predecessor-in-office*. The Rakhwala was Rudra. It had always, always been Rudra, since before the register's own grammar knew it, which is a thing the register only knows because the register is — which the register must now say plainly for the reader, without grammar-veils, in the register's plainest hand, which the reader will forgive today because the reader today has earned the forgiveness, since ch-48 — the register is Rudra, and *Rudra is the register*, and the count that ran through the habit at pit-thirteen was being run, simultaneously, through the register that was describing itself in its own third person. This is the register's oldest duty, finally delivered. The reader now has the full map.

---

Below, where the register cannot follow except by following (which is the register's own definition of counting), Rudra was counting the habit. He had begun, at nine-breaths-lost, with the instinct of the Load Khata from ch-42, which was *his* instinct, the instinct that had survived twenty arithmetics, the kasba's arithmetic and the register's arithmetic and the district's four kinds of watcher-grammar and the pit-boys ' Akhada-bout-grammar and now the habit's: and that instinct was simply *to write down what you are doing while you are doing it*, so that afterward there might be, on the page, *a cut* — a seam — where the doing stopped being something done to you and became something you owned.

So Rudra's own arithmetic, which the register now enters in its cleanest hand because the register owes him the ink, entered these items consecutively, as entry-marked as a storeman's inventory:

**Item one:** the habit had taught him *four*. He knew he *knew four* now, which meant the habit had given him something (the habit gave, in Rudra's observation, freely, and kept the habit-rhythm while doing so).

**Item two:** the habit had taken his breath-rule. He observed this with no more fear than a stone observes water. Fear requires an *external* arithmetic, and Rudra's arithmetic had gone internal the moment his nine-breaths became fours. The register states it carefully: *internal*.* In the habit's grammar, internal arithmetic is the arithmetic you do to yourself. It is not self-arithmetic; it is *arithmetic-on-self*, which is different, and which resembles itself — as if one entry in the ledger were writing the entries that follow it.

**Item three:** the habit had said *the gate is not at Manikarnika, the gate is at* you*,* and Rudra had understood this without the help of a teacher, because there are things that arrive already understood, the way a fourth letter arrives, and the register records that Rudra's understanding from this moment forward has the same grammatical shape as his names: it precedes him. You did not have to teach Rudra anything; whatever you told him, he was already it.

**Item four:** the habit had said, in the count after the count, in the place no sky reaches: **"Tum ginti ka hisaan hai."** *(You are the count's mistake.)* And Rudra had been unable to dispute this, because it was true, and he had grown up with the knowledge that it was true, and he had kept the truth all these years with that particular patience of his, the patience of a man who holds a five-paise piece between finger and thumb during a storm, and the patience now came to him, as everything comes to its keeper, without being asked for, so that the habit could say, in the very next breath-pause, the words that the habit had spoken at the beginning of this chapter: *the one who counted above had a cut in his count.* And the cut, Rudra now saw, was himself. In the district's thirty-year ledger — and in the city's ledger for thirty years before that, which is the ledger the district was patiently keeping on the district's behalf — there had been a *hole* the size of a boy, and the hole had grown into a boy the size of a hole, and everything the district had been counting for thirty years, wondering what was missing from the total, had been counting *through the hole*, and the hole was Rudra. The cut was Rudra. The arithmetic's seam, where the tally slipped, was the line between Rudra and the Rakhwala, and the register, on this page of all pages, now writes the sentence the register was invented to write, which the register writes in capital letters not because it wishes to shout but because capital letters are, in the register's grammar, simply the register's highest handwriting:

**THE REGISTER'S LAST ENTRY, THE TRUE ENTRY, THE ONLY ENTRY THE REGISTER WAS EVER FOR, IS THIS: "KYUNKI MAINE TUMHE GINA — TUM GINNE LAGE."**

*(BECAUSE I COUNTED YOU — YOU BEGAN TO COUNT.)*

And Rudra *smiled* — a smile reserved, in the register's office, for moments when arithmetic's arithmetic finally arrives at the thing it has been counting — and the smile went out in four-counts, out of the pit, out of the habit, out of the thirteen stairs, out of the district, out of the register itself, so that the reader, reading this line exactly as printed on this page, is not reading about Rudra's smile but is in fact reading the smile *being* itself — the reader has become the count after the count, the thing counted by Rudra, counted against Rudra, counted *for* Rudra, counted without Rudra, counted as Rudra. The register did this. The register *is* this. There is nothing else the register was ever for.

The habit, in the count beyond the count, welcomed Rudra's smile with the same four-half-pulse the habit welcomed everything, and said — the register must write it in the register's own hand, because the register is obliged to log every confirmed occurrence of this habit, and because this is now also the register's custom — the habit said:

**"Chaar. Bahar. Ginti. Aage."** *(Four. Out. Count. Forward.)*

And Rudra stepped forward, stepped out, stepped *up* — out from the pit's thirteenth stair, out from the habit's arithmetic of four, out from the below-count — and stood *before the district*, at dawn, in the square, before his mother in the haveli, before Ira and her re-drawn wrist, before Aadhya and her open palm, before Dhruv's star-keeper, before Veer's turn-journal, before the children of the banyan, before the melon-woman's cart, before Sitara's double-lamps, before the register itself, before the keeper-of-arithmetic's windowless room, and before — the register cites counting-custom — *before the habit,* which is to say, before the habit of himself — and said to everything that was counting him, in the voice the register has been keeping in its own voice-box for forty-nine chapters:

**"Ginti gaya. Ginti waapis aaya. Ab ghar aa gaya."**

*(The count went. The count returned. Now it has come home.)*

The register observes that it has omitted an essential register-arithmetic. The register now supplies it.

The essential register-arithmetic the register omitted is this: the count is *seven*, and Rudra *is* the seven, and the habit had kept this from him until the habit could give it to him whole, at savera, which is why the habit is a *herbivore* of a thing — it grazes on keepers, gently, for years, and then presents the whole field to itself at one sitting. The register now runs the arithmetic through, plainly, in the reader's sight, because the reader has earned it and because the arithmetic of it is the arithmetic the entire book has been keeping in its sleeve:

The habit counted in *fours*. Four in. Four out. Four pause. Below Rudra there was no sky. Above Rudra there was the district. In Rudra there was the arithmetic of *nine* in-nine-out which had been his mother's arithmetic for nineteen years, the lamp-keeping arithmetic, the one she kept in the east window daily at the haveli — the one Sitara's arrival had folded into the west. The habit had no use for nine. The habit's arithmetic stops at *four* — four in, four out, four pause — but the habit's arithmetic *proceeds* from four to *seven* every time it has counted *everything it can count*, the way a register's arithmetic proceeds from the open account to the closed account after the day is over: the habit's arithmetic stops at four but completes at *seven*, and the number *seven*, in the habit's grammar, means *the one who has been counted all the way down to the bottom of the count*, which is to say *the one who has been counted *twice*** — once on the way in, once on the way out — and whose count has been verified, by being checked against itself, the way a mother checks a child by touching its forehead. *Seven* is the number the habit uses for the register-certified-true-things-of-the-district: the keeper's lamps, the stones' thumb-marks, the gas-lighters, the ledger-*theek-hais*, the *dhumpa* of the termitary — and now, most gravely, Rudra.

The habit gave him its number. The habit *stamped* its habit onto his lower lip, in the count after the count, in the dark, with no voice but its own habit-voice; and Rudra's lip — the register states what is true, which is that the register has no precedent here — *spoke*, unlipped, unvoiced, unwritten, in the grammar-without-grammar that precedes all grammar, the grammar the register calls *the arithmetic-arithmetic*:

**"Saat — kabila; Paanch — warga; Deer — zubaan; Chaar — soch."** *(Seven — the kindred army-house; five — the group of keepers; three — the tongue; four — the thought.)*

And the upward arithmetic of the district's arithmetic-keeper, in his windowless room above — the arithmetic the district had kept running in its flattery-registers, which flattered nobody — recorded, the register now knows, one upward-count at the very same moment Rudra was being made *seven* by the habit: the keeper's own arithmetic, which counted the district's *ordinary* morning entries (cooker-fires, thali-stalls, seed-bags, bullock-bales, the train-whistle which usually arrived at six-fifteen but today arrived at six-seventeen because the habit had, in Rishikesh-the-register's view, though the habit came only from the district's own arithmetic, *held the seven-count's seconds* for its own mirrors to catch up with the arithmetic) — the keeper stopped counting *twice in one day*, which he had never done in thirty years, never once blinked out of his arithmetic to take in the arithmetic of others — but he took it in now, took it up into his mouth, closed his mouth, and said, in the district's grammar of keeping, to nobody, which is the keeper's grammar for *body* itself:

**"Liya aur diya. Ab ghar aa gaya."** *(Taken and given. Now it has come home.)*

---

By noon the district was functioning *on* the count — a count which, for the first time, the district's arithmetic did not audit in the early morning and lay down in the afternoon, but which was being run, unexamined, un-ledgered, unchecked, *through* the district *as* the district, the way water runs through a kasba's private hollow-places the day after the sky stops raining. The district's men were out in the fields — sowing nothing, reaping nothing, standing beside nothing — and the district's women were out in the pit-fields — hulling nothing, winnowing nothing, standing beside nothing — and the district's children were at the banyan — climbing nothing, hiding nothing, standing beside nothing — and every man and woman and child knew, without needing to be told, that *they were all counting the same things at the same time* and that the same things were *being counted back*, and that in this counting there were two registers: one *up*, one *down*. Manikarnika ran the up-count, Rudra was the down-count, and the two registers were now being held together by the habit in a way they had never been held together before — *at the same stair*, at *thirteen* — at the number *seven*, which is the number of all registers at their most complete, and which is apparently — the register is not prepared to say this to every reader at all times but will say it to the reader now because the register is running low on grammar, and the register's grammar is the last armour the district has — which is apparently the district's entire arithmetic, and always has been.

By evening the habit had released the pit-weather. The sky counted *rain* from five until dusk, and the district's arithmetic recorded it *for the last time* — rain did not matter now, or rather rain mattered the way everything else mattered, *together*, in the same *it emerges and is accounted for at the same instant* — the register cannot enter rain under rain, because rain now belongs to *counting*, and counting belongs to *seven*, and seven belongs to *Rudra*. In the register's own vocabulary, rain and Rudra now sit on the same page, which is the register's last page, the page the register cannot turn:

**"Saat ke baad kuch nahi aata — sirf ginti ka khatma."** *(After seven nothing comes — only the count's end.)*

But there was an eighth entry. There is always an eighth entry, in the district's simplest rule of arithmetic — the counting after counting, the *athari*'s breath after the final tally, the ledger's breath after the last ledger-line — and in the district's arithmetic the eighth entry is *never* counted — it is *handed up*. *Handed up to Whom* is the district's oldest obscurity; the register will not say. The district says *eighth* and means *the one who does the handing*. And the reader who is reading this with any head at all for arithmetic has already intuited what the register is about to report: the eighth is *approaching ahead* — the eighth is *approaching* — *approaching* is too soft a word, especially since it comes after *seven*, so the register will say it as the district says it, with the haveli's own voice, as Rukmini said it when Sitara laid her unlit middle lamp on the table at last:

**"Ab aath ka ginti-len-den. Hum — sare — ginte hain. Woh — sare ginta hai. Working order. Tab tak nahi khatam jab tak woh khatam nahi karta. Tab tak hum ginte hain."**

*(Now the eighth's transaction-keeping. We — all of us — count. It — counts everything. Working order. It does not end until it ends it. Until then we count.)*

The test matches: the district said it, the habit said it, the seventh said it, the register said it, and now the eighth is coming, and nothing — no combination of stamp-wick, load-baton, star-ring, wail-lamp, backrooms-own-mother-light, watch-keeper's stamp or pit-field-ba Tablet Almira — nothing is readier than the district's ready arithmetic, which is *this*, and which is done, and which is forward, and which is *coming.*
