# CHAPTER FIFTY-EIGHT — THE WEIGHT HE SET DOWN

---

> *"The river could be seen through the broken frame.*
> *That is the time entered for closure. It is not the time we stopped carrying the wounded."*
>
> *— Manikarnika recovery report, copy attached to the casualty list*

---

The crack reached the door hinge.

Aadhya took her shoulder off the wood. The upper fastening came out of the wall where her head had been. She backed onto the sound middle of the passage as the door fell across the empty board.

Dust covered her feet.

"Main nikal gayi," she called before Rudra could ask.

He could not turn to see. The upright lay across his forearms. Every tooth of the wheel dragged it a little farther against him; every movement travelled up the loaded cable to the house.

His left knee was on stone. His right foot would no longer lie flat.

"Ghar ke neeche se hato," Ram called towards the upper turning.

The warning went ahead of the falling plaster. People beyond the bend moved away from the adjoining wall. Dhruv came down the last two river steps with his angle-ring already in his hand.

He did not put himself beneath the iron beside Rudra.

He knelt at its loose foot and looked for somewhere to return its weight.

"Veer. Neeche ka kona. Bas kona."

Veer pushed himself up from the sack. Ira kept her hand on the terrace stone instead of taking the arm he could not offer.

The joined pale bands lit across his shoulders. His spear formed in his right hand, bright along the length, the point wavering once as he tried to lower it.

He put the butt against a sound step. The shaft came up beneath the fallen frame's lower corner.

Rudra felt a little weight leave his left arm.

Not enough to stand. Enough to stop that arm being ground into the stone.

Veer bent over the shaft. His ribs would not let him take the next full breath.

"Patthar," he said.

Dhruv caught a loose block beside the channel and began rolling it towards the lifted paving.

"Wahan nahi," Aadhya said. "Uske neeche khokhla hai."

The peepal root had lifted that paving before the fighting. Now the stripped length lay exposed beneath it. She had felt the loose bed when she tried to hold it earlier; covering it with a block would not make it a foundation.

She pointed towards the stair cheek at Rudra's back.

"Yahan. Seedhi ka hissa."

Dhruv changed his position. His heel struck the stair-side stone twice. It did not rock. He rolled the loose block onto that sound ledge, keeping his hands clear of the falling foot.

A thin pale line ran from his angle-ring to the iron's lower edge.

"Do ungli," he told Rudra. "Uthao. Kheenchna nahi."

Rudra pressed his back harder into the stair cheek. The red-black thickened along his forearms. Gold crossed the torn cloth at his neck, and fresh blood entered the collar beneath it.

The upright rose an inch.

Veer's spear shook.

Dhruv rolled the block into the gap with the edge of his ring. Ram pushed a smaller stone behind it with his shoe, preventing it from sliding back towards the channel.

"Neeche. Dheere."

Rudra let the weight return.

Stone grated. The block held between the sound ledge and the bent foot. Dhruv kept his line there until the iron had stopped moving against it.

Then Veer took the spear out.

It disappeared before he could use it as a walking stick. He lowered himself onto the terrace step with his good hand against the wall. Ira named the edge for him this time.

Rudra's left arm came free.

It would not straighten at once. He held it against his ribs until the fingers stopped trying to close without him.

"Chhodo ab," Ram said, looking at the other forearm.

Rudra tried.

The iron settled another fraction. He put the arm back.

Ram watched the block rather than his son's face.

"Aur nahi baith raha. Dekho."

Rudra looked. The block had found its bed. The next click of the wheel travelled through it into the stair, not down through his arm.

He drew the arm away.

For a moment he remained kneeling beneath something he was no longer holding.

Then Ram gave him his shoulder, and Rudra got his left foot under him. He did not put all his weight onto the cut right ankle.

---

The opening had widened while they worked.

A strip of the far bank should have been visible above the lower ring. There was only darkness there, with smoke descending through it in long straight threads. Cold slid over Rudra's bleeding wrist.

He turned his head towards the exposed coupling.

Its solid shoulder passed the stair every turn. Behind it, teeth met and parted. From the winch came the same short pull, then the click which kept the load from returning.

Ram pointed to the place where the shorter shaft entered the turning ring.

"Yeh tootega toh ghoomna rukega. Bahar ki tang kheenchoge toh ghar aayega."

Rudra could see the difference now. The part he had caught was holding the assembly upright. The smaller joint was driving its turn.

He wiped his left knuckles against his shirt. The plate cuts stung. He needed that hand to open properly, not simply become strong enough to close.

At the desk the Right Hand reached for the longer control handle.

Water rose behind Ram's heels.

Rudra pulled his father onto the dry strip before the ridge struck.

The blow split the small stone Ram had used behind the block. Dhruv's line caught the block before it shifted off the ledge.

The main lever stayed locked down.

Rudra turned towards the hand making the corrections.

They had to break more than a piece of iron.

---

"Main handle rok sakti hoon," Ira said. "Thodi der."

Veer looked from her to the desk. "Main rokun?"

"Tum yahin rehkar."

He understood the part she had not said. His knee would not carry him across that stair again. Hers was not a request to pretend otherwise.

He rested his right elbow against the folded sack. A narrow pale curve opened above his hand, its lower interruption still there.

Ira kept her covered eye towards the wall. With her right she received the live curve. The second edge appeared beside the desk, too high at first, whitening the corner of the raised lid.

"Neeche," Rudra told her.

It moved.

"Ab uski taraf. Bas."

The Right Hand tried to pull the long handle back.

Its grip met the reflected curve.

Metal rang softly. The handle stayed short of its next notch.

The main lever below it remained locked down. The wheel continued turning. Ira had stopped one correction, not the opening itself.

Rudra moved towards the coupling.

Ram caught the loose cloth at his back.

Not pulling him away. Just enough to make him look.

"Khula haath," Ram said.

Rudra looked down. His fingers were already closing.

He opened them. Gold shivered across the cut knuckles. The left hand wanted to close again; he kept the heel of it ready instead.

Ram let go of the shirt.

---

The coupling's solid shoulder came towards the stair.

Rudra put the flat of his right palm against its outer face, well outside the teeth. He meant to turn the short shaft across its own line of movement, not hold the rotating iron in his fingers.

The first pressure skated his hand sideways.

He took it off before it could be carried round.

Pain ran up the old wrist into the shoulder. He had changed. The wrist had not become another wrist.

"Usko pakadke mat rukna," Ram said. "Side se. Saath mein pair."

Rudra set his left foot closer. The bruised knee bent badly. He lowered his hip to keep the weight off the cut ankle and waited for the solid face to come round again.

Behind him Veer breathed out through his teeth.

Ira's reflected edge wavered at precisely the same moment as his source. She did not fill its weak corner by wanting it filled. She brought the whole curve a little nearer the handle instead.

At the desk the bare injured hand withdrew.

The other reached across the lid towards the steel stop-pin.

"Pin le raha hai," she called.

Rudra could not look away from the teeth now.

The solid shoulder arrived.

He met it with the heel of his palm and moved his body across it, a short drive from the left hip. Red-black gathered against the iron. Gold held the line from wrist to elbow while the two counts fought for the breath beneath his ribs.

His right ankle dragged. The cut reopened against the sandal strap.

He kept moving sideways, away from the teeth, forcing the shorter shaft out of its straight alignment.

A bolt bent.

The coupling screamed against its collar.

The next click did not come.

The opening stopped widening.

Rudra felt the load seeking the house cable again and eased his shoulder rather than pressing the whole frame towards it. Ram pointed to the bent joint.

"Udhar. Jo mud gaya. Wahi."

A crack had opened across the coupling's collar. The joint was no longer turning cleanly; its bent face had jammed against the fixed housing beside it.

Rudra took his hand away.

The joint stayed jammed.

The house cable remained taut, but no new length travelled through its saddle. Beside the courtyard door the crack in the plaster stopped dropping dust.

"Ruki?" Aadhya called.

"Badhna ruka," Ram answered. "Band nahi hua."

Through the rings the darkness remained. Smoke still descended where the river should have been.

One result. Not a finished fight.

---

Ira's uncovered eye had begun to water. She could see the handle, but its edges were spreading into the glare of her own reflection.

"Veer. Chhodne ko bolna."

"Bolunga."

"Pehle bolna."

His right hand trembled. She could see it. That was why she needed the words before the light disappeared, not a brave nod after it.

"Chhod raha hoon," he said.

She released the reflection. His curve fell a moment later. Nothing stayed beside the desk.

Ira leaned back against the terrace stone. Fresh moisture warmed the edge of her left-eye dressing. She did not touch it with the hand which had been against grit and water.

At the desk the steel pin passed through the space the reflected curve had occupied. The long handle came free behind it.

"Ram," Ira said.

He was already looking.

The Right Hand drove the control over, towards the place where Ram stood between the joint and his son.

Rudra saw the water flatten along the channel.

Ram shoved him off its first line.

The blow took Ram across the right side and threw him against the fixed iron base.

The impact made a short, dull sound.

Rudra heard it through the strain of the iron.

---

Ram tried to get a hand beneath himself.

It slipped. Not on water. The towel round his palm had opened again.

Rudra reached him as he tried a second time.

"Mat utho."

His father looked at the jammed coupling over his shoulder. He drew a short breath, stopped halfway through it, and pressed his other hand against his lower right ribs.

Aadhya knelt on the dry side of the base. She set her forearm behind his shoulders without pulling him upright.

"Saans?" she asked.

Ram nodded.

The next one was shorter.

"Upar se medical waalon ko bulao," she called. "Abhi."

Veer repeated it towards the road. A watchman's answer came back from the turning. Feet started down the sound middle of the passage, away from the fallen door.

Aadhya kept her numb fingers out from under Ram's back. She could feel his next breath against her forearm. Uneven. Present.

Rudra wanted to stay with that one fact until it became enough.

At the desk the long handle moved again.

The water beside his father's knee drew into a flat shining edge.

Rudra stood between it and the two people on the ground.

He took the blow across his forearm. The impact drove his elbow against his chest and opened the seam above his collar. Gold broke into bright separate stitches. The darker outline held at the arm, thin at the shoulder, and he could not make the two counts agree enough to stop the shaking.

He turned with the force instead.

Water struck the empty lower step. Vedant caught its freed edge with the flow from his basin and sent it down the service channel.

Rudra looked towards the desk.

"Ab meri taraf dekh."

---

He did not jump the space.

The cut ankle would not give him that. He took the two short steps beside the winch, using the sound paving he had watched Ram cross, and put his left forearm across the long handle before it could return to its next setting.

The Right Hand let go of it.

The steel pin came up beneath Rudra's arm.

He saw its point too late to catch it. He turned his shoulder instead. Iron scraped along the outside of his upper left arm, opening cloth and skin instead of entering beneath his ribs.

No wall of power had made the flesh untouchable.

Rudra pushed the weapon arm out with his own elbow. His fingers wanted to close round the wrist. He did not trust what would happen if they did.

The man stepped inside that push.

A boot struck Rudra's swollen right ankle.

His leg folded.

The pin turned towards the raw line at his throat.

He let himself go down another inch instead of wrenching the damaged foot round. The point passed over his shoulder. He came inside the striking elbow, close enough that the long pin had no room to turn back towards him.

His left knee hit the plinth.

Pain emptied his mouth of the breath he had been trying to hold. The outline flickered along his jaw.

The Right Hand drove his free palm against that same shoulder.

Rudra slid. Caught the plinth's edge with his left foot. Felt the bruised knee threaten to give again.

He could have answered with everything in his right hand.

The force was there, hot and dense. He could feel it asking for the shortest path through the body in front of him. He could also remember his father's bloody palm slipping against the base, and the workman cradling an arm Rudra had only meant to stop.

He put his forearm over the elbow of the weapon arm instead.

Wood took the first pressure. The desk's edge split beneath the man's arm.

Rudra kept his palm open and drove the trapped forearm down onto the wood, away from the throat, away from the controls.

The pin fell.

He moved his arm off the elbow at once.

The Right Hand reached for the handle with his injured hand.

Rudra got his other forearm beneath the handle and drove it sideways. Its thin mount tore. The handle struck the raised lid and folded it back. One desk-leg went under as the mount tore through the wooden top.

The man lost his footing as the desk went down.

His back struck the low service ledge behind it. One knee folded beneath the fallen wood. He tried to rise on the injured hand, failed, and caught himself on the other elbow.

The control handle lay between him and Rudra, its narrow linkage torn out with the mount.

Rudra planted a foot on the steel pin before that empty hand could reach it.

He did not plant it on the hand.

For a moment they remained there, breathing on opposite sides of the broken lid.

Then the Right Hand drew his arm back against his body.

He was down. Disarmed. No longer making the pressure turn.

Behind Rudra, Aadhya called for the medical men again.

---

The main lever was still locked in its deeper notch.

Rudra could hear the drive straining against the joint he had jammed. Leaving the man on the stone was not enough.

He took his foot off the pin and turned to the lever's stop-plate.

He worked his forearm beneath the little iron throat that held the lever down. The first push bent its corner. He shifted closer, kept the force in that small plate, and tore it off its mount.

The main lever sprang up.

The deep note beneath the winch stopped.

The cable did not become loose. A toothed catch still held the wheel against the weight already on it.

But nothing was pulling it farther into the house.

Rudra let the broken stop-plate fall onto the empty step.

"Band karte hain," he told Dhruv.

---

A road medic came down with two watchmen and a folded canvas stretcher.

He stopped at the bend, saw the fallen door, and followed Aadhya's voice along the sound middle of the lane. The frame stood between him and the man behind the desk. He went to the patient he could reach.

Aadhya made room for his bag. She stayed at Ram's shoulder until he had taken her place.

"Seedha utha doon?" the watchman asked.

"Ruko. Pehle saans dekhne do."

Ram tried to turn towards Rudra. The medic kept him from twisting onto the injured side.

"Baat mat karo."

Rudra could hear each word. He could not take his eyes off the cable yet.

---

Dhruv's ring-hand was shaking. The old rope burns had opened where its edge pressed the palm. His thin line still kept the supporting block from sliding; he had not left it to help at the desk.

"Pehle bojh utaarenge," he said.

He pointed to the catch which kept the big wheel from returning.

"Tum wheel sambhaalo. Main yeh uthaaunga. Ek daant. Phir baitha doonga."

Rudra put both forearms against a broad spoke on the wheel's outer side, away from the teeth. The metal was thick enough to bear the pressure his hands could not measure finely.

He drove against it, backwards.

The bent coupling shifted a fraction in its jam. The catch loosened against its tooth.

Dhruv used the torn-off control handle as a short bar. Keeping his ring-hand at the supporting block, he lifted the catch with the bar's end.

"Ab. Dheere."

The cable took the wheel back against Rudra's arms.

It wanted to go faster than that.

He braced his left foot on the sound ledge and let his body move with the spoke. Gold crossed the darker pressure along his forearms. His wrists shook inside it. He could slow the return; he could not make it feel light.

Dhruv lowered the catch into the next tooth.

The wheel stopped against it.

Above the saddle, the thick sleeve had begun to sag.

They did it again.

This time a loose curve of cable touched the stone beneath the house wall.

No new plaster fell. The building did not straighten or mend. It simply stopped being pulled towards the iron.

"Bas," Dhruv said.

Rudra took his arms off the spoke.

The catch held. Dhruv set the broken control handle down, then eased his pale line away from the frame's foot. With the cable's pull gone, the block sat squarely under the load.

The outer frame stayed upright without a person holding it.

Rudra looked once at the house crack, following it up to the hinge Aadhya had stepped away from.

Empty. Damaged. Still standing.

He turned back to the ring.

---

"Mujhe hata do," Ira said to Veer.

He did not try to lift her. He named the stair edges while she moved back on her own hand and heels, one step at a time. Then he shifted along the terrace behind her, keeping the slung arm clear of the stone.

Vedant drew the remaining water down the empty service channel. Dhruv moved to the river-stair side and raised his angle-ring between the frame and the upper turning.

Aadhya helped the two watchmen take Ram's stretcher to the dry bend under the medic's direction. They carried the ends; she steadied the injured side with her forearm, not the two numb fingers. They had not reached the road, but the falling iron could no longer reach his chest.

"Neeche khaali," Dhruv called.

Rudra waited until Vedant had taken his basin clear of the frame's foot.

Then he put his left forearm against the fixed collar and the right against the short shaft beyond it.

The crack he had made was between them.

He pulled the two sides across each other.

Not the whole frame. Not the cable. That one broken joint.

Iron gave a low, tearing note.

The four struck beneath his breastbone. The longer allowance met it late; for a moment his arms tried to take opposite instructions. He could feel the outline thinning over his injured ankle, feel how much of him was already being held together by the act of staying upright.

He did not find another count.

He put his weight into the crack.

The shaft broke.

The inner ring dropped out of alignment.

Between the iron bands the darkness narrowed sharply, as if a curtain had been drawn edge-on. Smoke struck its edge and curled back towards the sky instead of going down.

Rudra caught the falling inner band below its broken mount, keeping his hands on iron, out of the black gap.

He turned it towards the empty service steps.

The band bent across the fractured collar. Bolts tore free. The remaining strip of darkness shivered between two pieces of metal which were no longer facing each other.

Then Rudra drove the bent band down.

It struck the stair in a shower of stone and split at the old mounting hole.

The opening folded to a line.

Closed.

A boat-chain knocked beyond it.

Through the broken frame Rudra could see a narrow flame on the far bank, doubled in ordinary moving water.

---

The water beside Rudra's foot lay flat.

He waited for it to rise again.

A leaf turned in the runnel and floated past the broken shaft. Beyond the iron, smoke drifted across the river. It did not fall into an unseen depth.

Nothing was open there now.

He took his hands off the inner band. It stayed on the empty steps where he had put it, bent through its mount, no longer joined to the split collar. The main lever was up. Its torn-off stop-plate lay on the empty step. The house cable rested loose beneath the saddle.

Behind the fallen desk a dark sleeve moved.

Rudra could see the empty hand feeling for the ledge. No handle remained within its reach. The man did not get back to his feet.

"Desk ke peechhe," Rudra told Dhruv. "Zinda hai."

Dhruv turned towards the river stair to call the watchmen down by the other side.

Rudra took a step towards his father.

The right ankle gave under him.

He sat hard on the sound ledge. The outline thinned along his shoulders, then over his arms. Red-black drew close to the skin and went with the next breath. The gold withdrew from the body-shape without taking the bleeding seam with it.

He had not decided exactly how to end it. He could not have kept it another moment simply because people were watching.

Without the form, the swollen ankle hurt even when he kept it still.

For several seconds he could only sit there and breathe.

Vedant came from the channel. Ordinary water continued down the drain after he put his basin aside. He opened the dressing packet the medic had left on top of his bag and pressed the clean pad against Rudra's neck seam.

"Haath yahan."

Rudra put his left hand over the pad. The knuckle cuts protested. Vedant kept his own hand there until the pressure was steady.

"Chal sakte ho?"

Rudra looked towards the bend.

"Chalna hai."

Vedant offered his shoulder and helped him stand. He kept the pace slow enough that the ankle did not fold again.

---

Someone at the road end had begun saying that the river had come back.

"Ganga kahin gayi nahi thi," a man answered. He was the householder, still carrying the unused length of his torn towel. "Darwaaza band hua."

A second voice was calling Rudra's name. A third asked which lane had the injured.

The words crossed each other above the stretchers. There would be a story later, much larger than the broken joint or the people who had braced it. For now the householder moved people out of the way and shouted for the middle of the road to be cleared.

Aadhya stayed beside the stretcher.

The medic had loosened Ram's outer shirt and was listening with a stethoscope at the right side of his chest. He shifted the instrument, listened again, then put two fingers to the wrist above the dirty bandage.

Ram's eyes were open. He had not taken his hand away from the ribs.

"Papa," Rudra said.

His father found him with his eyes.

The gaze went straight to the dressing against Rudra's neck, then to the blood down his left arm.

Even now. Even with that shallow breath.

"Band ho gaya," Rudra told him.

Ram's eyes moved beyond him towards the steps.

"Haan," Rudra said. "Sach mein."

A little tension left the hand against the ribs. Not the pain. The medic could see the difference too; he caught the wrist before Ram tried to push himself higher.

"Aap mat hilliye."

Ram's lips moved.

Rudra bent closer, letting Vedant take the neck dressing again.

"Tumhari maa?"

"Gaadi mein. Sitara saath hain."

It was the last thing Rudra knew, and he gave it without adding an arrival, a doctor's promise or a recovery he had not seen.

Ram shut his eyes for a moment.

When he opened them, they took longer to settle on Rudra's face.

---

The medic lifted one edge of the shirt farther from the right ribs. A dark bruise was spreading there above the iron-strike mark. Ram coughed once. A little blood marked the corner of his mouth.

He tried to lay two fingers beside the bruise. A little warmth gathered beneath them. Then the hand shook and fell back onto the cloth. His breathing did not ease.

Aadhya looked down at her own shaking hands and kept them away from the bruise.

The medic looked only at Ram now.

"Hospital ko bolo chest injury hai. Nabz kamzor. Ambulance kahan tak aayi?"

The watchman at the road called back that another vehicle had been requested. No light was at this turning yet.

"Main road tak le chalo. Ruko mat."

The two bearers lifted together. Aadhya kept pace on the injured side, her forearm ready if the shoulder shifted. Her right thumb and index finger stayed straight; the other fingers shook against the rail.

Rudra reached for the stretcher's upper handle.

His hand would not stay closed.

He let the bearer keep it. Put his own empty hand beside his father's instead.

Behind them the river remained visible through the broken frame. Nothing rose to follow. The wheel did not turn.

Ram's eyes closed again.

"Papa?"

The medic touched the wrist, then leaned close enough to feel the next breath.

"Saans hai," he said. "Chalte raho."

Rudra laid two fingers in the open, bandaged palm as they went towards the road.

"Is baar aap chhodna mat."

His father's hand did not close around them.
