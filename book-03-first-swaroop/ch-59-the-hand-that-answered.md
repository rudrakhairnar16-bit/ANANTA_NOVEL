# CHAPTER FIFTY-NINE — THE HAND THAT ANSWERED

---

> *"We were told the boy had broken the iron rings with his hands.*
> *He needed help to open the packet for his father's clothes."*
>
> *— emergency nurse's statement, copied after the Manikarnika admissions*

---

Ram took another breath. His hand stayed open.

Rudra watched the shirt rise. Waited for it to fall. The fingers beneath his own remained open while the bearers carried the stretcher past the householder and into the cleared strip of road.

"Yahan mat rukna," the medic said.

Rudra had stopped without knowing it. Vedant put a hand at his back, below the new arm wound, and got him moving again.

Aadhya walked beside the other rail. Her right thumb and index finger stayed straight against the bandage. The others kept touching the canvas, checking that Ram's shoulder had not slid towards the injured side.

The ambulance had been called. It had not arrived.

The medic crouched beside the stretcher in the space the householder had cleared. He connected a mask to the road team's portable oxygen cylinder, fitted it over Ram's mouth and nose, spoke into a phone, then listened to the answer without taking his eyes off the shallow movement beneath the shirt.

Rudra heard a location being repeated. Not a promise of a time.

"Meri maa bhi—" he began.

The medic held up one finger while the person on the line finished speaking.

Rudra waited.

"Pehli gaadi kahan gayi?" he asked when the finger lowered. "Rukmini Vashisht. Kandhe pe chot. Sitara saath hain."

The medic gave the name to the control desk.

Behind the waiting families someone tried to lift a phone towards Rudra's face.

The householder put himself between the lens and the stretcher.

"Raasta chahiye," he said. "Tasveer baad mein."

The phone lowered. Its owner moved aside and helped clear another pace of road.

---

The ambulance reached them with its siren off. The driver had followed the last watchman's raised hands through the stopped traffic. Its rear doors opened as soon as the vehicle stopped.

Another attendant brought a wheeled trolley down the ramp. The medic gave him the time of the blow, the side which had struck iron, the weak pulse and the blood after the cough. Nobody asked Rudra to explain the machine before lifting his father.

He tried to keep two fingers in Ram's palm during the transfer.

"Abhi haath chhodiye," the attendant said.

Rudra let go.

They transferred Ram from the canvas stretcher to the trolley. Ram's head moved with the small change of surface, and Rudra's own body went forward as if it could prevent that one ordinary movement by getting close enough.

Vedant caught his elbow.

"Unko karne do."

Rudra stood out of the way until the trolley was locked into its rails.

One seat for family. The medic climbed in beside the equipment. Vedant looked at Aadhya, then at the people still being helped up from the ghat.

"Tum jao," he told Rudra. "Hum doosri gaadi se aayenge. Veer aur Ira ko lekar."

"Dhruv—"

"Neeche hain. Unke paas log hain. Tumhare papa ke paas ab tum."

Aadhya had found a sealed dressing packet in the medic's bag. She passed it to the attendant with her left palm open beneath it.

"Gardan ka bhi dekhna," she said, nodding at Rudra. "Khoon ruk nahi raha."

He wanted to object to being given a place among the patients.

The attendant had already made one for him on the narrow seat. He checked the pad at Rudra's collar, replaced it, and told him to keep his hand there without tightening anything round his throat.

The doors closed with Aadhya still outside them.

For the first few yards Rudra could see her through the rear glass, her hands held clear of the turning wheel, waiting until the vehicle had room to leave.

Then the road bent.

---

The journey did not become short because nobody spoke.

Traffic opened in pieces. A scooter edged onto a pavement. Two men moved a handcart sideways. Once the ambulance stopped beneath a strand of wedding lights while the driver asked a bus to move back enough for the corner.

The oxygen mask clouded and cleared over Ram's face.

Rudra watched that instead of trying to make the changing numbers on the monitor tell him more than he knew.

The medic checked the wrist again. Spoke to the receiving hospital. Used the word *critical* without raising his voice.

Rudra put his unbandaged fingertips against the edge of his own knee. The bruise hurt through the cloth. He held to the small, certain pain while the larger thing in front of him refused to give him a measure.

There had been a time when nine breaths were an allowance. He could remember four beating against them until his chest locked. Neither count could tell him how long a father was allowed to stay.

He stopped trying to count Ram's breaths into either one.

"Naam boliye," the medic said.

Rudra looked up.

"Patient ka. Reception ko bhejna hai."

"Ram Vashisht."

He said it again when asked to spell it. No title. No account of what those hands had done. A name the people waiting at the other end could use to find the correct bed.

Before they reached the hospital the control desk called back.

Rukmini's ambulance had delivered her there. Sitara was with her. That was all the dispatcher could confirm from the receiving entry.

Rudra shut his eyes once. Opened them before he could miss the next movement of the mask.

When the doors opened, a nurse was already beside the trolley.

"Right chest trauma?"

The medic answered. The hospital took the weight of the rails.

Rudra tried to follow through the inner doors.

The nurse stopped him with a hand against the door, not against his wound.

"Yahan tak. Aapka treatment idhar."

For the first time since the iron fell, he could not stay beside the stretcher by choosing to stay.

---

The nurse brought a chair before she brought a question.

Rudra sat because his right ankle had begun shaking inside the sandal. When she cut the strap away, the swollen skin rose against the loosened leather. She put the foot on a support and asked him where else he was hurt.

He started with the neck. Forgot the arm until she saw the blood down the sleeve. Forgot the knee until she tried to straighten it.

"Ek-ek karke," she said.

He looked towards the inner doors.

"Unke liye team hai. Aap yahan behosh honge toh ek aur bed chahiye."

He let her work.

They cleaned the outer-arm cut and dressed it. A doctor checked the neck wound, the wrist and the circulation in the foot. The gold line remained where the clean cloth ended; stopping the skin from bleeding did not make the old seam disappear.

A porter came for an ankle image. Rudra tried to get up before the chair's footrest had been moved.

"Weight nahi," the doctor said. "Image ke baad decide karenge."

The porter unlocked the wheels instead.

Rudra went past the resuscitation-room doors sitting down. That was how he met Sitara.

She stood out of the porter's path first, then bent until he did not have to lift his injured neck to see her.

"Maa yahin hain. Dressing ho gayi. Abhi observation mein hain."

"Saans?"

"Khud le rahi hain. Bol rahi hain. Gala dukh raha hai."

"Kandha?"

"Bleeding control ki hai. Baaya haath uthana nahi. Doctor bataayega aur."

Four useful facts. She gave him those rather than the word *fine*.

He nodded towards the doors behind her. "Papa andar hain."

Her gaze moved there at once.

"Pata chala. Main nurse se baat karke maa ko bataati hoon. Tumhara pehle yeh."

She touched the chair's handle, not his shoulder, and let the porter take him on.

---

By the time the ankle image was done, someone had attached a paper band to his wrist. He kept turning it away from the blood on his cuff, as if keeping that small white strip clean were something he had been asked to do.

The doctor found no fracture visible on the ankle image. That did not give him permission to walk on the injured ligaments and cut. The foot stayed raised. The knee needed rest; the wrist needed support; the dressings would have to be checked again.

Rudra heard each instruction. He asked when he could see his mother.

The nurse went to find out instead of letting him push the chair himself.

Rukmini was in a monitored treatment bay along the adjoining corridor. A curtain separated her from another bed. Her left arm rested on a padded support, the shoulder broad with dressings. A bruise had begun to darken beneath the jaw where the pressure had held her against the wall.

She turned her head towards the chair, winced, and stopped turning.

Rudra asked to be moved where she could see him without trying again.

Sitara stood on the other side of the bed. She had already told her about Ram.

There was no easier version left for Rudra to choose.

"Doctor ne critical kaha," he said.

Rukmini's good hand went flat against the sheet.

He told her about the blow. The fixed iron. Aadhya calling for the medic. Oxygen on the road. He did not say Ram was only tired, and he did not describe every second in which the open hand had failed to answer.

"Abhi andar kaam kar rahe hain," he finished.

Rukmini looked at Sitara. "Mujhe—"

"Abhi yahan se nahi," Sitara said. "Unka doctor aayega. Main bulaaungi."

Rukmini shut her eyes. Her fingers caught one small fold of the sheet and held it until the knuckles paled.

Rudra knew that grip. The cart handle had begun breaking in his own hand. He looked down at the unbroken cotton between hers and wished strength had been a thing he could lend without sending more pain along with it.

"Maa."

She opened the good hand before he could touch it.

He laid his fingertips there.

For a while neither asked the other for a promise.

---

A nurse came to check the shoulder dressing. The wounds had needed cleaning and closure; the pressure marks at the neck still needed watching. Rukmini would stay. She would not walk down the corridor to make a frightened family believe she could.

The nurse asked Rudra to move his chair a little.

He let her move it at once, then waited while she finished.

"Dadi ko khabar?" Rukmini asked Sitara.

"Phone karti hoon. Seedhi baat."

No voice from a tea stall. No stranger's picture with the wounded cropped out of it. Sitara went to the end of the bay to call the family herself, keeping Rukmini in sight.

"Tumne paani piya?" his mother asked him.

He almost said yes.

She looked at the dry blood at the corner of his mouth.

"Nurse se poochho. Jo bolen."

He asked. The answer was a little water, slowly, while they waited for the remaining checks. The nurse brought the cup within his good reach.

Rukmini watched him drink without trying to take the cup in the arm she could not lift.

For a few minutes that was all they managed: each allowing the other to receive help from somebody else.

Then a member of Ram's team came into the corridor and asked for his family.

Rudra put the cup down.

---

The doctor spoke to Rukmini and Rudra together, from the open side of her bay.

Ram was alive. He had broken ribs on the right, a badly bruised lung, blood and air collected around it. The team had put in a chest drain. He was receiving oxygen and treatment for low blood pressure. They were watching the drainage and his breathing closely.

"Abhi critical care mein jaayenge," the doctor said. "Yeh raat important hai."

Rukmini asked whether he had woken.

"Hosh aa-ja raha hai. Abhi baat nahi karni. Saans ka kaam pehle."

Rudra kept his hands flat on the chair's arms while he listened. The doctor did not make a promise, and did not leave them with only the worst word either. There were people with Ram. There was a treatment being done. Someone would come back to them if his condition changed.

The doctor asked them to repeat the parts they had understood.

Rudra managed the drain, the oxygen and the watching. Rukmini remembered the instruction about not making him speak.

They let the doctor return to him.

---

Vedant reached the corridor while Rudra was waiting to be taken back to his own treatment space.

The road party's driver had brought him in the patient van with Aadhya, Veer and Ira. Veer was having the arm and elbow checked again; Ira had gone to the eye team with spoken directions all the way from the van. Aadhya sat at a dressing station while a nurse examined the two fingers which still had no feeling.

"Dhruv ka phone hai," Vedant said.

Rudra took the phone on speaker. Vedant kept a hand beneath it until it lay securely on the chair's arm.

The sound from the ghat had changed. No grinding wheel behind the voice. Men calling to one another over loose stone. Someone asking for a torch to be held lower.

"Woh nikal gaya," Dhruv said.

Rudra looked up at Vedant.

"Kaise?"

"Do aadmi le gaye. River crew ke. Jo do pakde the, woh hamare paas hi the."

One captured workman still needed his injured arm examined; he had been sent up with an escort. Others had come for the man behind the broken desk.

Dhruv and the watchmen had needed the other stair. The inner band lay sharp across the short approach, its broken edge shifting when the first man tested the footing. They could see over it. They could not safely run through it.

From the lower landing they had seen two men helping a third along the mooring board. His injured leg would not take his weight. One arm stayed against his chest. The dark sleeve had torn at the elbow; the hand below it was bare where the finger-guard had been.

A service launch had already come off its first rope.

"Rok nahi paaye?" Rudra asked.

"Beech mein teen naav thi. Log the unmein."

A mooring line pulled across those boats would not have struck only the launch. Dhruv had kept the watchmen on the landing, called for the lower posts to watch the river, and made them report the direction instead of throwing force through the people in between.

He told Rudra that plainly. Not asking him to like it.

Rudra looked at the closed critical-care doors at the corridor's end.

"Khud chal raha tha?"

"Nahi. Uthaake le gaye."

Downstream, other grey-sleeved men had been seen taking covered cases off their shore fittings. Some went with the boats. Some disappeared through the emptied service alleys before the watchmen could reach them. The abandoned brackets and cut lines stayed on the stone.

Aegis Meridian was withdrawing from the ghat.

Not everyone had escaped. Not everyone had been caught.

"Frame?" Rudra asked.

"Toota hai. Darwaaza band hai. Waise hi."

The answer mattered. Rudra let himself hear it before reaching for the next fear.

Dhruv had put a watch around the wreck. Nobody was to crawl under the propped outer foot for a souvenir. The main cable was now slack, but the house wall remained cracked and the grain-store lintel still rested on its separate improvised support. Residents would not be sent home beneath either because the fighting had stopped.

"Tum yahin aao," Rudra said.

"Pehle yahan ke log. Phir. Vedant hain tumhare paas."

Rudra did not argue about which wounded man should be allowed to matter more.

"Papa ko drain daala hai," he said.

There was a pause in which the river could be heard at Dhruv's end.

"Mujhe update dena."

They ended the call.

---

Vedant did not take the phone away immediately.

"Uske peechhe jaana hai?" he asked.

Rudra's first answer sat hot behind his teeth. He looked down at the footrest, the swelling beneath the loosened dressing, the hand which still could not be trusted to hold a small object steadily after everything it had held that night.

"Abhi nahi jaa sakta," he said.

"Woh bhi sach hai. Doosra?"

At the far door a nurse stepped out and spoke to the attendant changing a cylinder. Rudra listened until he knew she was not calling his father's name.

"Yahan se jaana nahi hai."

Vedant took the phone back.

"Theek. Usse kam mat samajhna."

He asked a porter to take Rudra back for the rest of his dressings.

---

Ram's clothes came out before he did.

A nurse brought a clear belongings packet and asked Rudra to check the name on it. Inside lay the shirt, cut open at the right side, and the towel strip the householder had given him for the palm.

Rudra put his left hand against the seal. Tried to draw it apart with the supported right wrist.

The nurse took it back before he could tear his knuckle dressing.

"Main khol deti hoon."

He let her.

The shirt had been white at the shoulder before the grain-store lintel. Now dust had worked into the weave beside the blood. Rudra could see the place where his father had wiped his own palm instead of asking for another clean cloth while Rukmini lay on the cart.

He did not take it out.

"Rakhna hai?" the nurse asked. "Ghar bhej dein?"

"Yahin. Unko chahiye hoga."

She closed the packet and wrote on the outside that the family would collect it. No argument about whether a shirt cut that far open would ever be useful again.

Rudra watched her put it on the lower shelf behind the desk.

The hands that had tried to open it lay uselessly together in his lap. He separated them before the pressure hurt the bandages.

---

Veer came out of treatment in a chair with his injured leg supported. The left arm remained in its sling. The right elbow had a fresh dressing; somebody had made him tell the whole story of how an already injured man had managed to hurt that side as well.

"Daant padi?" Rudra asked.

"Tumhara naam nahi liya."

"Mera tha hi nahi."

"Isliye aasaani hui."

Neither laughed properly. Veer shifted until he could see the critical-care door without turning over the slung arm.

Ira joined them after the eye examination. Her covering had been replaced, not removed for a demonstration. There would be more checks; the doctor had not promised when the blur would clear. For now she was to stop reaching through that sight and let the injured eye rest.

She listened to the account of Ram's treatment without asking anyone to uncover anything so she could look for herself.

Aadhya sat beside her. Both palms had new dressings. The numb thumb and index finger had been examined and noted, not made obedient by the attention of another pair of hands.

Vedant brought food from the hospital canteen after asking who was allowed to eat.

Nobody wanted it until the lids came off. Then Veer found he had been hungry enough for his good hand to shake. Ira asked for the spoon to be put down on her right rather than handed from the covered side.

Rudra ate a little with the spoon resting across his left fingers and stopped when the mouth wound protested. Aadhya was trying to hold her own spoon in the left hand without pressing it into the torn nail-beds.

They made room for the effort each person needed.

"Roop ka naam poochh rahe the," Veer said after a while. "Bahar."

Rudra looked at him.

"Maine patient ka naam bata diya."

"Kiska?"

"Tumhara. Doctor bulaa raha tha."

Ira's mouth moved into a tired, uneven smile. Aadhya put the spoon down before she could drop it.

For a moment four friends sat in a hospital with food going cold between them, and nobody asked any of them to become something else.

---

A watchman passed the corridor with a man whose forearm was in a temporary splint.

Rudra recognized the torn work shirt before he recognized the hand. The man who had surrendered the cable-shears kept his eyes on the floor. The escort stopped at the next treatment desk.

"Iska haath bhi," Rudra said to the nurse beside them.

"Dekhenge. Injury ka time bataaiye."

He told her. Then he told her he had been the one pressing the hook-shaft when the man cried out.

She wrote down what was useful for the examination. She did not ask whether the injured man had been on the correct side of the fight before calling someone to assess him.

The escort took him through.

Rudra kept his own fingers open on the chair's arm until the urge to replay the moment had passed. There was no different grip he could use now to change what had already happened.

There would have to be one he could learn before the next time.

Not tonight, in this corridor, with his hands dressed and his father behind a closed door.

---

The night thinned towards morning.

Rukmini remained under observation. Sitara brought her the updates herself, then returned with the questions she had not wanted to send through a stranger. Dadi had arrived with a neighbour and a parcel of clean clothes. When the visitors changed, she sat by Rukmini while Sitara went to ask for the next update. She had heard what happened from family, not from a stranger's account of the battle.

There had been no new opening at the ghat. Dhruv sent that once, with the word that the unsafe houses had been kept empty and the remaining injured were being found transport. He would come when the relief watch could take over.

Rudra dozed in the chair and woke each time the critical-care door moved.

Near dawn a nurse called Ram's name, then looked directly at him.

"Woh aapse milna chahte hain. Do minute. Doctor ne bola hai, thakaana nahi."

Rudra straightened without trying to stand.

This time he waited for the chair's brakes to be released.

---

The nurse asked him to clean his hands before she would take him in.

For one second Rudra looked at the dressings as though they were an answer to that. She covered what had to stay dry, showed him what he could clean, and waited while he did it without pulling at the injured skin.

A small instruction. Possible to finish.

He held to it.

Vedant took the chair as far as the marked line outside the unit. The nurse took over there. Aadhya rose, then sat again when Rudra looked back. She could not make his next two minutes easier by following him into a room which had space for one visitor.

Veer lifted his good hand an inch off the chair's arm. Ira turned her uncovered eye towards the sound of the wheels.

Rudra nodded to them all.

He had not been asked to go alone into the fighting. This was different. There were people waiting for him to come back out.

---

Ram lay a little raised against the bed support.

Oxygen ran through short tubes at his nose now. His chest was dressed on the right; a drain line led down from that side to a collection chamber below the bed. The nurse checked it before bringing Rudra's chair alongside the left side.

His father's face looked older under the flat ward light. Not changed into another man's. Simply unable to spend strength on hiding the pain from the people who knew it best.

"Do minute," the nurse repeated. "Badi baat kal."

She stayed within sight of the bed.

Rudra put his hand on the sheet where Ram could see it.

For a while they looked at each other without making the time smaller by asking how much of it was left.

Then Ram moved his fingers.

Rudra waited instead of closing his own hand round them.

The touch came to him. Weak. Deliberate. Two fingertips against the back of his hand, then the smallest curl of the bandaged palm.

He breathed out so hard that the mouth wound hurt.

"Haan," he said, though his father had not asked anything.

Ram's gaze went to the brace at the wrist. The raised ankle. Back to his face.

"Maa baat kar rahi hain," Rudra said. "Yahin hain. Shoulder ki dressing hui. Gala abhi dukh raha hai. Dadi unke paas hain."

He described the shoulder support and the way she had watched him drink the nurse's water.

Ram closed his eyes once, opened them again, and let his hand stay against Rudra's.

"Sab nikle?"

"Wahan se. Jo kamre mein the. Patient aur beta bhi."

He did not make it a claim for the whole city. His father had been there. They both knew there were other stretchers on the road.

"Machine toot gayi. Paani normal hai. Dhruv ne check kiya."

A slow blink.

"Woh aadmi nikal gaya," Rudra added. "Do log uthaake le gaye."

Ram's fingertips tightened once. The nurse checked the small changes on her monitor; Rudra kept watching the hand he could understand.

"Uske peechhe nahi gaya," Rudra said.

Ram looked at him.

"Yahan tha. Rehna tha."

His father's fingers eased against the sheet.

---

Rudra had imagined telling this story differently.

Not all night. Only in the small, shameful part of him which still believed that if he could finally do the impossible thing, his father would stand in the courtyard and say it had been enough. The iron would break. The bleeding would stop because the fight had stopped. His mother would bring water and complain that he had ruined another shirt.

The shirt was in a packet behind a desk. His mother could not lift her left arm. His father's next breath still needed help from a clear tube.

He could not ask a victory to turn into those ordinary things by wishing hard enough.

"Mujhe rokna aur seekhna hai," he said.

Ram's eyes stayed on him.

"Haath. Force. Sab. Roop tik gaya. Rokna abhi nahi aaya."

He had held the form. He had hurt a man he meant only to disarm. Both facts would have to leave the hospital with him.

Ram tried to speak. Stopped to take air.

Rudra glanced at the nurse, then back at him.

"Abhi nahi. Main yahin hoon."

Ram moved his head a fraction. No.

The small grip on Rudra's hand returned.

The nurse stepped closer. She checked his face and the breathing, then held up one finger to Rudra. A little more time, not a new permission to exhaust him.

Rudra leaned forward without pulling on the hand between them.

"Aapko laga tha yeh hone wala hai?" he asked quietly. "Aegis. Yeh sab."

Ram waited for the next breath before answering.

"Aegis was just the beginning. The one they serve... he's been waiting for thousands of years."

The room's small sounds went on: a wheel in the corridor, plastic touching the bed rail, the oxygen which did not stop for the words.

"Who?" Rudra asked.

Ram's fingers lay still against his hand.

"The one who broke God."
