# CHAPTER SIXTY-TWO — THE PATIENT IN THE BACK

---

> *"They called it the road of no Kul after the first ambulance got through.*
> *The man in the back remembered only the ceiling."*
>
> *— ambulance dutybook, account added after the northern closures*

---

The doctor asked the man at the barrier to give the phone back to the driver.

"Pehle aap bataaiye—"

"Mujhe patient ki saans sunni hai. Driver ko dijiye."

His voice did not rise. Beside the desk, the nurse had already written the location on a slip and put it in Dhruv's uninjured hand.

A scrape sounded through the speaker. The ambulance driver returned, breathing hard with anger rather than effort.

"Doctor-saab."

"Medic paas hain? Unko dijiye. Gaadi se patient ko abhi utaarna nahi."

The next voice was a woman's. She gave the patient's condition, the oxygen she was using, the change since leaving the school. The doctor listened. Asked two short questions. Told her what he wanted checked while they waited for a way through.

Rudra could not see the numbers she read from the monitor. He could hear somebody behind her trying to finish a sentence on too little air.

Then another woman said, "Bhaiya, inko pahunchaa do. Jo naam likhna hai likh lo."

The driver moved the phone closer to the medic. The plea went on, quieter now, beyond her account.

Rudra turned his head towards the curtain.

The seam caught against his collar.

---

The constable came up from the statement desk with his notebook still open. Dhruv gave him the location. He read it back, called the vehicle outside, and went down before the call ended.

Vedant rose from the chair beside the bay. The cold pack slid off its towel. He caught it against his thigh without closing the bruised arm round it.

"Tum raho," Dhruv said.

"Chal sakta hoon."

"Pata hai. Hospital waalon ko yahan bhi ek aadmi chahiye jo doosri taraf ki khabar sun chuka ho. Main pahunchkar phone karta hoon."

Vedant looked towards the desk. The nurse was asking that the main gate remain clear for the returning ambulance, not filled by people going out to watch.

He sat again.

Sitara stood at the open end of the bay until Dhruv saw her. She nodded once: the patients would not be left without someone they knew.

He checked the knot of the pouch at his waist. The angle-ring stayed inside it. Beneath the fresh dressing, his palm had begun to ache simply from the morning's walking and questions.

At the stairs he paused for a porter bringing a cylinder up. The porter thanked him, scarcely looking at his face. Dhruv waited for the load to cross the landing, then went down.

---

Veer had heard the call from the next bay.

"Kis taraf ke hain?" he asked when Vedant came back within hearing.

"Barrier waale? Abhi unse sunenge."

"Patient."

Vedant looked at him.

Veer's face changed before he answered his own question.

"Nahi. Mera matlab—"

"Pata hai," Vedant said.

He put the cold pack back over the towel. Veer stared at the ceiling for a moment, then turned his good hand palm-up on the sheet.

"Bas wahi poochh diya."

Nobody reassured him that a habit could not enter through words used by someone he hated. Aadhya, sitting across the bay while her hands were checked, asked whether the family had somebody at the school who could meet the ambulance if it had to reverse.

Vedant called the volunteer there. They needed the road behind the vehicle kept clear as well as the road in front. The school sent two people out to turn approaching handcarts back before they joined the queue.

It was a small useful task, finished before any of them had found something brave to say.

---

Outside, the afternoon had the flat white glare which makes every shutter seem hotter than the wall around it.

Dhruv followed the constable past the pharmacy and along the northern approach. The police vehicle had gone ahead by the wider road. He could hear its siren starting and stopping beyond the next row of houses.

At the tea stall a man pointed without being asked. A woman coming the other way held a schoolbag against her chest and said the van was still there.

"Aage mat jaiye," the constable told her.

"Mere pati hain usmein. Mujhe bahar bhej diya."

He stopped. Asked her name and whether she had come with the patient.

She had. The medic had needed space; men at the barrier had begun asking her questions through the open door. She had stepped down to answer them, thinking that would make the delay shorter.

Now she was on the wrong side of the crowd.

Dhruv turned his body to give her room beside him.

"Hum gaadi tak jaa rahe hain. Aap saath rahiye. Jahan bolenge wahin rukna, theek?"

She nodded, still clutching the schoolbag.

"Kapde hain," she said, seeing his glance. "Do jode."

He did not tell her she would not need them.

---

The ambulance stood behind a low farm trailer slewed across the lane. Its towbar pointed into a brick stack. A rope ran from its other end to the iron post outside a closed shop.

Enough room for a person on either side. Not enough for the van.

Three men stood by the rope. Behind the ambulance, a motorcycle and two carts had become a second obstruction. The driver had left a few feet between his bumper and the trailer, as though the small visible distance could persuade someone to clear it.

A young man at the post was writing on a board balanced against his stomach.

Across its top, in thick blue letters, was the word **PROTECTION**.

---

The constable went to the post first.

"Kiski gaadi hai? Hataao. Ambulance nikaalni hai."

The young man lowered his board. Beside him, an older man put a folded towel on the head of a boy sitting in the shade. The boy's nose was bleeding. He wore the same narrow cloth band round his upper arm as the men beside the rope.

"Hamaara bhi aadmi ghayal hai," the older man said.

"Usko bhi bhejo. Pehle rasta khol."

"Udhar Prakriti waale gaadi rok ke baithe hain. Unko khulwaaiye pehle. Rakshak ke aadmi yahan se hat gaye toh yeh poori line unki."

The constable gave the ambulance one look, took his radio out and repeated the request for the vehicle to come to the post, not stop at the wider junction.

Dhruv moved along the van's side with the patient's wife. He did not ask her to argue for the road.

The driver opened the sliding side door far enough to let him hear the medic. The air from inside smelled of vinyl, sweat and the sharp clean edge of spirit.

"Kitna time?" she asked.

"Police aa rahi hai. Main dekh raha hoon."

"Mujhe andaaza nahi chahiye. Agar gaadi nahi niklegi toh doosra tareeka dekhiye. Inko pahunchna hai."

Dhruv nodded. She let the wife speak to her husband for a few seconds, from the step, without climbing across the equipment.

"Aa rahe hain," the woman said. "Hum aa rahe hain."

The patient could not turn towards her. His eyes found the roof light, lost it, found it again.

---

At the trailer, the constable had untied the rope from the post.

One of the men caught the free end. A pale curve flashed above his wrist and stood between the constable and the trailer's handle.

"Haath peechhe," the constable said.

The curve held.

A phone rose above the crowd. Another followed it. The man with the towel looked towards the lenses, then pushed one down. Its owner recoiled; the screen struck the closed shop's shutter and cracked.

The sound brought more people to their doors.

Dhruv looked past the post. The trailer was loaded with paving blocks. Without it, the lane was wide enough. Beside it, the gap narrowed at a jutting stone doorstep, not much wider than his shoulders.

He could send a person through there. Not a stretcher kept level.

The constable spoke into his radio again. No flourish with the lathi this time. His free hand held the phones and the shield-man apart.

Behind the ambulance, a cart owner began backing his empty vehicle. The school volunteers had reached the rear of the queue. They turned the motorcycle sideways into a doorway and made another space behind it.

Dhruv went back along the van, looking at the buildings rather than the men.

"Us taraf se?" he asked the wife.

He pointed to the narrow iron gate beside the shut shop.

"Kapde dhote hain andar," she said. "Peechhe gali hai. School se aate the hum. Ab gate band hai."

"Kiske paas chaabi?"

She named the woman who lived upstairs. Someone in the watching crowd called up to her before Dhruv could ask.

A window opened. A grey head appeared above the clothesline.

"Kya?"

"Dono gate kholna hai! Mareez hai!"

The woman looked down at the trailer, at the ambulance, then at the man whose pale curve still held a policeman away from a public road.

"Aa rahi hoon," she said.

---

The medic came down to inspect the passage as soon as the first gate opened. The driver stayed with the patient. Dhruv and the woman led her through a washing courtyard striped with wet sheets.

A second iron gate stood at the far end. Beyond it, a service alley ran past the back of the pharmacy towards the hospital. The building owner unlocked it. She took two buckets out of the path, then went back for the clothesline which sagged across the centre.

"Yeh utaar do," the medic said.

"Saaf kapde hain. Upar kar dete—"

"Sir pe lagega. Poora utaariye."

The woman unknotted it without another word. Wet cotton settled in a heap on her verandah, touching the floor she had just washed.

Dhruv walked the route again, this time looking at the thresholds. At the back gate a broken drain cover left one dark opening along the side. He pointed it out to the medic. She measured the usable strip with her eyes and asked whether the hospital could meet them at the alley mouth with a trolley.

He called Vedant. Gave the exact gate and the drain, not merely the name of the road.

"Wheels ke liye nahi hai," he said. "Stretcher uthaake. Seedha."

The medic made the decision. They would transfer with the portable oxygen and a carrying team, if the hospital sent bearers to the back entrance. Not the wife, not a few excited men grabbing whichever end they reached first.

Dhruv relayed that too.

Outside the first gate the man with the board had stopped writing.

"Yeh kiska ghar hai?" he asked.

The woman came back from dropping the clothesline.

"Mera."

"Aise khol dogi toh sab aayenge."

She took the empty buckets off the step so nobody could trip on them.

"Abhi ek aayega," she said. "Usko jagah do."

From the ambulance came three quick taps. The driver wanted the medic back inside.

She went at once.

Dhruv followed her as far as the open door. The wife had stopped repeating that they were coming. Both her hands were pressed flat against the outside of the van.

---

The porters arrived through the back alley with a carrying stretcher and another attendant. They left the trolley on the hospital side of the broken cover. Dhruv went ahead of them, pointing out the opening before anyone could step beside it with a load.

At the van, the medic told the wife to wait inside the courtyard.

"Main saath—"

"Aap saath hi hain. Bas abhi haath lagaane ki jagah chahiye."

The woman went through the gate. The building owner took her schoolbag before its strap could catch the latch.

They brought the ambulance stretcher level with the carrying one. The medic counted for the transfer. Staff hands moved together. The patient's mouth opened; no full cry came out. She checked the oxygen line and put two fingers where she wanted the leading bearers to slow.

Dhruv stood back. The lifting was not his work to improve by adding an injured hand.

The man with the pale curve left the rope.

"Ruko."

The constable moved across his path.

"Mareez jaayega. Tum yahin raho."

"Yeh doosra raasta kisne khola?"

The rear bearers had already turned towards the courtyard. One had to take a half-step back to keep the stretcher level through the first gate.

The man lunged for the iron leaf.

Dhruv caught it with his left shoulder before it struck the bearer. His dressed palm hit the latch plate. Pain shot across the old rope burns.

He did not reach for the pouch.

"Andar," he said to the carrying team.

The constable caught the man's elbow. The pale curve scraped the gate, taking a thin white shaving off the paint. A second man came forward from the rope.

The building owner seized the inside handle with both hands.

"Mera gate chhod!"

A neighbour reached her from the verandah. Together they pulled the leaf towards the wall, not shut across the patient. Dhruv got his shoulder out of the pinch and stood between the hinge and the passing stretcher.

At the street's end the police vehicle turned in. Its front wheel mounted the edge of the paving to get round the waiting carts. Two more constables got out before the driver had straightened it.

The men by the post stopped advancing.

The stretcher crossed the first threshold.

---

The courtyard was not long. Under a load, every object in it acquired an edge.

A bucket hook at the height of a sleeve. A patch of soap-water near the tap. The bent foot of a washing stand. Dhruv named them as the bearers approached. The neighbour moved the stand against the wall; the building owner kicked a folded sack over the wet patch and warned the men not to put a heel on its loose end.

The medic walked at the patient's head. She did not look back at the shouting.

"Seedha. Ab dheere."

The wife went behind the oxygen, close enough to hear her husband breathe. She was no longer promising him arrival. She named the things passing above him instead.

"Kapde hain upar. Ab gate. Roshni aayegi. Aankh band kar lo."

At the rear threshold the leading porter stopped.

The drain opening lay exactly where Dhruv had said. The route had space for the stretcher only if its left edge stayed close to the gatepost. He put his uninjured hand flat against that post.

"Meri taraf. Thoda. Bas."

The porter placed his foot on sound stone. The man behind him waited until the weight settled before stepping through. Cloth whispered against brick. The oxygen cylinder cleared the latch by the width of two fingers.

Then they were in the alley.

The trolley waited at the far end. A nurse had come with it. The medic handed over while the porters made the next transfer, giving the delay along with the condition in which the man had left the school.

The wife tried to follow when the trolley began moving. The courtyard owner had come after them with her schoolbag.

Dhruv took it with his left and held it where she could reach.

She caught the strap. Went after the trolley.

There had been no moment in which the patient rose and thanked the people who had carried him. The doors took him while his wife was still walking quickly enough to lose a sandal.

A porter brought it back from the ramp.

---

By the time Dhruv returned to the first gate, the shield-man had been put beside the police vehicle. His pale edge was gone. One constable watched his hands while another asked the shopkeeper to describe what had happened.

The older man still held the towel against the boy's bleeding nose. He looked towards Dhruv's dressing, where a dark line had appeared.

"Hamaare ladke ko bhi—"

"Le chalo," Dhruv said. "Yahin hospital hai. Kisi ki parchi nahi chahiye."

The man opened his mouth, then shut it. The boy got up slowly. His cloth band stayed on the arm; no one made him remove it to be examined.

At the iron post the writing board lay face down. The cracked phone's owner stood beside the constable, angry enough for his account to need repeating. The constable let him finish before asking for the sequence again.

The police were bringing a towing vehicle for the loaded trailer. Until then, the opened courtyard could carry people in a single controlled direction, with someone at each gate.

The owner looked at the wet clothes on her floor.

"Mere bachche upar hain," she told the constable. "Aap chale gaye toh?"

He asked his senior to leave a man at the gate. When permission came, he gave her the post number and showed her which vehicle would stay.

Only then did she bend to pick up the clothes.

Dhruv helped with the part he could do one-handed. His palm dressing would need changing. The gate had a bright scrape through its paint. Neither had been the largest possible cost.

That did not make either imaginary.

---

Rudra knew the ambulance patient had arrived because the doctor left the desk in the middle of a sentence.

Not running. Moving with the particular speed which made everybody else get out of the way.

The nurse who remained brought the man's wife to a bench and asked her to wait while they examined him. She had the schoolbag again. One sandal was on crookedly.

Rudra saw her through the opening between the curtain and its rail.

He had once believed the worst part of bringing someone to a hospital was being stopped outside a door. He could picture another door now, in a washing courtyard, which a woman had needed permission from nobody to open and protection from somebody to keep open afterwards.

Vedant came to his bedside.

"Aa gaye. Treatment shuru hai."

"Saans?"

"Oxygen pe hain. Doctor andar hain."

Rudra let the answer be as small as it was. Across the passage the wife folded and unfolded the loose end of her bag's strap.

"Unko pata hai?" he asked.

"Wahi bataaya hai."

The woman looked towards the treatment doors. Rudra looked away before she had to acknowledge another stranger watching her wait.

---

Dhruv came back with his palm redressed and a warning not to lean on it again. The angle-ring had stayed in the pouch throughout. He set the pouch on the chair beside him rather than wearing its cord against the new padding.

Veer studied the thicker dressing.

"Gate ne kaata?"

"Latch ne."

"Complaint gate ko deni padegi. Latch toh employee hai."

Dhruv looked at him until the corner of his mouth moved.

"Tumhara review kitne din chalega?"

"Jab tak discharge na kar dein."

Veer's mother, returning with water, said she could arrange an early end to the commentary without troubling any doctor. He accepted the glass in a chastened silence which lasted until she had turned away.

Aadhya smiled. Then her right hand slipped a little on the pillow, and she had to ask for it to be placed back properly. The numb fingers had not noticed the edge.

The smile faded while she watched the nurse protect them from a pressure she could not feel.

"Kal bhi yeh covered rahega?" she asked.

"Doctor dekhenge. Feeling aa jaaye tab bhi jaldi kaam mat shuru kar dena."

Her mother was coming for the evening review. Aadhya had asked for an ordinary change of clothes, loose sleeves, nothing which had to be pulled hard over the hands.

Not a uniform. Not clothes in which somebody could photograph a champion leaving for her own territory.

She asked Vedant to tell the desk that only her family should be told where she would rest after the doctor permitted her to leave. Anyone claiming to have sent a vehicle could wait outside.

He took the request to the nurse rather than promising to guard every gate himself.

---

The recorder-lady arrived near the end of the afternoon.

Ira heard her before opening her eye: the familiar small clearing of the throat, never quite a cough, with which she had announced herself at a desk when she thought its occupant had been sitting too long.

"Kya laayi?" Ira asked.

"Khaana."

"Aur?"

"Chamach."

Ira opened the uncovered eye. The older woman stood between her and the strongest light until Sitara adjusted the curtain.

"Kaam nahi laayi?"

"Tumhe dekhne aayi hoon. Office kholne nahi."

The food went on the little table. Sitara had already checked what Ira could eat. The recorder-lady placed the cup on her right, described where it stood, and waited for her to find it herself.

Ira's fingers trembled round the handle. She put the cup down before lifting it.

The older woman slid a folded towel beneath it so it would not scrape away while Ira changed her grip.

"Main de doon?"

"Nahi. Bas paas rakhna."

"Yahin hoon."

For a while their conversation concerned the thickness of dal. Too thick to drink, too thin to accuse anyone of having cooked a proper meal. Ira argued that the hospital had probably commissioned it to prevent patients from staying for the food. The recorder-lady told her to complain softly; the cook might take a complaint as an invitation to add more water.

Ira laughed once. It hurt behind the dressing. She let the older woman move the bowl closer and stopped trying to turn her head towards every sound.

Rudra heard none of the joke clearly from his bed. He heard the change in her voice.

There had been a chair at Rajghat which she had chosen to stand beside. There were people who came when she was hurt. No one at a checkpoint had the authority to make those things temporary.

---

The school called before evening.

A small delivery had reached it by the checked approach, with a police escort for the last stretch. Not the full flour consignment. Enough food for the people who had begun stretching their dry stores into another meal.

Vedant gave that news to the group without calling the roads safe.

The northern trailer had been moved. A constable stayed at the courtyard gate until the owner could secure it for the night. Elsewhere, another driver had been turned away from a lane whose entrance had been open an hour before.

Rudra asked for a fresh update to go to the critical-care desk as well. His father did not need to be moved; the people going home after duty did.

He was thinking of the nurse who had waited while he cleaned his hands before a visit. She would have to leave this building eventually. So would the man who had taken Ram's chest image. So would the porter still bringing cylinders up a stair.

The hospital was not an island merely because he had learned to remain inside it.

---

The man with the reopened stitches returned to the visitors' seats after his statement.

He did not ask for Veer to be brought out again. He gave the guard a folded receipt and asked that Vedant look at it.

"Flour waale ka hai," he said. "Jo gaadi mein bacha hai, woh bhi chala gaya toh kal mujhe jawaab dena padega."

Vedant met him at the recess, leaving the bays quiet. Sitara stayed where she could hear the patients if they called. The recorder-lady came to the clinical door with Ira's permission to hear anything concerning the kitchen records.

The receipt had been kept in a plastic sleeve. Its corners were worn, but the amount and the words *paid in full* were clear. There was a dispatch number beneath them and a promised collection at the grain-market warehouse.

"Kisne paise diye?" Vedant asked.

"Humne jama kiye. Chaar ghar se. Rasad ke liye. Hamaare log hain school mein bhi."

"Naam ki jagah Kul kyun poochh rahe the phir?"

The man looked towards his freshly wrapped forearm.

"Pehle chori hui thi. Bola tha, maal uth gaya. Doosri taraf pahunchaa diya."

"Kisne bola?"

"Jahan se khareeda."

He tapped the seller's line on the receipt.

Vedant asked the constable to hear the account. A piece of paper presented after an assault could be evidence of a transaction; it could not turn the assault into an official duty.

The constable wrote the man's statement in his own notebook and asked him to identify the men who had stood at the cart. The visitor named two. When asked who had struck the first relief driver, he said he had not seen that blow.

His account stopped there. Nobody filled the gap for him.

---

At the clinical door the recorder-lady asked whether the school kitchen had kept its purchase note.

Vedant showed her the copy sent by the cook. The original was with the driver, who had been told to keep it dry. The surviving flour sacks were still awaiting a documented move from the carrier; police would not let another group simply take them while arguing about yesterday's losses.

She studied the note without announcing a verdict.

"Transport waale ki kitaab dekhni padegi. Aur bechne waale ki. Yeh alag kaagaz hain."

Ira called softly from the bay. The older woman went back at once.

"Kya hua?"

"Aawaaz aa rahi thi."

"Isliye khadi thi darwaaze pe. Tumhe uthna na pade."

Ira asked what the receipt actually said. The recorder-lady read only the relevant lines. No one put the small print in front of an injured eye and asked for a miracle.

"Maal diya, yeh likha hai?" Ira asked.

"Abhi is copy mein paise liye likha hai. Dispatch number bhi hai."

"Toh number ka matlab poochhna. Apne aap gaadi mat maan lena."

The older woman looked at her over the paper.

"Wahi karungi."

Ira lay back. Her right eye shut before she finished asking when the doctor would come again.

"Shaam ka review abhi hai," Sitara said. "Kal ka kal dekhenge."

---

Aadhya's mother brought the loose-sleeved clothes. She also brought a comb and asked which way her daughter wanted her hair tied.

Aadhya said the usual way.

Halfway through, she asked for it to be looser.

Her mother undid the tie without saying it had always been comfortable before. The hands which normally did it were lying on the pillow between them.

The doctor examined those hands again that evening. There was no sudden return of sensation. He described how the dressings would be managed outside observation if the next check allowed it, what help she would need, when to return. Permission to rest elsewhere would not mean permission to put the injured fingers to work.

Ira's eye team would examine her again in the morning. Rudra still had no weight-bearing clearance. Veer was to leave only with family help after his remaining checks, not walk out because a better rumour was waiting outside.

The ordinary future returned in pieces: a place to sleep, a lift to arrange, clothes with sleeves which would pass over a dressing.

Rudra heard his mother's bay settling for the evening. Ram remained in critical care. The new patient from the school was still being treated; his wife had been given an update and somewhere to sit.

A whole day had passed without making either man's next day certain.

---

Just before the visiting queue thinned, the guard brought another envelope to Vedant.

A woman from the seed-shop group had left it. The recorder-lady opened it with him at the visitors' recess, beside the constable who had returned for the first receipt's details.

Inside was a second paid claim for flour.

Different purchaser. Another collection amount. The same warehouse seller's stamp.

Vedant put the plastic-sleeved receipt beside it.

The recorder-lady ran one finger down each page, stopping below the words *paid in full*.

"Number ek hai," she said.

The constable turned his notebook back to the visitor's account.

From inside the bay came the small sound of Ira asking for the light to be lowered. The older woman waited for Sitara to answer, then looked again at the two receipts.

Both sides were claiming to have paid for the same promised flour.

Only one load had reached the crossing.
