# CHAPTER SEVENTY-SIX — THE SPACE INSIDE THE GUARD

---

> *"The crowd remembered the shield which stopped a hand.*
> *The losing corner remembered the shield which was not there.*
> *Neither account could put the earth an inch nearer."*
>
> *— notes from the tournament's second evening, copied after the final*

---

Green put the foot down beyond the chalk.

She took the lost point instead of putting her dressed palm beneath her weight.

The referee stopped them. Yellow had stayed inside the boundary; the line judge showed the clear space between his foot and the outside. There had been no final shoulder touch to argue over.

Two–one, yellow.

Green stood with the good hand open and the other held clear. White came as far as the allowed corner mark with her water. He did not ask why she had failed to save a point which saving might have made much more expensive.

The clinician asked whether the hand had taken any weight. It had not. Green showed the movement she had stopped, without doing the fall again. The referee waited until the corner and clinician had finished before bringing either contestant back.

---

At the post, Veer heard the result through a burst of wind across the caller's microphone.

He asked him to say it again.

"Do–ek. Peela. Pair baahar."

"Haath?"

"Neeche nahi rakha. Check ho gaya."

Veer let his own shoulders settle. He had wanted her to win. He had wanted, with the greedy certainty of somebody watching from safety, the free hand to find a way to do everything two healthy hands could not always do.

He could want it without making it her obligation.

Through the phone, somebody shouted that the green corner should change its contestant if it had come to protect a bandage.

White's answer reached the microphone more clearly than the shout.

"Match iska hai. Tumhari kalpana ka nahi."

The referee moved the loud man back from the rope. Green drank, gave the bottle to her corner and returned to the mark herself.

---

She began the next exchange without searching for the grass.

Yellow held the pale curve close again. The forearm behind it had begun to shake on the long hold. Green waited long enough to see that much, then moved towards the shoulder which was not covered.

He gave ground, but not the straight retreat which had taken her to the chalk.

His back foot turned inward. He stayed nearer the centre.

She checked her own step before following. The corner White had watched her lose twice was behind Yellow now, safely farther away. There was room for a second attempt if the first did not work.

Her good hand went towards the tab.

The guard turned.

She did not press into it. She withdrew the hand and passed to the other side, trying to bring him round after the shoulder she had already left.

He let the guard go.

This time she expected the absence. The free hand stopped where she meant it to stop. Her feet stayed under her.

Yellow had not offered the old trick again.

He stepped towards the space her change of sides had opened and touched the tab beside her dressed arm before she could bring that shoulder away.

The referee's signal came with his fingers still flat against the cloth.

Three–one.

---

Green looked first at the hand, then at the official.

She had felt the touch. She nodded.

Yellow stepped back. The curve was gone, and he did not summon it for the announcement. He rubbed the holding forearm with the other hand until the medical helper asked him to leave it alone for the examination.

The woman went to her corner with both feet steady.

She had lost. She had not needed to be carried out to prove how much she wanted to stay.

White offered the water and said nothing. After a moment she took it.

"Aakhri mein samajh aa gaya," she said.

He waited.

"Usko bhi."

The man across the ring was smiling at something his corner had said. He had changed his answer when she learned the first one. That was allowed. It was why an opponent was more than an exercise set up for her improvement.

She drank before the disappointment could dry her mouth completely.

---

The referee announced the result after the medical checks.

Yellow's movement to the later round was entered beside his number. The next appearance would still depend on the next examination. Green received the payment for the bout she had actually completed, not the winner's addition.

She asked the steward to count it back once. Her good fingers were tired, and she preferred an honest second count to pretending they were not.

White held the open bag while she put the money away. She told him to leave the water where she could reach it herself.

At the public side, the caller told Veer the final score.

"Teen–ek."

Veer thanked him and let the promised one-match call end. There were other people in the club who might want the phone; the man holding it had a throat which needed water too.

For a little while, the post room was quiet enough that Veer could hear the kitchen worker scraping the last rice out of a pot.

---

Rudra got the result after his evening examination.

Veer gave him the score first, then separated what the caller had described from the little he had heard for himself. The break in the sound had swallowed half an exchange. He did not fill it with a hand he wished the woman had used.

"Tu hota toh?" Rudra asked.

"Main nahi tha."

"Maloom hai. Bas—"

"Main hota toh bhi uska haath uska hota."

There was no sharpness in it. Veer sounded more tired than wise. He had spent the morning wanting to stand, the afternoon wanting someone else to find a way around a limit and the last few minutes discovering that wanting was not always a plan.

Rudra looked at the wrist resting beside him.

"Jeetne waale ka naam?"

"Number suna tha. Naam nahi maanga. Club Rakshak."

"Khush ho?"

Veer considered it.

"Match dekhna chahiye tha. Result ka kya karun akela?"

Rudra understood the answer. A victory could become another wall if all one knew of the person carrying it was which side he had been born on.

He asked Veer to tell him the first exchange again, the one where no guard had appeared. This time, they let the unknown movements remain unknown.

---

In Mirzapur, the girls heard the same final score later.

Ira had finished the prescribed period of ordinary reading before the message came. The lamp was low, the left eye still covered. Aadhya asked for the spoken account to be replayed once and then let the phone go back to the person holding it.

"Bina zameen chhuye kheli?" she asked.

Only as far as the caller had actually reported. Nobody in this room had watched the whole bout.

Aadhya thought of the palm she had once put down without thinking of how much of her body would have to answer through it. She kept the supported left hand still and asked whether Green had chosen to stop after losing.

The bracket had stopped her. She had then chosen to leave with the payment and her hand protected. Both parts belonged in the answer.

"Kal uth ke phir kuchh aur kar sakti hai," Ira said.

"Haan."

Aadhya did not mean another match. There was comfort in knowing somebody could leave a story before the crowd had finished deciding what her pain ought to mean.

Sitara brought the next clinic time, then went to eat. The girls did not turn the message into a lesson they had to perform before she returned.

---

Rukmini's call reached Rudra while the volunteer was finding the clean shirt from his bag.

She asked which one had been washed. He told her the colour and got it wrong according to Dadi, who was certain that the shirt he meant had never been blue.

"Phone pe rang mat decide karo," Rukmini said.

Rudra could hear the little scrape of a chair behind her voice. They were in the ward visitors' space. Ram's care continued; he had rested after the evening check, and there was no reason to make him speak merely because Rudra could.

The clean shirt was white after all, with a narrow blue check which Rudra had been trying to use as a defence.

His mother asked whether the neck opened far enough to change it without pulling at the supported wrist.

The nurse would help. They had already asked.

"Button tod diya toh?" he said.

"Kapde ka hai. Badal denge."

She was quiet for a moment after that. Rudra watched the volunteer lay the shirt out on the chair, and neither of them tried to finish the sentence which had become too large for a button.

They agreed on the next ordinary update. She gave the phone to Dadi long enough for a complaint about the laundry and took it back before the conversation tired him.

---

The nurse came after the call.

She helped Rudra change with the supported wrist kept clear and the ankle still resting where it should. The skin above the collarbone was closed. The room smelled faintly of soap from the basin beyond the curtain.

Vedant arrived for the arranged visit as the nurse was settling the clean collar. He put the bag down with his good hand and asked whether the family had got the update.

Rudra began to answer.

Warmth moved beneath the cloth.

He stopped and told them immediately.

The nurse left the collar loose, checked him and called the doctor. Vedant brought the mirror only when Rudra could look without turning against the support.

Gold had appeared along part of the familiar outline. The small patch within the joined lines remained dark.

At its lower edge, a thin dark point lengthened into the gold.

Rudra watched its tip move a little farther before it stopped.

It had not reached the rest of the broken outline. The surrounding light held. His foot stayed exactly as heavy on the support as it had been before he changed his shirt.

---

The doctor arrived while the outline was still visible.

He did not need the account improved by another attempt. He examined Rudra, checked the ordinary observations and kept the wrist and ankle supported while the nurse settled the shirt clear of the warm area.

There was still no open skin for the new line to follow.

Rudra answered about pain, breathing and faintness. Vedant described what he had seen: a short dark tip extending from the existing patch while the gold around it remained. He showed the doctor the older drawing and left the new mark for the drawing made tonight.

The nurse gave the time at which Rudra had reported the warmth. The time belonged to the report. It was not silently made into the moment every earlier change must have begun.

The doctor arranged a senior review and continued the staffed observation. He wanted the first team contacted with this new event, not only the admission note they had already received.

"Kapda badalne se hua?" Rudra asked.

"Us waqt dikha," the doctor said. "Abhi usse wajah nahi bolenge."

That answer offered him neither a movement to fear for the rest of his life nor a clever movement to repeat in order to understand it.

---

The light faded before the new drawing was finished.

They recorded where the little offshoot had stopped while it was still visible. Vedant did not carry the pencil farther to make the uneven outline neat. The missing patch remained one small patch; the new line did not encircle the chest or run through every surviving piece of gold.

No one had a fragment to put in a box.

The skin was closed when the collar was settled again. The nurse stayed through the next observations. Rudra could feel the ordinary tiredness of the long day and an entirely different fear which had no interest in how ordinary the room looked.

He wanted to ask what happened when there was no more gold left to hold.

He asked it.

Vedant did not answer with a count.

"Jo aaj dekha, usko chhupaayenge nahi," he said. "Jawaab bana ke tumse karaayenge bhi nahi."

Rudra stared at him, angry for one breath at being given so little, then exhausted by the knowledge that a larger lie would have been easier to say.

He asked for the family to be told now, not after everybody had had a chance to make their voices sound calm.

---

Rukmini took the update in the ward visitors' space.

The doctor spoke to her with Rudra's agreement. A new visible change, actual assessment, observation continuing, further review requested. The ordinary checks did not explain the light. Nobody was calling its disappearance a repaired seal.

She asked the practical questions slowly enough to hear the answers.

Then she put the phone down beside her good hand.

Dadi brought the water closer. Rukmini drank from it before asking what the next call should be. Her supported shoulder had begun to ache; she had been holding herself too still to notice.

For a while, she watched the closed door through which the nurse had gone to Ram. Her pulse beat against the base of her thumb. She left the hand on the table and waited for it to stop shaking enough to lift the glass safely.

She did not call her son back to make him tell her not to be afraid.

Dhruv came when the staff were ready to discuss what information the hometown team could send. He took that task. Dadi kept the glass within reach. There were things a mother could not do from another city, and there were living people beside her who would not let that fact make her do everything else alone.

---

Sitara got the same observed account in Mirzapur.

She asked whether it had required a deliberate form. It had not. She asked what had been checked and who was with Rudra now. Vedant gave the handover without giving the dark tip a cause he could not establish.

The girls were told after their current rest interval, with the actual plan beside the frightening part. Ira asked for the drawing to wait until her next ordinary reading interval. Right now, she wanted to hear what the doctor had said.

Aadhya asked whether Rudra wanted a call before sleep. He did, briefly.

They spoke about the shirt. She told him the new one had better survive long enough for somebody to prove its colour in daylight. He said Dadi had already won that argument from Varanasi.

Neither of them pretended the joke was the answer.

When the nurse returned, Aadhya let the call end at once.

---

Vedant handed over before leaving the bay for his own meal and rest.

The nurse had the new report and the contacts. The senior review had been requested. Rudra was not being sent back to the post because the gold had stopped being visible.

He slept in intervals that night, waking to the room rather than a command, and each time a member of the ward staff answered before he had to decide whether he should bear a new fear quietly.

---

The following day was a full rest day for Veer.

Other qualifying bouts continued at the akhada. He did not go to watch them, and the club's caller was not asked to remain on the phone until every empty hour at the post had been filled.

The clinician saw him in the morning and again after the prescribed movement. Ordinary shoulder motion was easier than it had been a week earlier. The knee still disliked an impatient turn. Better in one place did not cancel the account in another.

A further short spectator session could be considered after another night and the next morning's review. Veer asked for it, made the return arrangement conditional on that decision and spent the rest of the day doing something he had never thought he would need to practise: letting an interesting place continue without him.

The kitchen worker brought lunch before he had worked out whether he was succeeding.

---

Green went to her dressing check that morning, then to the bus stop with White.

Her hand still needed protection. She had no further place in the bracket. Those were different facts, even if people kept asking whether the first had caused all of the second.

A boy from their club offered to put up the picture from her winning touch. She asked him to put up the score from the later bout with it. Not a photograph of her dressing. The result.

"Dono?" he asked.

"Tournament aadha nahi khela."

He nodded and went to find the other entry number.

White carried her heavier bag until the bus arrived. She bought the tickets with the money she had actually received, keeping enough separate for the return clinic visit. Losing had not made that money imaginary or made it last forever.

Before climbing aboard, she held out the pickle compartment she had forgotten to return.

White stared at it.

"Yeh jeet ke liye rakha tha?"

"Ismein toh tum pehle hi jeet gaye the."

The driver asked them to let the other passengers board. They stopped being a corner and a contestant for long enough to become two people getting in the way of a bus.

---

In the hospital, the senior review took place with the actual observations and the first team's information available.

There had been no further visible flare during the next checks. The offshoot had not been summoned again to see whether it would lengthen. The doctors could examine the body in front of them; they could not promise what the missing gold would do because a quiet morning had followed it.

The plan kept Rudra under staffed observation while the further assessment continued. It allowed the prescribed movements for his existing injuries, with support. It did not add a journey to the akhada or a trial form to the day's work.

Rudra asked the senior doctor to tell him what would change the plan and whom to call before waiting for the next scheduled round. The nurse went over that part with him afterward. Asking for an answer he could use today felt less like surrender than searching his own chest for one he could not prove.

When Ram was awake and the hometown nurse agreed, they had a short call.

His father had heard the new account. He asked whether Rudra had been alone when the warmth began.

"Nurse thi. Vedant bhi aa gaye the."

Ram closed his eyes briefly on the other end. Rukmini repeated the practical plan, not to correct Rudra but to make sure both patients had heard the same thing.

Ram asked whether he had eaten. Rudra began to answer about the examination, then realized his father meant dinner.

He told him the rice had gone cold while he was waiting. The nurse had brought another serving.

"Theek," Ram said.

He sounded tired, and relieved by an answer small enough to believe. Rudra let him rest on that one.

---

By evening, the akhada had the names for the later bracket.

Yellow had his place. A Prakriti club representative with a close-cropped beard would meet him in the first semifinal. The other half would be decided on its own side of the programme. The featured rounds would run to five points, with scheduled medical pauses and the same restrictions on targets and full forms.

The steward sent the schedule, not a demand that Veer be present to make it important.

He read it after the clinician had finished with the knee and left it beside the return-vehicle number. If the next morning's answer was no, the bout would still have two contestants.

---

In Mirzapur, Sitara had the photographed drawing printed at the clinic. Ira listened to the new clinical update, then used one short right-eye interval to look at that ordinary copy with her.

The earlier patch. The small new tip. No one had drawn the absent rest of an answer around them.

Ira stopped when the interval ended. She gave the sheet back rather than holding it until the lines seemed to move for reasons her tired eye might supply.

Aadhya asked whether it had frightened her.

"Haan."

"Mujhe bhi."

They let those two small answers stand without adding a plan which required either of them to hurt themselves. The next family update had a time. The next clinic task had a person to do it.

For the rest of the evening, they listened to the woman downstairs trying to persuade a child that the best part of a mango was not whichever part somebody else was eating.

---

The next morning's review permitted Veer another short spectator session.

The knee had settled with the full rest day. The shoulder still needed its support. The clinician kept the same essential limits and shortened the interval to fit the bout he had actually asked to see, not the whole later programme. A post volunteer would accompany him; the return vehicle would wait at the outer entrance again.

Veer gave the departure time to the driver himself.

Vedant would visit Rudra at the hospital. The ward had the new notes and the hometown contacts. Nobody had to carry two injured young men between rooms simply to make the day's arrangements look like a reunion.

Before leaving, Veer sent Rudra a message asking whether he wanted the score afterward or the story when he had time for it.

*Story,* came the answer. *Score se kal kya mila tha?*

Veer smiled and put the phone away.

---

At the akhada, water ran down the leg of a freshly wiped scoring board.

The first round's names and scores had been copied before the surface was cleared. The small damaged stretch of grass remained marked off. The grounds workers had not made the flattened runner stand again for a better photograph; they had inspected the remaining stretch and left the excluded part out of the usable surface.

Veer's chair stood at the same end of the verandah. The volunteer checked the knee cloth and the side passage while the driver confirmed where he would wait.

Below, Yellow was walking the ring with the referee and his opponent.

The Prakriti representative was broader through the waist than either of the men Veer had watched first. His beard was close-cropped. He crouched by the permitted grass strip, examined the earth without moving it and stood with one hand briefly braced on his own thigh.

Then he walked back to the centre under his own ordinary balance.

The announcer gave both entry numbers. The crowd supplied the Kuls louder than the names.

Veer watched the feet.

---

The referee went through the featured-round rules with both men.

Five points. The same shoulder targets and whole-foot boundary. No face, throat or eyes. No full forms. No shape or root held through a stop. Medical pauses at the stated intervals or whenever needed; either man could concede.

They both answered. Their corners heard.

The medical official confirmed the current release for each contestant before the referee brought them to the marks. The bearded man flexed his fingers once. Yellow rolled neither shoulder for the crowd; he had already warmed up at his own corner.

A man near the verandah asked Veer whether the Rakshak contestant would win this one too.

"Dekhne aaye hain," Veer said.

He said it with enough pleasure that it did not sound like an excuse.

---

The bearded contender let Yellow make the first approach.

His leading foot stayed on the packed earth, the rear one nearer the grass. He kept the palm close enough to the ground for the possibility to be visible, not hidden beneath a promise that this would be an ordinary shoulder game.

Yellow advanced with the little curve held low and close.

Two steps. A pause.

The Prakriti man gave him half a step towards the strip.

Yellow did not put his heel where the runner might tighten. He changed the angle, trying to make the man lift the working palm in order to keep the shoulder tab out of reach.

The palm stayed down.

At the front edge of Yellow's boot, a living runner rose a few fingers from the earth. Not a wall. Not a new length pouring out of the soil. The existing strand had been drawn into the short space his next step would need.

Yellow checked the foot before it came down.

The lower edge of his guard turned towards the runner. He lowered his centre, using the little curve to move the strand aside rather than putting the boot into it.

Veer saw the forearm angle change.

He had watched two people wait for that movement in different ways. The memory made him lean a fraction forward before the supported knee reminded him to leave the rest of his weight where it was.

---

The bearded man's working fingers pressed into the soil.

The runner slackened.

Yellow began the sideways movement which would carry the guard clear. The strand fell across its low face, almost at earth level. For an instant it looked like the harmless thing he had just moved out of the way.

Then the root drew tight beneath the lower rim.

Yellow's hand stopped short of the turn he had planned.

It had not caught his boot. Both feet were free. The curve he was holding had become the thing connecting his next movement to the palm still planted beside the grass.

The planted palm had kept the bearded man beside the source. Until now, Yellow had stayed just beyond the reach of the free hand. Lowering the guard had brought the shoulder forward; stopping the turn had left it there.

The Prakriti man's other hand came up towards the shoulder tab above that lowered arm.

Yellow could keep the guard or let it go.

The other man's fingers were almost at his shoulder.
