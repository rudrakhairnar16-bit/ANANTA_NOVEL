# CHAPTER SEVENTY-TWO — THE WORK NO ONE OWED

---

> *"No household was required to supply a replacement.*
> *For three mornings, that sentence cost more than the grain."*
>
> *— receiving-post account, transport expenses entered separately*

---

The cook took the first pot back inside.

She did not leave it on the trolley to cool while the organizer tried to persuade a frightened man that a different route would change his mind. The hall had to be told what could not reach it. The food here could go to people here while it was still fit to serve.

The kitchen helper stopped with his hands on the trolley.

"Hall mein kya boloon?"

"Sach. Aur poochho unke paas abhi kitna hai."

At the other end of that call, the hall's cook opened the store with the receiving worker beside her. She could begin a smaller meal from the remaining dry stock. It would use what she had hoped to keep for evening.

The organizer wrote that loss of margin down before calling the next transport number.

Three collection places had survived one blocked gate. They had not become places which could survive forever without the people moving between them.

---

The medical service found a car for the foot-injured yard worker.

It would take him to the assessment and back with an attendant. It would not become a food van first because the kitchen's trolley stood nearer the door.

The worker's replacement asked whether the cost would come out of his injured colleague's wage. The organizer put the medical transport entry where both could see it, separate from the pay for completed work.

"Nahi. Iska alag hisaab hai."

The car arrived late enough that the receiving clinic had to be called about the appointment. The clinic adjusted the time. Nobody sent the worker home to return another day merely because a privately hired driver had withdrawn from a relief run.

By then, the hall's first smaller pot was on its stove.

---

The bus-stand dispatcher offered a possibility, not a rescue fleet.

Two regular services had approved space for small sealed consignments. There were limits on the weight, the storage and what could travel with passengers. The clean compartment had to be checked. The post's worker would pay the carriage and send a named receiver to the upper stop; a bus would not leave its route to turn into the hall's lane.

The organizer went with the kitchen worker to see the space before agreeing.

They sent dry stock, properly packed, not the cooked meal which had already been reassigned. Passengers kept their seats. A parcel which did not fit stayed behind. The dispatcher would not turn a public service into a different kind of commandeered vehicle because last night's passengers had watched the police stop one.

At the upper stop, two paid replacement porters met the consignment with the hall's trolley. The men hurt at the dealer's yard were still off that work.

The distance from the stop to the hall looked short on the route sheet. On the paving it took two journeys, a rest and help over a broken edge which the smaller wheels could not cross loaded.

A woman waiting at the hall came out to steady the empty trolley while the porters moved the first load in.

She offered that much. When her father called from inside, she went back to him. Nobody called after her that an extra pair of hands was still needed.

---

The first bus consignment reached the hall before the evening stock was gone.

The cook did not smile until she had opened it. She checked the contents, the quantity and what had been promised elsewhere. Then she began replacing the share she had borrowed from the later meal.

The carriage cost more than a full vehicle divided across its old planned load. The porters' time cost money. The broken paving had cost another half-hour, and the bus would not wait at the stop every morning while a receiver was found.

The kitchen worker stayed to write the actual route and timing for the next shift.

"Roz karenge?" one porter asked.

"Tum bolo pehle."

He would take two mornings. After that he had other paid work, and a child to collect on the third.

His partner could take one of those mornings and the following evening. The receiving worker wrote the times they offered, then left the rest of the page blank.

Nobody filled the empty spaces with the names of their brothers.

---

That afternoon a driver came to the post and asked about the run he had refused.

He had not changed his mind about today. He wanted to know whether refusing it had cost him every later job.

The organizer showed him the cancelled entry and the payment for the runs already done. Both remained ordinary records of work, not a list of loyal and disloyal households.

The driver sat down.

"Kal ka route dikhaao," he said. "Haan abhi nahi bol raha. Pehle dekhunga."

The organizer turned the sheet towards him.

Outside, the trolley's parking space was still empty. For the first time that morning, the empty space did not mean nobody was moving food.

---

Over the next seven days, the transport sheet kept changing every morning. Sometimes it changed between two calls.

On the second morning, the driver who had asked to see the route accepted one run with the current public contact and a return time written into it. He did not take the next one. The hall used the bus consignment and the trolley again instead of treating his first yes as something which could be inherited by every later shift.

On the third, a kitchen which had lent grain received part of it back. The cook there checked the share before putting it into her own store. She still needed the remainder. The post's organizer left that debt on the page where the next worker would see it.

On the fourth, the woman from the boat brought her father home.

---

She had spoken to the clinician about the journey and arranged help at both ends. Her relative's bruised arm was improving, but he was not the lifting plan. A local vehicle took the old man along the checked longer road. The broken foot crossing stayed closed.

The child watched it through the window from well above the landing.

"Woh theek nahi hua?"

"Nahi. Isliye iss raaste se jaa rahe hain."

At the common gate, the caretaker met them with the helper. The entrance shift was a residents' arrangement with a police contact, not a private pledge of household labour. People could pay or volunteer for agreed work. Nobody's room key had become security for somebody else's night away.

Her father needed a pause before the last few steps. He was irritated at the pause and the people politely pretending not to notice it. She let him be irritated while the chair was brought closer.

Inside, she put the medicine tin back on its shelf. The child's sandals went beside the cot. Her own phone charged where she could reach it without leaving the room.

The municipal hall kept the departure recorded as a chosen return to the existing address. Another family took the space they had used. Their mat did not have to stay empty for the story of their rescue to remain true.

That night she called the hall worker herself and said they were inside, the entrance had stayed staffed and her father had found the right comb unsatisfactory after all.

He had wanted it for so long that the teeth now seemed too far apart.

---

The inspection report on the crossing came that week.

The far joining section had failed where its support timber had deteriorated around a fastening. The technical team had recovered damaged wood and part of the connection. A remaining piece had not been found. They could not turn a missing piece into proof of who had handled it last, or certify the whole event from that one damaged joint.

The near joining boards had slewed after the crowd changed direction. That movement had its own witnesses and damage. The technical team had found no cut there.

The post displayed the closure and repair instructions, not photographs of the families.

When a man said this meant the private guards had done nothing, the worker who had watched the rope come down pointed to the separate accounts.

People had still been stopped from stepping onto a bank. A man had still followed a family to treatment and swung a staff. The technical finding did not erase what people had seen, any more than the witnessed assault proved a hand had cut the far connection.

The woman read the short public summary later with the caretaker.

She asked when the crossing would reopen.

There was no safe date to give her yet.

She folded the notice about the longer route and kept it beside the phone.

---

In Mirzapur, the worker with the disputed watch deduction received a response at the time written on her copy.

Part of the amount had been restored. The remaining part was still being pursued from the people who had taken it. The account worker brought the money and the revised entry to her, rather than sending a message that she should leave her shift and come looking for it.

She counted it herself. Then she asked what would happen at the next wage issue.

The cooperative had stopped labelling the private deduction voluntary. It was not collecting another one through the same pay envelope. The unresolved claims stayed with the affected workers, who could choose whether their accounts were shared for the wider complaint.

That did not make the damaged cloth saleable. The customer accepted the undamaged part after inspection and granted time on the rest. The cooperative lost money on the sorting and the smaller delivery. Repairing the frame took paid hours from another task.

The kitchen women kept their meal arrangement because it spared families the loading court, not because they had acquired the right to decide the cooperative's wages.

Aadhya heard the first resumed packing shift from the clinic window. Ira asked the runner who was meeting the departing vehicle and whether the workers had been told the actual revised payment date.

They had.

The answer let her close her eye and rest. She did not need to stand beside the vehicle to make the departure count as work the team had helped defend.

---

At the end of that week, Rudra walked to the examination room's window and back with the physiotherapist beside him.

The distance was ordinary enough to be insulting until he reached the turn.

His ankle had tolerated more controlled loading over the intervening days. The adapted support still protected the wrist. At the window he began to pivot, remembered the instruction and moved through the smaller turn she had shown him instead.

The return hurt less than the first attempt after Varanasi. It still hurt.

"Distance badha hai," the physiotherapist said when he sat. "Permission ka matlab nahi badla."

Travel with support and rests, short seated tasks, prescribed movement. Not running, fighting or finding out how much a frightened crowd might make him forget.

Veer had his own reassessment. Some ordinary shoulder movement was easier, with the sling used as instructed rather than as a badge he could discard. His knee still caught after too long in one position. The physiotherapist changed the timing of the movements and the rest between them. She did not invite him to discover a stance which made pain irrelevant.

They left with small, separate improvements. Neither had the other's clearance.

---

The collar dressing came off after the clinician had checked the closed surface.

The skin was no longer open. Rudra turned his head carefully while the place was cleaned and inspected. A brief tightening across the chest brought gold into view above the loose collar.

Vedant held the mirror where Rudra could see without twisting farther.

The branch they had drawn after the assault had not disappeared. Its end now reached nearer the older broken line. A narrow strip still separated them, thin enough that Rudra had to wait for the reflection to settle before he could be certain it remained.

He did not try to make the light brighter.

"Aaj hua?" he asked.

"Aaj dekha," Vedant said. "Kab badha, uska record nahi hai."

The clinician finished examining the healed skin. Vedant marked the changed outline on a separate sketch, beside the earlier one. The two accounts were kept distinct. No ruler had been laid against the light; no measurement would be invented to make the fear look precise.

Rudra had not made a full form during those days. He had not trained through the night's call, fought at the market or tested the old count when nobody was watching.

That did not establish what had carried the damage farther. It did establish that he could not use the absence of another battle as proof that it had repaired itself.

He remembered wanting every visible wound to close so he could stop being a patient. Now the most reassuring part of the examination was the part least able to answer this other question.

"Dono jud gaye toh?" he asked.

Vedant looked at the two broken ends.

"Nahi pata. Isliye jod ke dekhna nahi hai."

Rudra let the mirror be lowered.

The old nine-breath memory offered him no safe test of a line which had changed between the moments in which he could see it.

---

Vedant's forearm was reviewed too.

The aggravation had settled enough for more ordinary use. Heavy bracing or another unannounced change beside a rescue boat was not part of that finding. He gave the next transport check to the organizer's trained replacement and went to eat while someone else held the phone.

The boys watched him leave the handset on the table.

Veer looked at his own sheet of permitted activity.

"Guruji ka bhi roster banta hai?"

"Banna chahiye," the volunteer said, and wrote the next contact in a space which had held Vedant's name all week.

The small joke did not need anyone to be secretly well.

---

In Mirzapur, the later hand review let Aadhya manage a few more ordinary things with the left hand.

The fragile new growth remained protected. The right thumb and index finger still failed to give reliable feeling. A short supported seated visit could be considered if the current site conditions were confirmed and the clinic vehicle stayed available. It was not clearance to handle a loom load, catch timber or put force through the fingers.

She repeated those limits before asking about the cooperative.

Ira's ordinary right-eye intervals remained bounded. She could listen to the short readback with rests and use the familiar room more confidently. The left covering stayed; there was no trial of second sight waiting at the end of a good morning.

Sitara asked each girl separately whether she actually wanted a short visit, or felt the work had become a debt for the care they had received.

Aadhya wanted to see the workers packing the part of the order which had survived.

Ira wanted to hear the corrected accounts read back to the people who owned them. She did not need to examine every page herself.

They agreed to decide after the next safety confirmation. If that changed, their accounts could travel without them.

The two bowls went back on the lower shelf. Aadhya placed one there herself, slowly, with the left hand supported. The other waited for the helper.

---

The cooperative confirmed the afternoon's conditions before the girls left the clinic.

The compound was open. The local officer knew about the short visit. Only the people needed for the readback would be in the welfare room, and the return vehicle would stay available. The packing work would continue without gathering an audience round two visitors who needed seats.

Aadhya climbed in with help. Ira let Sitara give the unfamiliar edges their proper distance instead of guessing at them to look ready.

The journey was short. The preparation for it had not been wasted because the road took less time than the instructions.

---

The repaired frame stood farther from the rear doorway.

One foot was new timber. The broken part lay beside the caretaker's tools, waiting to be taken away. The creeper along the wall had lost a length of bark and most of the leaves on one runner. A worker had supported what remained without tying it to the frame again.

Aadhya looked at it as they passed.

"Bachega?"

The worker said they were watching it. Watering it harder would not put the stripped bark back. One runner had already dried.

She kept her hand on the support inside the vehicle until Sitara was ready to help her down. The left nail bed had cost her days of use. The plant had no clinician to ask for a fresh permission sheet.

Inside, the welfare-room table had been moved clear of the passage. A chair stood where she could rest the arm without leaning on the fingers. Ira's seat faced away from the glare off the store roof.

The caretaker put his keys in his own pocket before beginning.

---

The corrected accounts were read to the people who had given them.

The worker from the looms described the shout and the operator's stop. The woman from the rear path described the falling frame. Neither was made to claim the whole confrontation because the page looked stronger with everyone seeing everything.

Ira listened with her ordinary right eye closed for part of it. When her own account was read, she corrected a word which implied she had held the live curve away from the workers.

"Dekha tha," she said. "Roka nahi tha."

A young packer looked up.

"Par log bol rahe—"

"Unse bhi yahi bolna."

She gave no demonstration to repair the small disappointment on his face. The covering and the ache had not become less real because somebody outside had supplied her with a more useful victory.

Aadhya's account stopped where the workers had taken the rail's weight. She asked that their part stay in the readback.

The woman who had been at the rear nodded.

"Woh humne pakda tha."

"Haan," Aadhya said. "Isliye main chhod paayi."

---

The customer had accepted a smaller delivery of finished cloth.

The packing workers showed the pieces which would go the next morning. Damaged lengths were kept separately. Nobody folded a stained edge under and called the full order restored because a visitor had come back to see it.

A man from the commercial side asked Aadhya whether she would sign the dispatch as the authority for the protection arrangement.

He had a pen ready. The ordinary loading receipt underneath was already filled in with the cooperative's own goods and carrier.

She asked him to read the sentence above the empty line.

It called her the guarantor of access for households under her Kul's protection.

For a moment she thought of the left hand which could now hold a bowl. A signature would be physically easier than the journey she had just made. It would also put her name where the caretaker's actual keys and the workers' decisions belonged.

"Main gawaah hoon," she said. "Maalik nahi."

She would confirm her own account of the inspection. The cooperative could sign for its cloth and its agreed carriage. She would not turn that into a private right over the people working beside it.

The man put the pen down. He did not become convinced of everything she believed. He took the added guarantee away from the dispatch receipt so the workers could complete the transaction they actually needed.

---

The short session ended on time.

Aadhya's fingers had begun to tire against the support. Ira had heard enough words close together to need the quiet they had promised. The caretaker did not ask them to wait for one more person who was on the way.

At the door, the packing woman asked Sitara whether tomorrow's load would have a public route contact or the private escort somebody had offered.

The offer was being checked. Two groups had each claimed they could keep their own households safe on the road. Neither had yet agreed to travel beside the other's men.

The caretaker said the cloth would not leave until the carrier knew the actual arrangement. He could lose a delivery window. He could not send the workers out under an argument and call it an escort.

Sitara asked for the update at the clinic, then took the girls back before the visit could become a vigil beside the gate.

---

The next shared delivery in Prayagraj would replace borrowed kitchen stock as well as supply the three points.

A full vehicle was cheaper than the little consignments which had kept them alive for a week. It also asked a driver, loaders and receiving workers to be ready for the same window. The organizer checked their agreement one person at a time.

A worker who had accepted the warehouse's paid watch shift came to ask about loading hours.

He did not want anybody to pretend he had been rescued from a job he chose. He had left after the paid hours and refused a second shift. Now the loading offered better timing for home.

The organizer gave him the rate and the work, not a test of whether he had changed sides.

"Watch kiya toh yahaan problem?" the man asked.

"Tumne kisi ko zabardasti bithaaya?"

"Nahi."

"Toh tumse tumhara kaam poochhenge. Uska jawaab bhi tumse hi lenge."

The man looked over the hours and agreed to the one run. His name went beside the actual job.

---

Two offers of escort arrived before the final route check.

One came from men who described the post as serving their Kul's people. The other came from a group representing households in the lanes beyond the upper stop. They said those same private protectors had threatened them and could not be trusted to carry a load into their area.

Some of the fear had names, dates and complaints. Some had become a right to decide which stranger could pass. The public contact asked about both without treating the second as a cure for the first.

The driver did not want either group's armband on his vehicle.

"Maal dono taraf ka hai," he said. "Mujhe waapas bhi aana hai."

The organizer asked for a public escort on the disputed stretch and local receiving workers at the actual stops. Neither private group would acquire the truck, the food or the passengers by walking beside it.

The officer agreed to check what staffing was available. The truck would not leave until that check came back. The boys were not offered as a substitute for the missing personnel.

---

In Mirzapur, the caretaker reached the same limit by a different conversation.

The cloth carrier had a legitimate route. The workers accompanying the delivery had agreed to the work and their return. A private escort wanted to lead them under its own protection mark; another group said it would stop a vehicle bearing that mark on the next stretch.

The caretaker refused to put the workers between those claims.

The public contact was already speaking to the carrier and the local officer. If the departure had to wait, the customer would be told why and the costs would be counted. The clinic was not to send the girls back in the morning to make a disputed departure look safe.

Sitara confirmed that change with the receiving woman. Aadhya's visit had ended. Ira's corrected account could be carried by the worker who owned the copy.

No one in Prayagraj would arrive before dawn to solve Mirzapur's argument. No one in Mirzapur could stand on the boys' road by keeping the telephone open.

---

At the upper stop in Prayagraj, the two groups arrived anyway.

It was still evening. The shared truck had not left the supplier. Only the regular buses and local carts were moving through the public turning space.

On one side of the turning stood an old roadside tree. Its soil patch lay between two broken paving slabs and a low stone post used to keep wheels off the roots. Beyond it, the lane to the receiving post narrowed away from the bus route. The opposite edge led towards the market.

The officer at the stop told the men the delivery was paused. Any escort arrangement would be confirmed with the driver and public contact, not won by getting there first.

A man from the market side pointed to the others.

"Inke saath aayega toh iss lane mein nahi aayega."

The opposite group moved closer. One of its men had a pale curve low beside his wrist. He kept it where the other side could see it without lifting it towards the officer.

The officer called for support and asked the dispatcher to keep the next bus in its bay. A worker turned an arriving handcart back before it entered the narrowing gap.

At the post, the organizer received that update and told the supplier to hold the truck. The clinic's travel contact was warned as well. No medical vehicle was to follow yesterday's route instruction into this gathering.

Rudra and Veer remained inside, listening only while the volunteer gave the facts.

---

At the roadside tree, a man on the lane side lowered his palm heel to the exposed soil.

A root tightened under the loose slab. The low stone post tipped out towards the turning space, still caught among the living fibres which had grown round its foot.

The officer shouted for both sides to step back.

The man with the pale curve stepped forward instead.

Behind him, the shafts of a local handcart struck a second cart already in the turning. The men had moved around them before either loaded cart could turn clear.

The root drew tight.

The pale edge rose.
