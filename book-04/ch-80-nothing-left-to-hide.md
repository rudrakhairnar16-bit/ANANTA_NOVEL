# CHAPTER EIGHTY — NOTHING LEFT TO HIDE

---

> *"The ward pulled the curtain because a boy needed rest.*
> *By morning the district said it was because no one could look at him."*
>
> *— correction kept beneath the first afwah-sheet*

---

The nurse saw the fingers move and asked for his eyes.

Rudra could hear her. Getting from the sound to the thing she wanted took longer than it should have. For a moment he stared past her at the strip of ceiling above the curtain, unable to make its white edge stop trembling.

Then he found her face.

The doctor was still beside the bed. The assessment had not ended when the gold broke, and the small movement of a hand did not end it now. He checked Rudra's response, the breathing, the pulse and the dressing which had begun to stain again. The nurse relayed the changes without making him fight through a whole sentence to be believed.

The next question reached him.

He managed a nod.

"Bolne ki jaldi nahi," the doctor said. "Sun paa rahe ho toh meri taraf dekho."

He did.

---

They moved him when the team was ready to move him.

The replacement bed had been brought close after the sound beneath the old frame. Staff made the transfer together, keeping the braced wrist and supported leg clear while the breathing equipment and leads went with him. Vedant stood back with the small bag. He could see the effort it took Rudra to let strangers move the body he had spent so much of his life being told to command.

At the damaged bed, an attendant tied the warning where the next person taking it would see it. The rail was bent. Whatever had made it possible for a frightened hand to do that, another patient would not be asked to discover the weakness by leaning against it.

The receiving space was a monitored bay near the duty station. The team handed over the actual observations and the acute changes. The old sketches came with the records, useful for showing what had been there, useless as a plan for putting it back.

Rudra watched the curtain rail pass above him.

He expected a piece of gold to catch there, something he could point out before it was lost.

Only a mark in the paint moved across his sight.

---

The fresh dressing lay flat above the collarbone.

The blood had needed attention after the break. The nurse checked it again, settled the support at his wrist and asked about the ankle when he was able to answer. These were still separate hurts, each capable of becoming worse if the rest of him distracted everybody long enough.

"Pair," he whispered.

She asked where. He found enough words to show her that the padding had shifted during the tremor. She adjusted it within the care plan, then waited until he could tell her the pressure was less.

For several breaths he looked only at the foot.

He had been afraid that everything ordinary would become impossible to describe once the wall went. Here was an ordinary ache, changed by somebody listening to where it was.

The doctor stayed for the next reassessment.

---

A little later, Rudra said his name without being asked twice.

The doctor heard it, checked him again and kept the breathing support and observation in place. The improvement belonged to those minutes. It did not give anyone permission to promise the rest of the night.

Vedant passed on the new account himself while the receiving contact could still ask the nurse questions. More responsive, moved for closer observation, still being assessed. The complete loss of the old cover was not edited into another small flare for the comfort of people far away.

Rukmini asked who was with him now.

Vedant gave the actual answer. Himself at present; the clinical team through the night. A colleague at the post had taken the agreed hours. When Vedant needed to rest, Rudra would not be left depending on whether an injured man could keep his eyes open.

She asked for the receiving contact again and repeated it back.

Afterwards Vedant sat where the nurse allowed, his supported forearm on his own lap. He had not noticed how hard he was holding the bag's strap in the other hand.

He put it down.

---

The bright colours no longer filled the curtain.

They had drawn closer to the body, red-black and gold still distinct beneath the examination light. Rudra could see a little at the loosened collar when he looked down. Where he had learned to search for a line holding around a break, there was no joined line to follow.

"Wapas?" he asked.

Vedant came nearer to hear him, then looked where he was looking.

"Nahi."

Rudra waited. The man did not add a number, a promised morning or a better way of asking the same light to stay.

The cuff tightened on Rudra's arm for the next observation. He let it finish before trying to speak again.

"Khatam ho gaya?"

Vedant understood which thing he meant.

"Kavach. Haan."

The nurse made the next note. Outside the bay, someone asked for a clean sheet. The request went down the passage and an answer came back.

Rudra listened until his eyes closed again. When the clinician checked, he opened them. The curtain was still there, drawn for a patient whose night was not over.

---

Before dawn, the nurse woke him for another check and he knew her voice before he opened his eyes.

His replies came more readily now. He could describe where the pressure had eased and where it still hurt. The new dressing had not needed changing again during the last checks; nobody lifted it for the sake of finding a more impressive light beneath it.

The doctor reviewed him and the investigations already started. There would be further assessment in the morning. For now, the support stayed, and when the nurse allowed a few supervised sips she held the cup where he did not have to work the braced wrist to reach it.

The water tasted of the cup.

He had wanted it so badly that the small, disappointing taste nearly made him cry.

"Aur?" the nurse asked.

He nodded. She gave him what had been allowed, then moved it back out of the way.

---

Vedant was at the chair when Rudra looked for him.

He had rested during a staffed handover. The supported forearm had not become less swollen because there had been something larger to watch. He settled it before leaning forward.

Rudra looked at the light at his own collar and then at the man's face.

"Dar lag raha hai?"

Vedant did not make him wait for a noble answer.

"Haan."

The answer left Rudra nowhere to hide the next question.

"Mujhse?"

Vedant took a moment.

"Jo hua, usse. Tumhe kya ho raha hai, usse."

He left his uninjured hand on his own knee. No demonstration of a fearless grip, no request that the boy trust himself with another person's fingers in order to prove a lesson had survived.

Rudra looked down at his open palm.

"Maine kiya?"

"Raat ko tumne koi roop nahi bulaya. Jitna humne dekha, doctor ko bata diya hai. Wajah ka jawaab abhi mere paas nahi hai."

Rudra had been asking for blame as if it might be easier to carry than an unknown cause. He let the answer remain unfinished instead of making the other man punish him into a simpler story.

---

"Ab kya karna hai?" he asked.

He expected the old sort of answer: a time to get up, a thing to repeat, the point at which pain could be made into evidence that he had tried enough.

Vedant glanced towards the nurse, who had heard the question too.

"Abhi ilaaj."

"Uske baad?"

"Uske baad ka aaj bana ke nahi doonga."

The refusal frightened him. So did the relief which followed it.

For once, there was no hidden task he could fail while lying still. Whatever would have to be learned later had not been placed under his pillow with an expectation that he practise it before morning.

Rudra turned his head towards the passage. An attendant crossed it carrying the folded blanket from the other bay.

"Woh aadmi? Paas waale?"

The nurse knew who he meant. She checked with the receiving staff and told him the man was in the staffed space to which they had moved him, with his own treatment continuing and his relative informed.

The bag had arrived too.

Rudra had not realized until then that he still needed to hear about the bag.

---

When he could listen without tiring so quickly, Vedant asked whether he wanted the message from Veer.

Rudra did.

The phone was set where he could hear it without reaching. Veer's recorded voice said he was at the post, that the phone was there, that an answer was not necessary.

It was shorter than Rudra expected.

He asked for it again.

This time he heard the little noise before the first word, the breath Veer had taken while deciding how to sound. There was no crowd in the recording, no bell and no person shouting the score over him. Just his friend, much too awake in another room.

Rudra shut his eyes while the last line finished.

"Jawaab dena hai," he said after a while.

Vedant waited until he was ready and held the phone through the short recording.

"Suna. Main yahin hoon."

He listened to his own thin voice afterwards. It embarrassed him. He left it as it was. Vedant sent it when he nodded.

---

Daylight entered along the edge of the curtain.

The red-black and gold had become less visible to ordinary sight. The morning clinician could still see what remained at the collar during the necessary examination; no concealing outline had returned around it.

Rudra watched the hands at work instead of asking for the mirror.

The examination, the renewed injury instructions and the continued observation were real things which could be done. The old wall was a real thing which could not be found by waiting for the light to brighten at a kinder angle.

He understood that much.

He had no way yet of knowing how far beyond this room the loss had already travelled.

---

In the chamber beyond Earth, the bowl remained dark at its centre.

The lamp beside it had been out for hours. The red-black mark still carried its fine gold margin. A hand came back to the ledge and rested beside the rim.

A sheet was laid on the ledge.

The hand copied what could be seen. Red-black, then the distinct gold. It did not write the name on Rudra's hospital chart; that chart had not arrived with the exposure. The page beside the bowl contained a record of the signature, not a route through a human city.

A door opened behind the ledge. Only the lower edge entered view, a dark line widening across the floor.

The hand stopped writing. At the doorway, a second hand took the sheet. The door closed again. The bowl remained on the ledge.

---

Elsewhere, someone had caught the suspended metal strip before it could strike the stand again.

Its ringing stopped against the fingers.

The red-black and gold remained on the polished side. The hand turned it through half a circle. A second hand entered the narrow space above the stand, stayed there for a moment, then reached for a covering cloth.

The object disappeared beneath the cloth. Its sound had already been heard.

In another place, a long row of hanging bells stood motionless above a stone floor. A small plate beneath them carried the same colours. Bare feet approached along the edge of the stone and stopped. The bells did not ring to tell anyone what to call the thing which had appeared below them.

The feet moved away again.

The plate remained.

---

The concealment had failed across all the Lokas.

Not only the chamber with the bowl, not only the rooms where something had been handled or a sound answered. There was no realm left outside the exposure, no surviving reach of the old kavach where the signature remained hidden simply because Earth could not see a witness there.

It did not follow that every inhabitant had turned towards a single light. The sky over each life had not become a hospital window. People went on speaking, sleeping, working; objects stood unattended, and some of those which answered the exposure did so in empty spaces.

The true signature stood exposed to all the Lokas.

The distances remained. A hand in one of those places still had to lift a latch to leave its room. On Earth, a nurse still had to walk to the foot of a bed to check the ankle there. The loss was in the hiding, not in the space between those lives.

The boy carrying that source was still breathing with help in Prayagraj.

He had not seen any of the hands.

---

In Mirzapur, the morning update reached Sitara before the girls' ordinary care was finished.

More responsive. Still under closer observation. No return of the concealing outline. She spoke to the ward, asked what could properly be passed on and gave the girls the account she had actually received.

Ira listened with the left covering in place. She asked for one phrase again, then for a pause. The right eye was open to the room, not to a chamber elsewhere; staring harder would not put a witness where a friend was needed.

Aadhya's supported left hand lay against the cloth. The right fingertips had not recovered while she waited. She looked down when the helper asked about the prescribed movement and found herself angry at the small task, the painful distance through which her own hand still had to be helped.

Then she let the woman finish the instruction.

Keeping her own care did not mean she had stopped being frightened for him.

---

"Phone kar sakte hain?" Aadhya asked afterwards.

"Abhi lambi baat nahi," Sitara said. "Jab woh chahega aur team theek kahegi."

Ira kept her face towards the sound.

"Hum bolenge kya?"

Aadhya had been collecting brave things since the night update. Each sounded worse when she imagined saying it to someone who had just struggled to give a doctor his name.

She looked at the helper putting away the heavier book they had not used that morning.

"Pata nahi."

For once, Ira did not hurry to fill the space after an admission like that.

"Woh bhi theek hai," she said.

The helper offered to take a message. Neither girl gave her a speech about strength.

Aadhya asked her to say they had been told honestly and that he did not have to record a better-sounding reply for their sake. Ira added that they could wait for a call without making the waiting his job to end.

Sitara sent the short message through the agreed contact. The phone stayed on the table afterwards. She had her own rest to take; another person would listen for it while she did.

---

The girls sat by the window when the care interval was over.

A cart passed below with cloth bundled higher on one side than the other. Its owner stopped to retie it before taking the turn. Aadhya watched until it had passed out of sight.

"Kal waala sawaal?" Ira asked quietly.

"Baad mein."

This time the word was not an instruction for Rudra. It was permission they gave each other.

They could be frightened, tired and unable to finish an ordinary page. They could also leave that page where another day might find it. Neither thing would close the distance to Prayagraj. Both belonged to the lives they were still trying to keep.

---

Veer heard Rudra's reply after his own morning check.

Two short sentences. The voice was thin enough to hurt him, and present enough that he listened again before putting the phone down.

The volunteer beside him waited.

"Kya bola?"

Veer repeated it. He had to clear his throat before the last part.

The man nodded, then helped him settle the knee in the position they had disturbed for the examination. Veer's shoulder stayed in its support. The person covering the night hours went to rest after giving the next willing volunteer the actual account of what still needed doing.

A volunteer carried the breakfast tray towards the next bed. Veer caught the smell and realized that being frightened had not stopped him from being hungry.

He asked for his share when it came.

---

Later, the organizer returned from the akhada.

The damaged part of the field was still roped off. The hired workers had begun the ordinary repair: loose material taken up, the unstable edge made safe, the ground worked where roots had torn it. The surface would not be ready for another bout merely because the first day's work was paid for.

The organizer put the labour payment beside the account of the initial treatment charges. The remaining claims stayed open. There was less money in the reserve than there had been before the event, and nobody used the night's news to make the difference disappear.

A man waiting at the table asked whether the champion had been taken to hospital because the last fight had broken his seal.

"Champion aurat hai," the organizer said. "Kal check ke baad apna paisa leke gayi."

The man had heard another version. A curtain had been pulled, people moved away, a brightness nobody could look at. By the time the story reached the table, it belonged to the final's winner because a story about power seemed to need a winner.

Veer kept his hand off the phone.

Correcting a false winner did not require him to offer Rudra's private night as a better story.

"Afwah ko chehra mat do," he said.

The organizer asked the waiting man what he had actually come to discuss. It was a copy of the grounds account. They returned to that.

Outside, the larger story continued. Not every telling accepted a correction. At one stall the winner was still a man; at another the whole hospital had gone dark. In the real ward a nurse was opening a fresh packet of gauze beneath an ordinary working light.

---

The volunteer taking Rudra's small bag went by the checked local route and reported at the receiving desk.

A clean loose shirt, the things which could be carried without asking Vedant's forearm to do it, the contact at the post if something else was needed. The visitor did not expect a bedside audience in return for bringing them.

The ward had already moved the phone, its working charger and the unfinished college page from the old bay. The nurse put the new bag with those belongings and told Rudra who had sent it when he was awake enough to listen.

He asked about Veer.

The account came from Veer's own morning reply: still at the post, checked, eaten, knee supported. He had heard the recording. Rudra did not have to make another one before resting.

For a while he looked at the shape of the clean shirt through the open bag.

The nurse asked whether he wanted it put away.

"Rehne do," he said.

There was nothing useful to do with it at present. That was part of why he wanted to see it.

---

By later morning, the pressure had eased enough for him to speak short sentences without having to arrange every word around it. The clinical team continued the reassessments and kept him under closer observation. Nobody had changed the instructions for loading the wrist or standing on the ankle.

Vedant read the girls' message after asking whether he wanted to hear it.

They had been told. There was no demand for a brave voice, no hurry to make their waiting stop. The familiar college question could stay unfinished.

Rudra looked towards the page stand.

"Sabko pata hai?"

Vedant answered the question within what he actually knew. The family, Sitara and the girls, Veer. The receiving team. He had not hidden the full break from any of the agreed contacts.

Rudra let the list settle.

"Theek."

The word meant he would not have to prepare different versions of the night for each person who loved him. It did not mean the night had become all right.

---

When the nurse next offered to move the page stand nearer, Rudra asked her to leave it lowered.

He was too tired to read. He could say so now without pretending he had already finished.

Beyond the bay, the shift went on. A patient asked for help with a pillow. Someone laughed once at the duty station and stopped to answer the next call. The ward held more than his emergency, and those other lives had not been reduced to spectators because something in him could no longer hide.

He closed his eyes for the rest interval the team had allowed.

The page stayed where he had left it.

---

In Varanasi, Dhruv waited until Ram's care was finished before asking whether he wanted the next update read to him.

Ram was awake. The old chest-drain site still needed care; the drain itself was gone. His breathing support was close enough for the nurse to use when he tired. Listening to news from another hospital had not taken him out of this one.

Rukmini sat beside the bed with the shoulder supported. Dadi had made her eat before the next call. Dhruv took the other chair, and for a little while nobody repeated a fact merely to prove they had been present to hear it.

The phone on the table rang.

It was the receiving ward in Prayagraj. Rudra was awake, the team had allowed a short family call, and he had asked for it himself.

Rukmini looked at Ram. The nurse checked him and helped put the phone where both could hear without either having to hold it aloft.

Dadi moved the water away from the edge of the table.

---

"Maa?"

The sound was thin. It was also his, without anyone making the voice larger before letting it cross the distance.

"Haan. Sun rahi hoon."

She asked whether he had been allowed water. He told her about the cup, the few sips, the strange disappointment of getting the thing he had wanted and finding that it tasted ordinary.

Rukmini pressed her good hand against her own knee until she could answer steadily.

"Hospital ka cup hai. Mithai thodi milegi usmein."

He made a small sound which might have become a laugh on a better morning. It hurt at the chest; he stopped and told the nurse beside him before his mother had to guess why the sound had changed.

The call paused while he was checked. Nobody filled the pause with an assurance on his behalf.

When he could continue, he said the clean shirt had come from the post.

Rukmini asked about the fastening. He looked towards the bag; the nurse told him it opened at the front.

"Achha," his mother said. "Haath mod ke pehenne ki zaroorat nahi. Jab woh log bolen tab badal lena."

"Maa, abhi bhi shirt?"

"Uski bhi baat karenge."

He looked at the folded cloth for a long time before answering.

"Theek hai."

---

Ram said his name.

Rudra had heard his father on the phone before. The particular quiet of this call made him remember the bandaged fingertips which had found his hand in the hospital, the effort it had taken Ram to make even that small touch his own.

"Papa."

"Sun raha hoon."

Rudra wanted to give him a report good enough to let him rest. He had been doing that in one way or another since the first morning the training injuries became something he could hide by moving carefully.

He could hear his own breath through the support. There was no good report waiting behind a better posture now.

"Raat ko bahut dar laga," he said.

The words left him without the apology he had expected to have to attach.

"Abhi bhi lag raha hai."

In Varanasi, Ram's hand moved against the blanket, then stayed there. He did not ask for a stronger voice.

Rukmini told their son what they had been told, so he could hear that the full break had reached them honestly. They knew about the night team, the move, the changed response and the care continuing now. They would keep the ward contact. He did not have to be the messenger for every check.

Rudra listened without interrupting.

For once, his parents knew he was frightened before he had found a way to make the fear useful to them.

---

The nurse in Prayagraj checked how tired he was getting.

He said he could manage another little while. She allowed the brief continuation with the phone still supported, and stayed close enough to bring the call to an end when he needed rest.

Vedant sat beyond her working space. The page remained lowered. Through the cloth of the loosened collar, the last visible gold lay within the red-black, not around it. There was no line left for Rudra to imagine holding until the people he loved stopped worrying.

He could still ask for help. He could still say his own name. Neither fact made what had happened a victory, but the absence of a victory no longer meant there was nothing he was allowed to say.

He looked at the phone.

In the other ward, his father waited for the next words.

---

Rudra had been asking whether the wall would come back.

There was another question behind it, older than the dark nick below the breastbone or the small patch at the collar. He had carried that question through classrooms, mirrors, streets and hospital nights, dressing it in smaller ones about what he should practise or how long he ought to endure.

The wall was gone now.

The doctor still needed to know where he hurt. The nurse still needed his wrist to stay supported. Those things would not wait for an answer from his father, and he would not ask them to.

But he could stop treating his own life as a question he needed permission to put into words.

"Papa," he said. "Yeh kavach mere andar kya chhupa raha tha?"

At the other end of the line, Ram drew a breath.

Rudra waited for the words.
