# CHAPTER SIXTY-FIVE — THE HOUR BEFORE WATER

---

> *"They remembered the hour at which the water returned.*
> *The men who opened it remembered leaving home."*
>
> *— ward pumping inquiry, testimony collected after the first closures*

---

The constable looked at the keys on the dispensary desk.

"Main aaunga," he said. "Par akele nahi. Control room se gaadi aa rahi hai. Municipal team bhi."

The operator did not pick them up.

"Mere ghar ka rasta bhi unhi ke paas se hai."

The nurse left the empty jug beneath the dead tap. The teacher had been holding the door for the final shelter vehicle. She let it settle against her shoulder and waited.

The constable asked who was at the operator's house. Wife, mother. A son working the night shift elsewhere. The operator had told his wife he was going to ask for help; she had wanted him to stay in the house until daylight.

"Unko phone kijiye," the constable said. "Kahan rehna hai, unse poochhenge. Aapke duty pe jaane ki sazaa unko nahi milni chahiye."

The operator looked at him for a moment, then took out his phone.

No one made the call on speaker.

---

The municipal supervisor arrived in a vehicle carrying two fitters, a torch and the station's duty assistant. The assistant had been turned away from the other approach. He had thought the operator was inside.

They stood facing one another in the dispensary light.

"Tum nahi pahunche?"

"Peechhe se gaya tha. Bola saamne se aa. Saamne waale bole peechhe jaa."

The supervisor stopped them before the conversation became an apology for having believed somebody in an armband.

He checked what each had actually seen. Who stood at which gate. Whether any municipal worker had entered the switch room. Whether a valve position had been checked, or merely reported by a man pointing through a fence.

Neither worker had inspected the equipment. Neither could certify the plant safe to start.

The supervisor called the control room again. He needed access for both men and the fitters, a protected working space, and a public distribution decision from his department. Not an escort which took them to the gate and left.

The nurse showed him the dispensary's remaining containers. Then she gave him the hospital's contact rather than guessing its reserve.

The teacher finally stepped outside to the waiting residents.

"Main saath aa rahi hoon," she said. "Bas ek phone."

She confirmed that the girl and her mother were still in treatment and that the brother would remain with her until his mother asked for him. The boy had curled up on a chair beside the old woman's borrowed blanket.

She woke him gently for the ride. He asked whether they were going back to school.

"Aaj nahi."

"Kal?"

"Kal dekhkar bataayenge."

He accepted the answer without liking it. She took his hand and went with the last vehicle.

The operator watched it leave. His own keys were still on the desk.

---

At the hospital, water became a task before it became a rumour.

The night supervisor checked the storage with maintenance staff. Non-urgent use was reduced. Drinking water and the clinical supply were handled separately; no relative was sent to dip a household bucket into a tank serving the wards.

Rudra woke when the nurse moved the wash basin.

"Kya hua?"

"Supply kam hai. Reserve hai. Abhi isi se chal raha hai."

He looked towards the critical-care passage.

"Udhar?"

"Unka intezaam alag se check hua hai. Aap rest kijiye."

She gave the answer she had, not a promise about the whole morning.

Vedant sat beyond the curtain, speaking quietly to the municipal supervisor. His bruised forearm lay on a folded towel. He passed on the hospital desk number and asked that any public notice distinguish an inspection from an actual restart.

"Switch tak pahunchna paani aana nahi hai," he said.

He finished the call before Rudra could ask him to go.

"Team jaa rahi hai. Engineer aur police."

"Aap?"

Vedant shifted the towel under the swollen place.

"Meri zaroorat yahan pade toh main yahan milunga."

Rudra listened to the movement in the next bay. Dhruv was still being observed after the smoke. His cough had interrupted sleep twice. Across another passage Ram's treatment continued behind the closed doors.

There was no uninjured version of the room waiting to stand up on command.

Rudra asked whether the school teacher had reached the next shelter. Vedant showed him the brief message confirming arrival, then turned the screen away when the nurse came to check the ankle.

---

At three-ten, the operator put the work keys into his pocket.

His wife and mother had chosen to spend the remaining night with his sister. A police vehicle had taken them by a checked approach; the address stayed with the duty officer, not with the men outside the pumping station.

The operator had spoken to his wife after they arrived.

Now he climbed into the escort vehicle beside the duty assistant. The supervisor and fitters followed in the municipal van.

At the turn towards the station, the first protection board caught the headlights.

A man stepped into the road and lifted his palm.

---

The escort stopped short of the man, leaving room for the municipal van behind it.

A sub-inspector got out. The operator recognized the guard's jacket; he had seen it beside the road two hours earlier. In daylight he might have recognized the face without the jacket too.

"Station kholna hai," the officer said. "Gaadi side karo."

"Kholne se pehle baat toh suno."

"Supervisor sunenge. Road pe nahi. Hathiyaar andar nahi."

Behind the guard, three men rose from a bench. One had a child asleep across his lap. Another moved a motorcycle out of the way. The guard with the raised palm stayed where he was until the officer walked towards him.

Then he stepped aside.

The vehicles crossed into the forecourt. Police cleared the strip between the station door and the open fence. The supervisor asked for the operator's account again in front of the duty assistant, so a warning given on the road could not later become an instruction nobody remembered giving.

The guard followed to the fence.

"Hamaare mohalle mein kal se nahi aaya. Udhar tank bhar rahe hain."

"Kaunsa tank?" the supervisor asked.

The man named a lane. The assistant knew it. The supervisor sent a fitter to check the corresponding readings with him once access was safe.

A real complaint went into the work list. It did not give the complainant the switch room.

---

The back approach had not emptied because the front one had opened.

Two women and four men stood beyond the service gate, under the station's broken outside light. One woman held the paper she had given the operator earlier. A Prakriti mark had been drawn at its top. Below were lanes to be supplied before the opposite side received anything.

The operator stayed behind the police line while the officer at the gate asked them to step back from the hinge.

"Wahan se aaye hain toh unka kholenge," the woman said, pointing towards the forecourt.

The supervisor came to where both approaches could hear him.

"Yeh municipal station hai. Pehle plant check hoga. Hospital aur public points ki supply restore hogi. Baaki pressure dekhkar. Kis ghar mein kaun paida hua, uska valve nahi laga hai."

A man behind the woman laughed once, without amusement.

"Kaagaz pe sabka hai. Paani unka jaata hai."

"Reading dekhenge. Shikayat likhwaaiye. Duty band karwaake reading bhi nahi milegi."

He asked each group to name one unarmed resident who could wait outside the working enclosure and hear the findings. They would not choose the valve positions. Other households could send their own witnesses when the notice went out.

The guard at the front objected to the woman being allowed to wait at all.

She objected to his jacket.

The supervisor looked at the police officer, not between their faces for permission.

The officer pointed out the line beyond which neither would come. A constable stayed at each entrance. The others went with the workers to the station door.

---

The operator put the first key into the lock and found his fingers shaking.

The duty assistant stood beside him. He did not take the keys away. He held the torch where it lit the lock rather than the operator's face.

Behind them the guard called, "Hamaare logon ko pehle nahi mila toh tum jawaab doge."

The operator left the key still for a moment.

He looked towards the supervisor.

"Yeh bhi statement mein lena. Naam se bola hai mujhe."

The sub-inspector asked the guard to repeat himself for the account.

The man pulled at his jacket's zip.

"Matlab duty ki zimmedaari—"

"Duty ki zimmedaari main loonga," the operator said. "Tumhaari pasand ki nahi."

The key turned.

Inside, the air smelled of warm metal, damp floor and a room shut longer than its shift allowed. The assistant checked the immediate working area before the fitters came through. No one reached straight for a switch to satisfy the men watching.

The supervisor told the operator to begin the inspection.

---

There were two faults, not one story.

A supply isolation which should have been open was partly shut. The department's previous shift sheet recorded it open; its seal had been disturbed. That established an unauthorized change since the check, not whose fingers had made it.

Farther along, a worn joint was leaking into the drain. The fitter showed the old staining below the fresh wet patch. It had not begun leaking because a protection group had arrived that night. It still needed repair before normal pressure could be restored.

The supervisor had both recorded. He did not let the seal become an excuse to ignore the joint, or the joint become an excuse to erase the interference.

He went back to the witnesses and explained the difference. The department would preserve the disturbed seal and logs for investigation. His crew would decide what could safely run now and what needed an isolated repair.

The woman at the service gate asked whether her lane would get water.

"Standpost pe pehle milega. Gharon mein abhi poora pressure nahi doonga. Test bhi hoga. Peene ka tanker alag aa raha hai."

The guard at the front leaned against the fence.

"Toh itna police leke bhi itna hi?"

The supervisor looked towards the men still kneeling beside the leak.

"Haan," he said. "Jitna sach mein ho sakta hai."

---

At three-forty-seven, a stone broke the station's side pane.

The fitter beside the wall jerked back with a cut along his cheek. Glass fell across the tool roll. His partner caught his sleeve before he could wipe his face with the dirty glove.

The operator took his hand away from the controls.

Outside, a constable caught the young man whose arm was still drawn back. Two people pointed towards him. A third tried to pull the constable away, shouting that nobody had been hurt badly enough for an arrest.

The supervisor went to the door.

"Kaam rok diya hai," he said. "Andar aadmi ko chot lagi hai. Jagah khaali karaaiye."

The officer moved the witnesses farther from the broken window. The young man was taken to the vehicle. His companions' complaints followed him; the officer asked for individual names and accounts, not a list of everyone wearing the same band.

The fitter's cut was covered by the first-aid attendant travelling with the escort. He would be taken for assessment. His partner checked the tools for glass and moved the roll clear. The damaged working area had to be made safe before anybody resumed.

Four o'clock kept approaching while nothing ran.

---

The operator's phone vibrated in his pocket.

His wife asked whether he had reached.

"Haan. Andar hoon."

"Chal gaya?"

"Abhi nahi."

She was quiet long enough for him to hear a chair scrape on her side. His mother was asking for the phone.

He could have told them the line was bad. Instead he said someone had thrown a stone, the police had taken him, and the workers were checking the room again.

"Main wapas aa sakta hoon," he said.

He looked towards the duty assistant as he said it. The assistant looked away, giving the conversation a little privacy inside a room with a broken pane.

His wife did not tell him to be brave.

"Abhi bahar nikloge toh akele mat nikalna," she said. "Aur duty khatam ho toh seedha yahan. Ghar pe subah bhi pehle phone karenge."

He promised that much.

When the call ended he asked the supervisor who would remain after the pump started. The threat did not end at a running motor. Somebody would have to stop the guards coming back to the door once the escort had its success to report.

The supervisor called the control room in his hearing. A relief police detail was assigned to the working site. The next municipal shift would arrive under escort too. Repairs and public supply times would be posted through the department, with a complaint number which did not belong to either group.

Nobody promised that every walk home had become safe.

The operator put his phone away.

---

By then, people with empty containers had gathered beyond the forecourt.

A few had come because a pump was being opened. Others had followed the police lights. The guard in the jacket told them to form a line beside his men, where their household names could be checked.

An older woman moved her bucket to the other side of the road.

"Tere paas naam likhwaaya toh udhar waale nahi jaane denge," she said.

"Toh udhar likhwaa lo."

"Mujhe paani bharna hai. Dono ki naukri nahi karni."

Two women joined her. Then a man carrying containers for neighbours who could not come out themselves. He asked the constable where the public queue was supposed to begin.

The officer marked a clear waiting space away from the vehicle entrance. He asked for two residents to help keep it clear for workers. The guard's bench remained behind the fence; nobody had to sit at it to receive a turn.

At the service gate, the woman with the priority paper watched the new line form.

"Hamaara bhi number?" she asked the supervisor.

"Public point pe. Baaki sabke saath."

She looked down at her paper. Folded it once. Put it in her bag.

It was not an apology. She stayed to hear whether the water actually came.

---

The crew finished the immediate safety checks. The leaking section would remain isolated for repair; a reduced service could run without pretending the whole network was ready.

The supervisor gave the instruction. The operator and assistant began the start under the remaining fitter's watch.

The motor's first sound was smaller than the voices outside it.

A tremor passed through the floor, then settled. Readings began to change. The fitter watched the joint they had isolated; the assistant checked the return report from the first downstream point.

The operator did not leave his position to announce anything.

At four-eleven, the supervisor received confirmation of flow at the public standpost. Flushing and checks were still underway. Drinking water was being brought separately; the first new water from a long-interrupted pipe was not a festival drink.

He called the hospital maintenance desk.

"Flow aa raha hai," he said. "Aap apni receiving check kijiye. Main yahan se confirm nahi karunga."

The guard at the front looked at the public queue, then at his unused household list.

For the first time that night, people were waiting for water rather than for his answer.

---

The hospital confirmed its incoming supply twenty minutes later.

Maintenance staff checked the receiving side and the storage rise before the nurse told the wards. It was reduced service. There would still be careful use, separate drinking-water arrangements and another check when the repair crew changed shift.

Rudra heard only the last part of the message at first. He had fallen asleep while waiting for the first.

"Aa gaya?" he asked.

The nurse nodded.

He looked towards his father's doors and then at the basin beside his bed. Neither had changed shape. The men who had gone to the pumping station had not entered either room, but something they had done outside now let the people inside continue their ordinary work.

He put his head back before the nurse had to ask.

---

At Rajghat, the recorder-lady woke to Sitara speaking to the driver.

The day's medical appointments were still needed. The roads had to be checked again. A working tap did not remove the men from every crossing, and yesterday's checked approach could not be carried in a pocket as today's guarantee.

Aadhya's mother helped her wash and change. The cup was held where she could drink without trying to pinch its rim. Ira sat beside the door with her uncovered side turned away from the courtyard glare.

"Aaj bhi wahi kapde?" Aadhya asked.

"Saaf hain. Dheele hain."

"Photo mein repeat ho jaayega."

Her mother looked up sharply, then saw the small movement at her mouth.

"Photo ke liye alag haath laa doon?"

Aadhya laughed and immediately regretted how much she had needed to.

Ira asked whether those spare hands could sign the hospital forms too. Sitara said she would ask for two pairs, since everyone in the room had discovered a sudden need to delegate.

They went to the vehicle slowly. The driver opened both doors before anyone had to stand waiting in the light.

---

The eye examination took place behind a closed door, not in a queue of people wanting to see whether Ira had brought her sight back from the ghat.

The doctor checked the injured eye and replaced the covering as directed. Ordinary rest had not made second sight safe. Her uncovered eye still tired quickly, and the tremor remained when she reached for the examination chair's arm.

She was given the next review and the same specific changes which meant returning sooner. The recorder-lady repeated the plan back. Ira corrected the time of one appointment herself, then closed the eye which had done enough ordinary looking for that morning.

Aadhya's dressings were changed nearby. The doctor asked her to distinguish what she could feel from what she knew must be happening because she saw his hand.

She failed to feel the light contact at the same fingers.

He wrote that down without asking her to try harder.

Her mother watched him protect the skin again. The nails would not grow back to an audience's schedule. Permission to rest outside observation remained permission to rest.

Neither girl was sent to the pumping station to stand beside a working tap for a photograph.

---

Dadi met them with breakfast after the reviews.

She had found a space where chairs could stay without obstructing the passage. Veer arrived for his check with his mother and was told the chairs were not a public invitation to reopen his commentary.

"Main toh chup tha," he said.

"Isliye pehle bataa diya," Dadi replied.

Rudra could hear them from the bay. For a moment the sound was not a report of another road closing. Veer's mother loosened the fold which pressed beneath the sling and gave him food he could manage with his good hand. Aadhya asked for the bowl to be steadied. Ira listened without making herself turn towards each speaker.

Nobody pretended they were back at a training breakfast.

Dhruv was brought past for reassessment. He lifted his good hand in greeting, then stopped to cough before the smile had settled. The nurse went with him. Dadi kept his portion covered rather than calling him back to eat it before it cooled.

The fitter from the pumping station sat farther down the waiting area with a dressed cheek. The cut had been assessed and treated; he had been told when it needed checking again. His partner had sent word that the temporary repair was holding.

A volunteer recognized his municipal shirt and asked whether he was the man who had opened the water.

"Aur log bhi the," he said.

Dadi asked if he had eaten.

He said he would go home first.

She passed him a wrapped portion for the journey. It was an offer, not another job he had to finish before being allowed to leave.

---

Later, Rudra was helped to the chair near his mother's bay.

Rukmini's shoulder stayed supported. She had slept through the first water update. Now she watched him arrange the injured ankle on the support before he looked at her.

"Raat bhar jaage?"

"Beech mein so gaya."

"Achha hua."

He had been preparing to apologize for that part. She turned her good hand palm-up beside the sheet, and he rested his uninjured fingers against it instead.

Dadi had put a small bottle within her reach, the cap loosened so she did not need both hands. Rukmini asked Rudra to tighten it a little before the bottle was moved; she did not want it spilling on the bed.

He turned it until it held, then stopped.

For a while they discussed nothing larger than where the bottle should stand.

---

By noon, three different stories were being told about the water.

At the tea stall, four young people had marched to the station and made the guards kneel. Outside the pharmacy, a woman had turned the pipes with a tree root. In the actual duty room, a tired operator was handing over reduced readings, an open repair and the names of the men relieving him.

The sub-inspector recorded the threat against him as well as the stone thrown through the pane. A police detail remained at the station. The two private household lists had not become distribution orders.

At one public point, neighbours from opposing lanes stood in the same queue because there was only one working outlet and both needed it. At another, men still argued over whose containers had arrived first. The drinking-water tanker needed a constable at its stop. Nobody who worked that shift mistook running water for peace.

The operator went to his sister's house in the escort vehicle after handover.

His wife opened the door before he reached it. She asked whether he had eaten. He asked whether his mother had slept.

Only after those answers did he take the work card from round his neck.

---

At the school, inspection tape replaced the blue cloth across the entrance.

The teacher was permitted to stand outside while the responsible officers checked the rooms. She was not told to bring the families back because the flames were out. Damaged bedding and food would need assessment; the clean community rooms remained the night's shelter.

The old woman's steel box was still unaccounted for. The teacher recorded what she had described without promising it would be found intact.

The kitchen put in another request for flour. The miller's daughter said her father would help find sellers, but he could not donate eighteen more sacks every time armed men ruined a load. The teacher thanked her for the first eighteen instead of making his limit sound like a refusal of the city.

The two private buyers were given the contact for the continuing warehouse inquiry. They still had claims against a seller, not against a relief meal. The loader's employer was asked about wages during recovery. His sister did not have to withdraw the assault account before anybody would discuss help.

The work of the previous day had not disappeared beneath the new emergency.

---

The first call from outside Varanasi came through the common kitchen.

A relief organizer in Prayagraj wanted to know whether the road-control groups had dispersed or merely moved after losing the grain crossing. Their own vehicles were being divided into permitted and forbidden loads, and a neutral receiving post was under pressure to let one group choose who could work there.

The cook did not tell him that the same men must be responsible. He gave him the municipal contact handling the local obstructions.

An hour later, a Mirzapur cooperative reached the recorder-lady through a records volunteer she knew. Families lived beside the weaving sheds. Men claiming hereditary protection were demanding levies and declarations of belonging before letting the common loading court remain open.

The older woman asked for the speaker's name, the cooperative's registered contact and which authorities had already been approached. She rang back through a separate contact before passing the request on.

The two accounts did not establish one commander. They established two more places where people were being told that public work and private ancestry were the same thing.

The recorder-lady brought them to Vedant after the girls were resting again.

---

He did not answer either city with four names.

First he called the receiving posts, then the officials working with them. Local volunteers were already protecting deliveries, documenting threats, moving families when necessary. They were not sitting without help until a group of injured strangers could arrive from Varanasi.

What they lacked, the organizers said, were people who could speak across the lines without claiming the place for their own. The ghat survivors' refusal to be taken away by separate Kul escorts had travelled farther than any accurate account of that morning's water duty.

"Woh sab patient hain," Vedant said.

"Aaj nahi maang rahe," the Prayagraj organizer replied. "Jab aa sakein. Lekin baat abhi se karni hai. Yahan ke log roz yahin rahenge."

Vedant asked what work could be done with protected travel and no combat role. He asked about medical contacts, safe rooms, actual access, who would meet an arriving vehicle. Nobody could supply all the answers on the first call.

He told them to begin with those, not a banner welcoming champions.

---

Rudra heard the second conversation when Vedant came back to the bay.

The speaker was a woman from the Mirzapur cooperative. Machines clattered behind her, making her repeat some words. She said they were trying to keep the loom workers' complaint separate from a demand to declare war on a whole Kul.

"Hamaare ghar mein bhi sab alag hain," she said. "Kaun kis darwaaze se andar jaaye?"

Vedant asked when the next local meeting would be held. She gave a time and said the authorities had been informed; the cooperative would send the notice through the verified contact.

Rudra looked towards his mother's bay. She was speaking quietly to Dadi. His father remained in treatment. His friends had returned to rest after the reviews. Every road out of this room began with something which could not simply be left behind.

Then the woman on the phone asked whether the four young people who had refused to be separated could ever work in separate places.

"Dono shehron mein zaroorat hai," she said. "Aap logon mein se kaun aa sakega?"
