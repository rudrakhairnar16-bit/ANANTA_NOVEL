# CHAPTER SIXTY-EIGHT — THE BANK THEY COULD REACH

---

> *"Afterwards they said the river stopped.*
> *We kept the engines running."*
>
> *— rescue launch log, witness correction added in the margin*

---

The woman was still asking where to step when the volunteer began repeating the rescue team's instruction across the water.

Stay on the walkway. Stop pushing towards the broken join. The boats were coming.

At the receiving post, Vedant asked the driver to put the official contact on the other phone. The two calls gave him different pieces of the same place: a visible gap at the far bank, a crowd at the near one, families turning between them because neither end offered a way off.

"Jo abhi udhar nahi pahunche, unko mat bhejna," he told the organizer. "Alternate road pe bhi pehle police se check."

The organizer went to stop the next group before it left the courtyard.

Veer had swung his good leg off the bed. The other stayed on its cushion until he saw Vedant look at it.

"Hum?"

"Yahin. Receiving mein zaroorat padegi. Clinician ko bula liya hai."

Rudra had not stood. His phone lay in his hand, the old fear of being left behind already making his wrist tense.

"Kitne log?"

"Abhi pakka nahi. Naam se poochho jab yahan aayen. Phone pe ginti banaake mat bhejna."

Vedant took his bag and went with the driver. A volunteer stayed with the boys while another cleared the post's shaded passage for people coming from the landing. The clinician was bringing supplies from the nearby room where the travel examinations had been done.

Nothing in the departure sounded like permission for a field fight. Veer put his knee back where it belonged and asked what could be moved without lifting.

The volunteer gave him the telephone.

---

The landing was two turns and a sloping lane from the post.

By the time the van reached the upper stopping place, an officer was clearing the lower stretch for the rescue vehicle. People coming to watch were sent back uphill. The van stayed above the steps so it would not take the space needed for a boat's passengers.

Vedant went down on foot. The driver carried the bag.

The temporary crossing ran across a narrow river channel towards a low opposite bank. Its walking boards rested on linked floats; handrails and bank lines held it in a shape which moved even when nobody walked. Near the far shore, the joining section had dropped away from its seat. One end lay slanting in the water.

At the near shore, people were still arguing about who could come off.

A length of rope had been put across the approach. Beside it, a man held a household list against a board.

"Yahan se udhar waale nahi—"

The officer took the rope off its post.

"Abhi sab utarenge. Peechhe."

The man caught the rope's loose end. A second constable took his elbow and moved him out of the approach. Neither the list nor the argument was allowed to remain between the rescue crew and the water.

The path was open.

The crossing was not safe to use merely because the man had let go.

---

The crowd on the floats began to move when it saw the rope fall.

Too many at once.

A rail dipped. Someone shouted that the bank was open and people behind pressed harder, not yet seeing how the joined boards twisted beneath those in front.

The rescue lead called through a loudhailer. A worker already on the crossing repeated him, spreading his arms to stop the next rush. Another worker made the people nearest the damaged join sit where they were instead of testing the loose section with a foot.

The near joining boards had slewed sideways under the change of weight. The crew checked them and refused to send a fresh crowd across the narrow contact that remained.

Boats would take the people off the steadier middle sections. Nobody was to walk out and prove the crossing strong with their own body.

Vedant listened to the boatmen describe the approach. The channel's current struck the upstream floats and came round underneath them. A boat needed to be held clear while people stepped down; it could not simply be tied to the rail and trusted to stay level.

He asked where a spare pair of hands would help.

The boatman looked at the forearm which Vedant still moved carefully.

"Uthaana mat. Baithe rehna. Jo bolo woh pehle bataana."

Vedant nodded. A crew member gave him a life jacket and checked its fastening before he boarded. The crew chose the route and the load. He would not be steering a boat by imagining a calmer river.

---

From the bank, the woman who had asked where to step looked smaller than she had sounded through the phone.

She stood beyond the crowd's centre, one arm round a child, the other holding the rail. An older man sat near her feet, holding out folded money towards the worker beside him.

The worker shook his head and pointed to the rescue jacket, then waited until the man looked at the boat.

Vedant could not hear the words over the motor.

He could see how long it took the older man to put the money away.

---

The boatman brought the bow beside the steadier section, not beneath the broken join.

A rescuer stepped onto the walkway in his safety gear. He spoke to the woman first, then to the older man. The crew fitted jackets and chose who would come down together. Nobody was told to jump for a hand reaching from a moving boat.

The older man showed the folded money again.

"Kisne bola paisa lagega?" the rescuer asked.

He pointed towards the near bank. The same bank which had finally let a rope be removed.

"Yeh rescue hai," the man told him. "Pehle aaiye."

The woman asked whether her father could go in the same boat. The crew made room for the three of them together. Her child would not be passed out over the rail to a stranger while she waited to be chosen later.

Vedant kept the landing space clear inside the boat. He left the lifting to the men with the position and strength for it.

The first movement went well. The child settled where the rescuer put him, angry at the jacket under his chin. His mother followed, talking quietly until he stopped reaching for the buckle.

Her father rose last.

---

The floats moved under a shift farther along the crowd.

A worker shouted. The boatman corrected. For a moment the boat and walkway were no longer moving together.

The older man's knee gave a little as he reached the edge. He caught the rescuer's shoulder. The rescuer took the weight, but his free foot had not found the boat's floor.

Water struck between the hull and the nearest float.

Vedant lowered his good hand towards the wash beside him.

Not the current out in the channel. The short tongue of water lifting between two hard surfaces, where another blow would close the gap against the man's leg.

He turned its face along the hull.

Water flattened, struck the float sideways and ran past. The boatman had time for one correction. The rescuer got his foot down and brought the older man onto the seat.

Vedant let the shape fall at once.

The next wash came normally. The crew had already moved clear.

His reassessed forearm had tightened with the effort of bracing himself. He kept it against his body instead of offering another hand to steady the man who was already seated.

"Bas?" the boatman asked.

"Bas."

The man looked at the water, then back at Vedant's face.

"Agli baar pehle bolna."

Vedant nodded. It was not a rebuke he had earned the right to ignore because the intervention had helped.

---

At the near landing, the woman refused to let her father step off first and vanish among the hands waiting for him.

The rescuer told the receiving volunteer where to keep the family together. Two people helped the older man up the lower steps. His daughter carried the child after the jacket was removed by the crew.

The man with the household list had been moved uphill beside an officer. He called to her as she passed.

"Naam likhwaake jaana. Baad mein ghar ka mat bolna."

She stopped for half a step.

The volunteer beside her said the receiving post was open and the officer would take any threat there as well. She did not have to go back to the list-holder.

She walked on. Her father had already turned his head at the familiar voice and needed reminding which way the path went.

Vedant stayed at the landing for the next arrivals. His arm would not help another boat as much as an accurate receiving report would.

The crew went back without him.

---

Across the water, people began believing the boats would return.

That belief made the worker's job easier and harder. Some sat where they were asked. Others stood too soon when a bow came near, trying not to be the last family visible against the darkening bank.

The rescue lead changed the pickup point when a float began riding badly under the weight. The crew did not argue with the water to preserve the first plan. They kept the crowd away from that section and worked from the next stable part.

One woman had a cut from the rail. A man needed help after the crush had left him unable to put weight through one foot. Medical staff at the landing were told before either arrived at the upper lane.

The empty section twisted farther once its people were off. A rail dropped into the channel and struck a float with a hollow clang.

Nobody ran back to catch it.

The crossing would not be reopened when the last boat left. Officials were already closing both approaches to new traffic while the landing remained open for rescue.

Vedant sent that distinction to the post. A closed crossing was not an instruction to turn away the people who had just escaped it.

Then the volunteer at the top of the steps called for him. An arriving family had been stopped again, this time by men who said they needed to record which side the boats had brought back.

---

At the receiving post, Rudra was telling the volunteer where to leave a clear path between the shaded seats and the clinician's room.

Not where to put people. That was the clinician's work.

Veer answered the landing call and passed on the report about the injured foot. He wrote the time on the loose sheet, slowly, with the hand which did not need the sling. His mother had asked him to call after his own review; the message waiting on his phone still said only that he had reached safely.

The woman from the boat came in with her father and child.

The older man looked at the table where another volunteer was taking names.

"Pehle yahan?"

"Pehle doctor," Rudra said. "Baad mein aap khud bataa dena."

A helper took them towards the room. The child had stopped fighting the jacket because the crew had already taken it back for the next passengers. Now he held his mother's sleeve as though it too might have to be returned.

One of the men waiting in the lane came through behind them, carrying a bamboo staff. He said he had come to help the older man walk.

The older man did not take it.

---

The man followed the family past the names table.

"Inko mere saath bhej dena," he told the volunteer. "Hamaare hain. Entry hamaari taraf hogi."

The woman stopped at the clinician's door.

"Mujhe pehle inko dikhaana hai."

"Dikhaa lo. Phir udhar chalna padega."

The volunteer asked whether the family wanted anyone contacted. The woman gave a relative's name. It was not the man standing behind her.

He looked towards the courtyard, where people arriving from the landing could hear.

"Kal apne mohalle mein kaise jaaogi? Inke paas rahogi ab?"

He pointed at the boys.

Rudra looked at her, not at the pointing hand.

"Kisi ko phone karna hai toh kar denge," he said. "Kahan jaana hai, aap bolo."

The man turned towards him.

"Tum decide karoge?"

"Nahi. Woh karengi."

The woman's hand tightened on the child's sleeve. She said she would wait for the clinician and the relative she had named.

For a moment the man stood still.

Then he caught her elbow.

---

The helper stepped between them.

The man shoved him away with the staff across his chest. A stool fell. The older man tried to move and could not get clear quickly enough.

Veer's small curve rose from his right forearm.

It caught the returning staff above the table edge. Not the whole pole, not the man's body. Veer angled the blow down into the empty strip beside his seat.

Bamboo struck paving. His knee jerked when he braced. Pain caught under the ribs and the sling pulled at the shoulder.

He could not keep the curve where the next swing would be.

"Peechhe!" he told the family.

The helper got the older man against the wall, away from the fallen stool. The clinician came out of the room and drew the woman and child in behind him.

The man yanked the staff back. Its end caught the light wooden screen which separated the waiting seats from the treatment door. The screen tipped towards the older man's legs.

Rudra caught its broad edge against his forearm.

His injured foot stayed on its support. The wrist brace took a strain it had not been cleared to take. He felt heat answer under his sleeve, an unwanted rush towards the shape he was not trying to summon.

One breath.

Another rhythm struck across it before he could settle the next.

Gold burned at his collar. He pushed the screen sideways just far enough for the helper to take its weight.

Then he let go.

---

The courtyard guard and two volunteers reached the man together.

The staff went down. An officer coming up from the landing took him outside while the clinician ordered the waiting space cleared around the injured men, not around the argument.

Veer's curve was gone. He was holding the side of his knee with his good hand, trying not to cough against the ache beneath his ribs.

Rudra touched the collar with two fingers. They came away wet.

He looked at the red on them without understanding for a moment why the skin which had closed so patiently could be open again.

The clinician asked him not to keep touching it.

---

Vedant came back to a courtyard where a volunteer was putting the fallen stool out of use.

The clinician gave him the facts first. He looked from Veer's knee to the pad being held beside Rudra's neck, then at the empty place where the family had been waiting.

"Unko?"

"Andar hain," the volunteer said. "Relative ko call ho gaya. Police account le rahi hai."

Vedant put his bag down with the arm which did not hurt.

At the landing, the last boats were coming in. A crew had checked the empty walkway before closing off the damaged sections. Nobody was left visible on the boards. The landing team was still matching families and receiving accounts; it would not turn that report into a guess about everyone who had ever approached the crossing.

Inside the post, the woman sat beside her father while her child slept against her.

She had reached a bank.

She had still needed someone to stop a hand reaching after her from it.

---

The clinician kept the boys in the examination room after the family had been moved to a quieter part of the post.

Rudra's bleeding stopped under a clean pad. The skin had opened along a short part of the old injury, not along every place he could remember the gold. The clinician dressed what he could examine. He asked about the heat, the breathlessness, the movement against the brace and the pain afterward without treating any of Rudra's answers as a reason to omit the ordinary checks.

The wrist needed another assessment. So did Veer's knee. His shoulder had pulled when the sling moved, and deep breaths still hurt in the familiar place. The clinician arranged a fuller review rather than asking either boy to prove the joints worked across the small room.

Their evening duty was over.

Vedant went next. His forearm had not opened, but he had aggravated it. He let the clinician finish before answering the call from the landing.

"Koi reh toh nahi gaya?"

The rescue lead reported the completed section check. Accounts from the people brought off were still being compared with the two approaches. The post would be told if a family reported somebody missing.

He also asked Vedant for his account of the change beside the boat. The boatman had included it in his report.

Vedant gave the size and the duration as honestly as he could. He included the warning he had failed to give.

The man on the other end thanked him for helping and repeated that the crew needed to know before the water beside its hull changed.

Both things could be true.

---

The helper who had taken the staff across his chest was examined too. His breath came more easily once the first fear passed, but the clinician kept him for checks and told another volunteer to take his place.

The woman from the boat gave her account to the officer in the adjoining room. He asked whether she knew the man who had caught her arm.

"Jaanti hoon. Isliye darr lag raha tha."

She lived in rooms beyond the far bank. The man had collected the private protection money in the lane before. Tonight he had told people at the near approach which household queue they belonged in. She had tried to turn back when the arguments grew; by then the far joining section had begun to move.

The officer asked what she had actually seen at that end.

"Takhte jhuke. Kaise chhoote, nahi dekha."

He read the distinction back to her before writing further. Her father remembered a jolt and people moving against him. Neither account made the man with the staff the person who had loosened the crossing.

The list-holder's obstruction, his later threat, and the staff assault had witnesses of their own. The police did not need to settle a different cause first to act on those.

Her relative answered the phone. He was on the longer road and would come when the police confirmed the approach was open. She asked him not to walk down to the broken crossing in search of them.

Then she borrowed the volunteer's phone again and called a neighbour about the room.

---

In Mirzapur, Sitara took the call outside so Aadhya would not try to sit up before she knew who it was.

Aadhya heard Prayagraj and sat up anyway. The fresh dressing at her left hand caught against the sheet. She stopped, freed it slowly with help and waited.

Ira lay on the next bed with her eye covering in place. The room's lamp faced the wall. Her ordinary right-eye ache had eased in the dimness; the clinician's instructions had not changed because easing felt like forgiveness.

"Nadi?" Aadhya asked when Sitara returned.

"Channel ki crossing. Boats se nikaala. Abhi post pe checks hain."

Sitara told them about the boys' new strain, the collar dressing and Vedant's arm. Rudra could speak for a short while once the clinician had finished. There was no journey for the girls to prepare and no suggestion that their own reviews could wait.

Aadhya looked at the two bowls on the shelf.

"Usne khaaya?"

"Abhi laa rahe hain."

"Garam cheez phone pe bolte-bolte thandi kar deta hai."

Sitara passed that on. Across the distance, Rudra made a small sound which might have become a laugh on another evening.

"Tumne?" he asked.

"Khaaya. Haath bhi check hua. Subah phir dekhenge."

"Wapas mat jaana kal."

"Tum bhi post ka darwaaza mat banna."

He said nothing for a moment.

She had meant a joke gently enough to offer him somewhere to put his fear. Once spoken, it had found the thing both of them had done: stayed where a door was falling because someone else still needed to pass.

"Theek," he said at last. "Darwaaze ke paas aur log rakhenge."

She let him end the call first. Then she asked Sitara to move the bowls lower. Wanting to help him was no reason to make somebody catch one from her numb hand tonight.

---

By the time food came, the courtyard had been arranged for people to wait without sitting in the lane.

The broken screen stood against a wall with its loose foot tied up. A volunteer put a bench across the space it had failed to protect, leaving the treatment door visible from the entrance. People still needed to get through. They did not need to pass an argument to reach the clinician.

The officer asked the organizer to keep a worker at that entrance and call the stationed constable if anybody tried to take a family out against its wishes. The staff lay outside in police hands. It would not be returned as a walking aid before the accounts were taken.

Veer ate with his knee raised again.

The man at the household list had recognized him. So had the man with the staff. Being recognized had not made either listen. Veer pushed the rice together and looked at the place where the helper had been struck.

"Mujhe phone hi diya tha," he said.

Vedant sat beside him.

"Diya tha."

"Main sambhaal nahi paaya."

"Tumhari wajah se woh second hit nahi laga. Aur tum ab review pe jaaoge. Dono likhenge."

Veer took another mouthful before it could become an argument about which sentence counted more.

---

The woman from the boat would not eat until her father had somewhere to sit with his back supported.

A volunteer found a chair. The old man opened the packet placed in his lap and tried to give half to the child, who was still asleep. His daughter wrapped that part again.

Rudra sat where the clinician had left him. He asked whether her relative had reached.

"Raaste pe hain. Udhar se bhi ghoom ke aana hai."

She held out the borrowed phone so he could see the failed call beneath the latest answered one. Her own was in a bag at home. She had left to collect her father from a visit on the near side and expected to be back before evening.

The child woke enough to ask for his sandals. One had loosened in the boat. It had been brought in separately and now waited under the chair, small and muddy and unremarkable enough to make Rudra's throat tighten.

The woman put it on him before answering another question.

"Ghar jaa sakenge?" Rudra asked.

She looked at the pad beside his collar, then back towards the lane.

"Aapko kya bola unhone?"

"Crossing band hai. Longer road check kar rahe hain."

"Road mil bhi jaaye toh."

She did not finish. The phone rang in her hand.

---

Other families were asking to leave as well. Some had a checked address on this side. Some needed transport to relatives farther away. Two women asked whether staying the night would put their names on the list they had refused at the crossing.

The organizer brought the public relief board inside and sat beside it, explaining the post's record. Contact details for finding somebody were not a household affiliation. A person could ask a volunteer to write for them, or wait until the family member they trusted arrived. Treatment would not be held behind a signature.

The district contact found places at a staffed municipal hall reached by the upper road. The receiving workers there had to confirm space before a van left. The post could keep the people waiting in the meantime; it could not promise every person a bed merely because it had a roof.

One man wanted to stay near the water for news of the crossing. A worker showed him the landing contact and offered to call from the hall. He went to find his wife before deciding.

The decisions took time. Nobody packed the rescued people into a vehicle as though getting them out of this courtyard would complete the rescue.

Rudra told the organizer what the woman had said about the room. Vedant asked for the police contact rather than the van keys.

---

The neighbour's first words were difficult to hear. The woman moved towards the examination room's open door, where the courtyard voices fell away.

Her father called after her. She raised a hand to show she was staying close.

Two men had come to the common gate, the neighbour said. They said people brought back through the near-bank rescue had placed themselves on the wrong side. The households would have to be checked before anyone was allowed in.

"Mera kamra andar hai," she said.

The neighbour knew. He had seen her leave that afternoon.

She asked him to fetch the small tin from the shelf above the cot. Her father's evening medicines were there. He had brought only what he expected to need during the visit.

The neighbour's voice dropped.

The officer came over at her signal. He took the location from her, called for a local unit to attend the gate and asked the neighbour not to confront the men. The clinician went to check the prescription in the old man's papers while another worker arranged what could safely be supplied until the tin was recovered.

The woman put her hand into her blouse pocket and took out a key on a red cotton loop.

The child reached for it, awake now.

Then, through the phone, came the sound of metal striking metal.

The neighbour had stepped back into his own doorway. The men were fastening a new hasp across the common gate.

"Subah list leke aana," someone said beside him. "Aaj kisi ko andar nahi lena."

The woman closed her hand over the key before her son could take it.

Behind the gate were their medicines, their clothes and the bed she had promised her son they would reach tonight.
