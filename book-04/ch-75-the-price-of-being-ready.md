# CHAPTER SEVENTY-FIVE — THE PRICE OF BEING READY

---

> *"The appearance had been called harmless.*
> *The man who missed the next month's wages did not remember appearing.*
> *He remembered putting his hand down."*
>
> *— testimony attached to the tournament's first medical review*

---

The edge of the wrist support struck the earth before the porter's palm did.

It kept the hand from turning one way. It could not keep his falling weight from travelling through it.

His elbow folded.

He made a short sound which Veer heard above the crowd, then lay on his side with the protected wrist drawn against his stomach. The little blue band had caught beneath his sleeve.

Red let his guard go completely. He took one step towards the fallen man and stopped when the referee put an arm across his path.

"Haath mat kheenchna. Jagah do."

The medical helper was already through the rope. The clinician came by the officials' strip with the examination bag. Nobody needed an announcer to turn the fall into a serious enough event before they could attend it.

The bell stayed silent.

---

The clinician knelt where the porter could see him.

He asked about the pain, whether his head had hit, whether anything else hurt. The man answered in bursts. His wrist. His wrist again. His head had not struck; he was certain of that much.

The clinician checked before accepting that certainty as the whole examination. He kept the injured arm supported while the helper opened the strap carefully. The porter's fingers moved when asked, but the attempt made him shut his eyes.

"Bas. Ab khud se mat ghumaana."

Red stood outside their working space, one empty hand still held where the guard had been. The referee made him lower it.

A helper brought the low stool. The clinician did not use it to hurry the porter upright. He wanted the arm secured for the journey and further assessment where imaging could be done.

The wrist protector had not made the introduction safe. Taking it off now would not make the injury go back to what it had been before the bell.

---

On the verandah, Veer sat with the walking support beside his good hand.

The knee had settled after the aborted rise. His shoulder had not liked even that much. He could see the porter's face between the people working below and hated how easy it was to imagine a version in which he had got there first.

That version had no weight in its limbs. No rope between the chair and the ring. No injured man beneath the other injured man who might fall trying to save him.

He called to the helper at the steps instead.

"Medical waali gaadi ko jagah hai?"

The helper went to the outer lane. The vehicle arranged for Veer's return remained clear of the entrance; a separate clinical transport was being called. There was room for both if the waiting spectators did not spread into it.

Veer asked the announcer to make that request, not tell the crowd that everything was fine.

The loudspeaker crackled. People moved reluctantly. A father took his child's hand and led him away from the rope before the adults behind them tried to get a better view.

---

The porter wanted to sit before they carried him out.

The clinician helped him change position only after the arm was secured and the rest of the examination allowed it. The helper stayed at his side. He did not have to walk across the whole akhada holding the wrist up for the people who had paid to watch guards.

Vedant asked whom they should call.

The man gave a number, then asked him not to shout it towards the registration table. Vedant stepped back to make the call. The companion who answered was close enough to meet them at the receiving clinic; the clinician gave the destination and the reason for going there.

"Ghar pe abhi?" Vedant asked afterward.

"Bata do. Gir gaya. Haath dekh rahe hain."

No one promised the family it was only the old strain.

As they brought him towards the exit, Red moved alongside for a moment.

"Maine suna tha bas shape milaana—"

The porter looked up at him.

"Maine nahi bola tha."

The clinician asked that the explanation wait until the patient had been received. Red stopped. Whatever he had been told at his corner, he would not make the man answer it while being taken for care.

---

The referee closed the ring.

Not just the scored bouts. No further introductions, salutes or paired photographs with live shapes. The grounds helper put the entry board across the waiting gap while the medical table retained the actual names and papers.

An assembly helper began explaining that Blue had already had a number.

The referee held out his hand for the copy.

"Number ne haan nahi boli."

He laid it flat on the table, away from the medical notes. Then he asked both corners to stay available for their own accounts, separately, after the immediate care arrangements were complete.

From his chair, Veer watched the small blue band being folded beside the discarded protector and put with the porter's things.

It had held perfectly through the fall.

It was the wrong thing to have trusted.

---

Veer's return vehicle left before the ring reopened.

A post volunteer came with the driver to help him through the side passage. Vedant checked the handover and gave the post clinician the short account before accompanying the porter in the clinical transport. He would call after the receiving team had made its assessment.

Veer used the support. At the outer door he paused, impatient with the distance and grateful that nobody clapped for him when he finished it.

The driver did not ask whether he had enjoyed the morning until they were clear of the lane.

"Pehla match achchha tha," Veer said.

He left the answer there. It remained true. The fall remained true beside it.

At the post, the clinician checked the knee and shoulder. Veer told him about the first movement towards standing, the catch and the decision to sit back. There was no reward for leaving that out.

The rest interval was lengthened. The morning's permission had ended; another day would need another decision.

---

At the receiving clinic, the porter asked the examination nurse whether the old note had arrived.

It had. The clinician read it with the account of the fall, then examined what was in front of him. An earlier strain did not prevent a different injury from happening to the same wrist.

Imaging showed a small fracture near the wrist. It was not displaced on the views they had taken. That did not mean he could carry with it or try the little arc again to see whether the pain belonged to bone or shape.

The arm was immobilized for the swelling and the next review. He was given treatment for pain, a follow-up time and clear instructions about changes which meant returning sooner. The person he had chosen to call listened too, then repeated the plan back before the nurse let them leave the examination space.

Vedant waited outside for that part. The patient had not asked for every visitor to hear every instruction concerning his body.

When the porter called him in, the first question was about the protector.

"Woh waala kaam aayega?"

Vedant looked at the old cloth and stiff support in the bag. The clinician answered. The new immobilization had its own purpose and fit; the old one was not to be put back on by guesswork.

The porter nodded slowly.

He had bought the old support so that he could return to lifting sooner. It had become one more thing he could not afford to have bought for the wrong job.

---

The club organizer arrived after the examination, not in place of it.

He brought the porter's things and the money promised for the introduction. The porter watched the notes being placed on the chair beside his good hand.

"Pura?"

"Jo bola tha."

"Jeeta nahi."

"Uska nahi hai."

The demonstration had begun. The allowance had been earned. It would not be withheld because the man had fallen before the crowd got the display it wanted.

The organizer also confirmed that the immediate transport and assessment charges were being met from the event's expense reserve. The purse board would be corrected if the remaining programme had to shrink. Competitors who had already earned their payments would not have them quietly taken back.

A reserve was not a month's wages. Nobody called it one.

The porter asked whether he had to sign that the fall was his fault to receive the money.

"Nahi," the organizer said.

He moved the payment receipt away from the separate account form. The man would get his copy and could give his account when the pain allowed. Taking treatment would not settle the unanswered question of who had sent him into that ring under which instructions.

---

The chosen companion had to return to work before evening.

The porter did not want him to lose the whole day as well. They called another person who could help at home for the first part of the night. The clinic checked the ordinary journey and assistance he needed; a smaller vehicle could take him and the companion without using the medical service for an unrelated delivery.

The organizer made the arrangement. Vedant gave the current post number, then handed the practical accompaniment over before his own forearm began paying for another long day of carrying other people's bags.

The porter stopped him at the doorway.

"Us din gate pe jo kiya tha—"

"Woh badla nahi."

The man looked down at the newly supported arm.

"Yeh bhi likhna. Aaj main khud aaya tha. Par jo kaha tha, woh bhi."

Vedant said the account would contain both.

The porter had chosen to seek money. He had not chosen every use somebody else might make of that need. Neither truth had to be erased to let the other stand.

Outside, the clinic attendant helped the family find the vehicle. Vedant remained until the supported arm was settled and the return instructions were within reach. Then he went back to the post, leaving a qualified team and a chosen companion in charge of the patient he was not required to own.

---

At the akhada, the first person to leave was a contestant whose afternoon lift home would not wait.

He told his corner, collected the kit he had brought and asked what withdrawing did to his registration payment. The steward checked the entry rather than calling him afraid of an honest contest.

The men who had shouted loudest for the programme to restart did not offer him a ride.

Two other competitors sat beneath the verandah sharing a steel tiffin. The winning woman from the morning kept her dressed palm off the hot lid. White opened it for her, then ate most of the pickle while she was finding a spoon.

"Haar ke bhi itna?" she asked.

"Iska point nahi kata tha."

She pulled the little pickle compartment towards her with the good hand.

They had another match to watch and, for one of them, another match to enter. Neither task required becoming an emblem for every person born to the same Kul.

---

The referee heard Red's account at the empty scoring table.

The assembly helper had shown him an opening with both forearms. Red had understood that the two little guards were to meet. He demonstrated the empty-handed angle now, slowly, away from anyone else's body.

The helper said he had meant a display of the positions. He had not repeated the porter's stated limit at the other corner.

The referee asked why he himself had treated the entry copy as an answer to the medical question.

Nobody supplied a satisfactory reason for him.

There was no hidden line on the paper granting a harmless kind of contact. He had not seen actual clearance. The porter had spoken close to him, and he had still let the assembly continue while turning to ask someone else.

Red's release after the stop belonged in the account. So did the contact which had already happened. The helper's gesture did not become the patient's consent merely because it had been intended as a shortcut.

The referee asked a different official to handle the resumed ring while his own part was reviewed.

He did not leave the akhada. The organizer still needed him to answer questions.

---

A replacement clinician arrived after lunch and received the medical handover before anyone called the next pair.

The event's organizers removed paired introductions from the day's programme. Only an actual released entrant could be sent from the waiting side to a corner; both contestants would hear the same rules together. The ring official would ask them, not take an assembly helper's moving hand as two agreements.

The replacement did not reopen the ring because those words had been spoken. He checked the medical arrangements, the clear route and the current entrants before giving his own answer.

One club leader complained that his people were ready.

The woman with the dressed palm looked up from her tiffin.

"Toh unse poochho."

Her corner companion closed the empty pickle compartment. The waiting athletes were close enough to hear.

Some stayed. Some asked new questions. One man removed the corner band he had been given before the pause and waited for his actual turn rather than standing in the entrance to look like an answer.

---

In Mirzapur, Aadhya was trying to fold a small cloth with one supported hand.

The woman helping with the laundry had offered to finish it. Aadhya had asked for the light piece to be left with her, and now discovered that a handkerchief could display a surprising amount of independence on a flat table.

One corner escaped. She stopped before trying to pin it with the numb fingers of the other hand.

Ira listened to the small exasperated sound.

"Seedha ho gaya?"

"Kapda haan. Main tedhi hoon."

"Toh aaj uska din hai."

Aadhya laughed and asked for the loose corner to be brought within reach. She folded only that piece. The heavier laundry went with the woman, as agreed; an ordinary success did not have to be enlarged until it hurt enough to resemble work.

Sitara told them about the porter after their rest interval. The injury was confirmed, care and a journey arranged. They were not being asked for a demonstration which could somehow pay his wages or repair his wrist from another city.

Ira asked whether the public account carried his household's address. It did not. Aadhya wanted to know whether the companion at home had the instructions, not merely a bag with a new support in it.

Sitara had the answer to that part. They had read the plan back at the clinic.

---

The girls sent a short message for Veer and Rudra separately.

For Veer: they had heard he was back at the post. He need not give a better version of his own knee because somebody else had got hurt.

For Rudra: tell them one ordinary thing from the room, not another promise that the next review would explain everything.

His answer came after the nurse's round.

The replacement charger plug did not fit the socket beside his bed; the volunteer had found the working one without moving the bed or putting him in the passage.

Aadhya listened to it twice for the pleasure of a difficulty which somebody had actually solved.

---

The woman who had won the morning's first bout asked for her afternoon examination before taking a fresh corner band.

The dressing came off in the medical room, not on the public platform. The scrape at the heel of the palm was shallow but still tender. She answered the questions about the forearm and the movement she had used to tighten the runner. The clinician checked the actual hand and the rest of what the morning had cost her.

A protected, limited control bout was possible if she did not plant or load that palm. Any pain or change would stop it. The clinician discussed what that removed from her choices, not only what it left on the schedule.

She asked whether using the other hand to touch counted as breaking the limit.

It did not. Throwing her weight onto the dressed hand to recover from a bad turn would.

She took time with that distinction.

The small grass strip was still there. It would not be her answer today if getting to it meant the old easy palm drop. She could withdraw with the morning's win and payment intact.

She chose the one bout, heard the limit repeated and asked her corner to stop her if she tried to forget it in a hurry.

---

Her next opponent wore yellow at his corner.

The announcer gave the entry number and the Rakshak club he represented. He was not White from the opening match or Red from the introduction. A narrow scar at his chin whitened when he tightened his jaw to fit the mouthguard; it was an ordinary old mark, not a sign Veer or the officials could read as a history.

The man checked both shoulder tabs before warming up. No guard appeared during the first few steps.

Green watched from the waiting bench.

White sat beside her and held the water while she adjusted her dressing with the uninjured hand. He did not offer a plan in exchange for opening the bottle.

"Pehla dekha hai isne," he said.

"Maine bhi."

"Uska?"

She shook her head towards the ring.

"Apna. Do baar wahi karungi toh uska kya dosh?"

White smiled and gave her the bottle.

---

At the post, Vedant finally ate the meal which had been covered for him.

The forearm stayed supported between the small ordinary movements he had been cleared to make. He gave the next caller to the organizer and told Veer when he would be available again. The akhada had its replacement clinician, officials and exit plan. It did not need him standing in another doorway to remain real.

The man arranging the broadcast for one club offered Veer an ordinary phone call during Green's bout. One match, from the public side, with the corner's agreement. The stream for his own club would continue without Veer. He was not being recruited to guarantee every later result.

Veer asked for sound rather than trying to hold a small moving picture close while his knee and shoulder were settling. The volunteer propped the phone within reach. The call might fail; somebody could tell him the result later.

Before it started, he sent Rudra the account of the morning's first match and the injury update the porter had agreed could be shared.

"Tu kya kar raha hai?" he asked when Rudra called back.

"Ruk raha hoon. Bada boring kaam hai."

"Maine kaha tha. Log baithne ka point nahi dete."

Rudra's laugh was softer than the morning's. The nurse was coming for the next check. He said so and let the call end.

---

In the observation bay, the earlier drawings lay inside a plain folder.

Rudra did not take them out to compare his chest by memory. He asked the nurse to put the folder where the doctor would see it on the next round. Then he watched the ordinary clip being removed from his finger.

Outside the curtain, somebody was arguing about a lost sandal. Somebody else had found a pair which was not theirs.

He had been in enough hospitals now to know that fear did not arrive with its own silence. It lay among visiting hours, changed bedsheets and the scrape of a stool, sometimes large enough to hide all of them until a stranger's complaint about footwear returned him abruptly to a world which had other things to lose.

He wanted to be at the akhada. He wanted the doctor to say that wanting it was the only difficulty remaining.

Instead, he asked when he could make the next call home.

---

At the ring, the new referee called Green and Yellow together.

He showed both the chalk line and the stop signal. They repeated the targets and the first-to-three rule. Green confirmed her own movement restriction; she did not have to announce the contents of her medical note to the spectators.

Yellow listened without offering to make the match easy.

When they returned to their corners, Green flexed the free fingers once and left the dressed palm open at her side.

---

Yellow did not open a guard at the bell.

He came off the mark with both hands visible, one a little higher than the other. Green waited for the pale curve which ought to explain where he meant to put his weight.

He gave her a step instead.

Not straight towards either shoulder. Across the front of her leading foot, close enough to make her turn to keep the dressed palm out of his path.

She turned.

His free hand came up. She moved the near shoulder away and found the chalk behind the heel before she had found his other hand.

The whole foot crossed.

The line judge's arm went up. The referee stopped them with a clean gap still between their bodies.

One–nil, yellow.

He had scored without raising a guard.

---

At the post, Veer heard the point before he heard how it had happened.

The man holding the call explained the foot beyond the line. Veer asked whether there had been a shove. There had not. Yellow had offered a shoulder touch she chose to avoid, and the room she had left herself had run out.

Veer looked at his own foot on its support.

From a bed, it was easy to give an athlete an extra half-step which the earth had not provided.

He asked the caller to tell him where the next exchange began. He would not invent the missing space because he liked the woman who had won the first match.

---

Green walked back to the centre.

She did not test the dressed hand against her thigh or ask the corner to tighten the bandage until it felt like another palm. White held the bottle up. She shook her head, then pointed with her good hand to the place where Yellow's first crossing step had landed.

Her corner saw it.

So had she, now.

At the reset, she stood a little farther from the living strip and kept the dressed hand close enough to protect without turning that whole side of her body away.

Yellow showed her the crossing step again.

She let him have the space in front of the foot. Her own step went back towards the centre, not along the retreat he had just measured. When his hand came up for the expected shoulder, hers was already travelling towards the opposite tab.

The pale curve appeared.

Small. Close. Late enough that she could not treat it as a wall which had always stood there.

She stopped the touch before driving her fingers into its face, shifted her weight and waited for the hand behind it to change angle.

He did not change it.

For a moment they stood within reach, both knowing exactly which movement the other wanted.

Then Green left the apparent opening entirely. She passed to his outside with the shoulder he had been trying to count and touched the rear edge of his other tab as he turned.

The referee stepped between them.

One each.

---

White let out a shout from the corner, then looked almost embarrassed by how much he had enjoyed somebody doing to another guard what she had done to his.

Green came back breathing harder. She accepted the bottle this time. The dressed palm remained open and dry above the wrist band. The good fingers trembled a little after the stopped attempt at the curve.

The clinician asked the corner a short question. Green answered it herself. No new pain in the dressed hand; she had kept it off the contact. The official waited for the clinician's nod before calling the reset.

Nobody announced that the question proved her weak.

Yellow rolled the holding shoulder once without bringing the guard back up. His corner spoke close to him. He listened, then nodded towards the strip of grass without pretending the woman was required to use it.

The next exchange belonged to what either of them could actually do from here.

---

In Mirzapur, the girls' lamp was being moved nearer the wall so the next rest interval would not begin with light in Ira's usable eye.

Aadhya asked the woman helping them to leave the little folded cloth on the table. She liked looking at it finished, even if the corners were not exact.

The woman left it and went to her own supper.

Neither girl knew the score at the akhada. They had agreed to hear it later. There were ordinary limits to a day which did not have to become a failure of friendship.

---

Yellow raised the little curve before the next bell.

Green saw the difference. The thing which had arrived late at her fingertips now offered itself early, one pale face between his nearer shoulder and the approach she had just used.

She came towards it on short steps.

He moved back.

She followed on the same short steps, dressed palm clear, using the free hand to make him turn the curve. Behind his heel, the chalk drew nearer. A shout from the standing row urged her to go through it.

White told her to keep her feet.

The curve tilted. Its far edge opened a narrow way towards the shoulder tab. Green put the good hand beside that edge and began the step which would carry the shoulder past it.

Yellow waited until the step had left the earth.

He stepped sideways along the inside of the boundary and released the shape.

What she had planned to turn against was suddenly empty air. Her good hand travelled farther than the guard had allowed, pulling her upper body after it while the next foot was still looking for a place to land.

The dressed palm started to open at her side.

Green caught that instinct before the hand went down.

The chalk lay under the foot she had not yet put down.
