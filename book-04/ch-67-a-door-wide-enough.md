# CHAPTER SIXTY-SEVEN — A DOOR WIDE ENOUGH

---

> *"The cloth was entered as damaged stock.*
> *The space it bought was not entered anywhere."*
>
> *— cooperative loss statement, corrected after the first inspection*

---

By morning, the chain was in police custody. The marks it had made were still on the gate.

The officers had come during the night. They had made the goods vehicle clear the access, stopped the new padlocks being fitted and stayed until the night workers could leave. The complaint about the levy remained open. Nobody had given either claimant a title to the court because he arrived with a chain.

The local officer confirmed the morning's arrangements before Sitara agreed to leave the clinic. The public inspection would go ahead. Two constables would be present while the department representative heard the workers. The girls would sit in the welfare office, take part briefly and return to rest.

Aadhya had slept badly. Ira had slept with the notebook still unopened beside her bag. Neither was sent out to finish the argument the police had interrupted overnight.

At the cooperative, the receiving woman showed them where the chain had scraped the paint.

"Nikaal diya, phir bhi log sign karke aaye hain," she said.

The first working household to arrive had brought a thumbprinted acceptance of the protection levy. The man who carried it asked whether he could take the acceptance back.

His wife stood beside him, holding their rent receipts.

"Raat ko bachche andar the," she said. "Tab haan bola."

Ira asked the representative to keep those words with the paper. A thumbprint did not describe the room in which a frightened person had given it.

The man lowered the document onto the table as carefully as if it might break.

---

The loading gate opened from the street into a rectangular court.

Along the left side stood the long weaving shed. Finished cloth was kept in a separate store at the back. The welfare office ran along the right wall, ending short of the store. A low ramp faced the court. Its rear door opened beside the store into a service path which continued along the back wall to the residential strip.

The receiving woman took Sitara along that path before the meeting began; Ira listened while the way out was described, then walked the short distance with help rather than trying to memorize a place she had never seen.

Against the office's rear wall stood a folding timber drying frame, hung with lengths brought in from the open court before the night's threat. A woman moved it nearer the wall to widen the service path. Beside its foot, a creeper grew from a strip of soil where drain water had kept the ground damp. Its lower stem had been tied loosely to the frame to keep it off the walking line.

Aadhya noticed the knot because the stem had room to thicken inside it.

The woman who tended it put her watering tin down and went back to the meeting.

---

They were given chairs near the office's rear door, out of the central crush of people coming to speak.

Sitara stayed beside them. The receiving woman kept the front entrance clear. In the weaving shed, the morning shift was finishing work which could not be abandoned without losing another day's pay. The machine operator knew the inspection was underway; representatives came from the floor in turns.

The department representative began with the tenancy and work arrangements. Who paid rent. Who received wages. Who had authority to collect which charge. None of those questions required a family to choose a hereditary side.

The men demanding the levy arrived after the first two workers had spoken.

One wore a narrow pale band at his wrist. Another had a green thread tied above his elbow. They came together, bringing a ruled payment book and an empty handcart.

"Humse bhi poochh lo," the man with the book said. "Raat ko ek taraf ki sunke gaadi hata di."

The officer told him he could give his account, not move goods while the dispute was heard.

He sat on the front bench without being invited nearer the girls. Put the book on his knee. When a worker asked about the compulsory affiliation, he turned the page as though a different charge might be easier to defend.

---

Ira listened until the ordinary effort began to pull behind her uncovered eye.

She asked the receiving woman to read back one sentence from the account she was taking: the workers had asked for access to their homes and work, not for one protection group to replace another.

The woman read it. Two workers agreed. A third asked to have his family's specific threat given privately, not spoken in the room.

Ira nodded and let the notebook stay with the woman. The wide lines had space for another hand, exactly as the recorder-lady had intended.

Beside her, Aadhya kept both hands supported. She could hear the looms through the open front door and the representative asking for proof of a levy which the men had brought only themselves to authorize.

The man with the payment book shut it.

"Proof store mein hai," he said. "Maal ka bhugtaan nahi hua. Hum uthaa lete hain, baat baad mein kar lena."

Outside, the handcart's wheels began to move.

---

The constable at the office door moved before the handcart reached the store.

"Roko. Abhi kuch nahi niklega."

"Hifaazat ka paisa baaki hai," the collector said. "Maal yahin toh hai."

The department representative closed his file.

"Toh aap levy ke badle kaam rok rahe hain. Koi maal khareedne ki delivery nahi lene aaye."

The collector stood up.

One of the men at the cart shoved it forward anyway.

A worker caught the front rail. Another reached for a bale which had begun sliding. The constable got between them, palm out, telling everyone to let go. His radio crackled while he asked for the waiting support vehicle to come to the entrance.

Behind him, the collector said the inspection could continue after the store was emptied.

The representative told him to remain where he was.

The man with the pale wrist band stepped across the office's front ramp. A curved edge brightened along his forearm.

People near the door recoiled. The bench scraped backwards. A woman who had come to give her account stumbled against the table and lost her grip on the rent receipts.

Sitara rose beside the girls.

"Peechhe se chalenge," she said. "Saath."

---

The loom operator heard the warning from the court and stopped the machines.

Their clatter broke into separate dying movements. A worker at the shed door kept people from reaching into equipment which had not yet stopped. He sent them along the open side of the court, away from the cart.

No one had to keep working for a photograph of order.

The second constable moved towards the bright forearm. The officer at the table ordered the man to lower it, then stepped between him and the residents trying to leave. The curve caught the officer's lathi with a sharp hard sound.

The man did not lower it.

At the back, the cart lurched when the worker trying to protect the cloth released the rail. A loose bale fell sideways. Its wrapping split. Lengths dragged across the ground, taking dust and dirty water into the weave.

The driver who had brought the empty cart shouted for room to pull it straight.

Another guard told him to keep loading.

---

Ira saw the pale curve through the office doorway.

It was a live source. She recognized the low gap at the wrist before she could stop thinking about it. She had once placed such a reflection where a machine could break itself against it.

Pain stirred behind the eye covering. The visible curve was not a whole wall, and her injured sight would not become a good receiving angle because people needed one.

She turned towards Sitara's voice instead.

"Rear door kitna?"

"Do kadam. Phir chhota edge. Mera haath lo."

Ira took it. Aadhya rose with the receiving woman's help, keeping the numb fingers away from the chair's arm. They moved towards the exit they had walked before the meeting.

The woman who had dropped the receipts came with them. She bent once for a page; Ira asked her to leave it until people had room. The department representative had heard her account. The page did not have to be rescued ahead of her.

At the front, the officer's radio called the location again, louder than before.

---

The collector reached for the representative's file.

The representative pulled it back. A cooperative worker stood beside him, holding the table steady instead of running after the men with the cloth.

"Naam chahiye toh police ko apna do," the worker said.

The collector struck the table with his palm. The officer caught his wrist and took him away from the residents. He kept shouting to the men outside, but his words no longer controlled where the file went.

The bright-edged man backed towards the court. The constable followed at an angle, not putting another resident between the curve and his lathi. From the street came the sound of the support vehicle stopping.

At the store, the cart driver tried to turn away from the arriving officers.

The court had space. The people beside the office did not.

The cart's rear corner struck the splayed foot of the timber drying frame.

---

At the service path, the receiving woman had just stepped out ahead of Aadhya.

Sitara was guiding Ira over the low rear threshold. Two residents were behind them. The narrow path would take people in single file, not three shoulders abreast.

Aadhya heard the timber scrape. She turned towards it.

The frame's upper rail leaned away from the wall. The stem loosely tied to its foot drew tight. Cloth slid along the rail, bringing the weight towards the door where people were coming out.

"Frame!" the receiving woman shouted.

Sitara pulled Ira close to the wall. A resident behind her stopped. The woman at the front tried to move back into the court and found the cart across her way.

The frame kept tipping.

Aadhya could not take its weight in the hands which had only just closed over their wounds. The right thumb gave no warning even when she knocked it against the doorpost.

She dropped to one knee beside the strip of soil.

Her left palm heel touched the damp ground below the creeper. The thin stem was already straining against its knot.

There was no time to ask it to become a wall.

---

Aadhya did not try to lift the frame.

She reached along the living stem into the roots already holding its small patch of earth. The nearest timber foot was touching the loose end of that growth.

Hold here.

The thin roots gathered. One curled across the foot nearest the office door and pulled tight.

The nearer leg stopped. The far one kept sliding towards the court under the cart's shove.

The frame turned.

Its upper rail scraped along the wall instead of falling straight across the doorway. Cloth dragged from it in a long dark fold. Sitara moved the residents through the gap while the receiving woman pulled Ira clear of the crosspiece.

"Cart peechhe!" the woman shouted.

The driver finally obeyed the people nearest him. A worker caught the far rail, grunted under it, and called for another hand. Two more came from the shed side.

For one breath Aadhya felt the roots take the strain without knowing where the next demand would end.

For the second, pain split through a nail bed on her left hand.

She did not give it a third because the first two had worked.

"Pakad liya?" she asked.

"Haan! Chhod!"

She let go.

The roots slackened. A strip of bark peeled from the creeper's lower stem. The workers lowered the frame onto the now-clear edge of the court, letting the cloth slide away rather than trying to save its clean folds with people beneath it.

One timber joint broke as it landed.

The sound was small after all that waiting for the whole frame to strike.

Aadhya lifted her hand from the soil and saw blood at the edge of the new nail.

---

Sitara knelt beside her as soon as Ira was with the receiving woman.

She did not praise the work before looking at the hand.

"Aur kahin laga?"

"Right hand door pe. Pata nahi kitna."

The numb fingers had not acquired sensation because the left hand hurt. Sitara checked where the knock had landed without squeezing them to see whether Aadhya could bear it.

"Clinic. Abhi."

Aadhya looked towards the frame. The woman who tended the creeper was crouching beside its stripped stem. A worker helped her loosen the original cloth tie so it would not bite into the damaged part.

"Bach jaayega?" Aadhya asked.

The woman shook her head a little.

"Dekhenge. Pehle apna haath."

Sitara helped her up. No one asked for a second root to repair the first.

---

In the court, the extra officers had reached the cart.

The man with the pale curve lowered it when he found an officer behind him and the residents already out of reach. The constable in front kept his lathi raised until the forearm was dark again. Then the man was moved away from the workers, hands in view.

The collector was being taken to the police vehicle. His payment book stayed with the officer who had heard him call the levy a right to seize cloth.

The department representative asked the machine operator whether the shed was clear and the equipment stopped. He checked with the people from each work section before anybody was allowed to resume moving about the machines.

The driver said he had been hired only to carry goods.

The cooperative worker who had caught the rail showed him the split wrapping, the broken timber and the office door from which the residents had come.

"Aaj yeh bhi bolna statement mein," he said. "Kya uthaa rahe the, woh bhi."

The driver stayed.

---

At the clinic, Aadhya was put back in the chair she had left that morning.

The clinician cleaned and covered the reopened nail bed, examined the right-hand knock and checked the existing numbness against the handover. Further assessment was arranged for the injury which could not be judged by pain alone. She would not return to the cooperative that day.

The next forty-eight hours of rest and reassessment were not optional because the intervention had helped.

Ira sat on the other bed with her uncovered eye shut. She had used no second sight. Ordinary glare, fear and sustained attention had still brought the ache back. Sitara called the eye contact about the symptoms rather than deciding that an injury must be harmless if no power had caused it.

The receiving woman stayed until the clinician said what help was needed. She would take the account back to the inspection team herself.

Aadhya asked whether everyone had come out of the office.

"Sab jinhe humne nikalte dekha, court mein hain," the woman said. "Officer alag-alag section check kar rahe. Puri khabar aayegi toh bataaoongi."

Ira nodded without opening her eye. An incomplete answer was still better than giving a frightened person a certainty which would have to be taken away later.

Aadhya looked at the new white dressing.

She had held a timber foot for two breaths. Her hands would be paying for it after the people in the doorway had gone home.

---

The account which came back from the court contained losses as well as arrests.

Two workers needed examination after the shove and the falling rail. A constable's wrist had been struck against the cart. Cloth was torn and stained; the wrapping could not be changed to make it clean again. The stopped shift would have to be paid for somehow, and the cooperative could not recover an order's deadline by pointing at a police complaint.

No missing participant had been reported after the work sections and residential passage were checked. The machinery would remain stopped until the operator had checked it properly. Those were the limits of the first update, and Sitara passed them on without adding a triumph.

The inspection continued after the immediate danger was cleared. No one was asked to sign an affiliation to get back to a rented room. Questions of rent, work and legitimate dues would be handled through the appropriate process; the compulsory protection levy had supplied no authority for that morning's seizure.

A police detail stayed while the cooperative arranged its own access watch. The people who had keys before the men arrived kept them. They did not hand the keys to the visitors from Varanasi instead.

---

The man who had brought the thumbprinted acceptance came to the clinic door with his wife.

The representative had kept their account of the threat with the paper and recorded that they disputed the supposed consent. Their names would go to the officers handling it, not onto a public list of families to be admired or punished.

"Humein bola tha maan gaye toh apne ho," the woman said.

Aadhya looked at her from the chair.

"Ab?"

"Ab keh rahe inki baat mein aa gaye."

She meant the visiting girls, the inspector, anyone who had made the household's first frightened answer reversible.

The husband turned his rent receipts over in his hands.

"Ghar se nikalna nahi chaahte. Kisi ka dushman bhi nahi banna."

"Wahi toh aapki baat likhi hai," Ira said, her eye still closed. "Dono baat ek saath sach reh sakti hain."

The woman sat for a minute without asking what either girl had been born as. Her husband went to tell the relative keeping their children that the family could come back to its own room that evening.

The fear would go back with them. So would the account they had finally been allowed to give.

---

Sitara asked Aadhya to describe the intervention for the clinician.

Not the legend version. The turn, the knock, the pressure through the left palm, the two breaths, where the first pain had appeared. She did not let gratitude replace a history the doctor needed.

Aadhya gave it. Her right fingers still could not tell her how hard they had struck the post. The left nail bed was the new wound; the rest of the slow healing had not vanished, but neither could it protect that thin fresh skin from strain.

She had wanted, for a moment, to call the work small.

The dressing made the word inadequate. Small had meant a timber foot rather than a wall. It had not meant free.

The clinician arranged the further hand check and told Sitara what changes required urgent return. Ira's symptoms were passed to the eye clinician, with her actual work and rest described. She was to keep the planned review and seek earlier assessment if the specified warning changes came. Neither girl was promised that being brave had shortened the recovery.

Aadhya asked after the creeper once more.

The woman who tended it had supported the damaged stem and stopped anyone pulling the broken frame across its roots. It might survive. There would be no attempt to force new growth to prove that it would.

Aadhya nodded and let the nurse place her hands where they could rest.

---

In Prayagraj, the boys' first morning had been quieter.

They sat with the receiving-post volunteers for a short meeting, then stopped when the agreed time ended. Veer listened to a driver explain how one private guard had tried to turn a worker's surname into a transport permit. Rudra asked which part he had seen himself and which had come through a caller.

The driver drew the approaches on a scrap of packaging.

A temporary foot crossing over a river channel was still used by families coming to the post from the far side. Supplies went around by road when the crossing was unsuitable. The volunteers checked with the workers managing it before directing anyone there. Rival claimants had begun demanding that the two banks keep separate queues.

The post had notified the local officer. Two volunteers were at the near landing, helping people who had already set out. The boys were not being asked to inspect it on foot.

Vedant took the route information to the official contact while Rudra and Veer returned to the rest room. The post would need an actual safe arrival route, not another paper certifying the wish for one.

The Mirzapur update reached them there.

Rudra waited until Vedant had spoken to Sitara before asking to hear Aadhya himself. The first message had already confirmed she was at the clinic and under care. Fear did not have to invent an empty interval while the actual facts were being gathered.

---

Aadhya called her mother before the cooperative could send a grateful version home.

She gave the clinic's name first. Then the hand, the treatment, what the clinician was checking next. She said the right fingers were still numb and the new bleeding had been on the left.

Her mother asked whether Sitara was there.

"Haan."

"Unko do. Phir tumse baat karti hoon."

Aadhya handed the phone over with help. She could hear the questions, not all the answers. Her mother wanted to know whether she should come, whether the return vehicle was still available, who would call if the hand changed during the night.

Nobody told her not to worry because everybody was alive.

When Aadhya spoke again, her mother asked whether she had eaten.

"Abhi nahi."

"Pehle khaa lo. Main yahin hoon."

There was no punishment hidden after it. Aadhya let the bowl be placed where she could use it without finding out how much the new dressing would bear.

---

Ira's call to the recorder-lady was shorter.

She described the injured workers, the file staying with the representative, the families not being forced to choose one hereditary mark. The older woman asked whether Ira had used her sight.

"Nahi. Bas dekha zyada."

"Doctor ko bola?"

"Haan. Sitara ne bhi."

The recorder-lady waited until Ira stopped trying to make the answer sound lighter than it was.

"Notebook?" she asked.

"Doosri writing aa gayi."

"Achha."

Ira could hear the small pleased breath after the word. Someone else had taken an account in the room where she could not keep reading. The work had not ended at the edge of her injured eye.

She put the phone down before the older woman had to tell her to rest.

---

The receiving woman returned with the office keys and the plain notebook.

The keys were going back to the cooperative caretaker. The book had notes in two hands now; one worker had dictated his own correction when a sentence made his household sound more willing than it had been.

Ira asked that the notes stay available to the people who had given them, not disappear into the visitors' bags. Copies for the complaint would go through the representative with the contributors' agreement.

The woman said she would arrange that. Then she asked who would sit with them at the next meeting if the girls had to stay at the clinic.

Sitara named the local people already doing the work and offered to attend only after care was covered. The inspection did not require Aadhya's blood to remain valid. It did not require Ira to read through pain so the accounts could be believed.

"Aap dono nahi aaye toh bhi hum bolenge," the woman told them.

Aadhya looked up from the bowl.

"Wahi toh chahiye."

The woman took the keys to their owner. The loading court was open when she went back, with a constable at its entrance and workers arguing over the order in which the damaged cloth should be assessed.

The argument belonged to their work again.

---

In Prayagraj, the call between the two pairs lasted only a few minutes.

Rudra asked after Aadhya's new wound. She told him what she had told her mother, without reducing it to a scratch. Veer listened to Ira describe the live curve she had not tried to borrow.

"Achha kiya," he said.

"Source ki burai kar rahe ho?"

"Source bhi galat aadmi ke haath mein tha."

She smiled with the uncovered eye closed. The joke did not need a demonstration to finish it.

They ended the call so the girls could rest. Sitara stayed clinic-side until the care arrangements were settled; nobody in Mirzapur was waiting for the boys to cross the distance and make the room safe.

Vedant went back to the receiving table. Rudra kept the supported foot up. Veer adjusted the cushion beneath his knee before anyone asked.

Outside, volunteers were setting aside space for families expected from the river channel.

---

Late in the afternoon, the near landing called again.

The receiving-post volunteer there called the official contact, then the post. A group had blocked the approach and begun turning people into separate household queues. Families already on the temporary crossing were trying to go back rather than choose a side at its end.

At the opposite approach, a section joining the walkway to the bank had come loose under the crowd. The volunteer could see the gap. He could not yet say who had moved what before it failed.

The police and water-rescue team were being called. The near landing had to be cleared for them, not packed with more spectators going to see whether the report was true.

Vedant asked the post to keep its arriving families off that route. The driver reached for the official contact number instead of starting the van on a guess.

Rudra could hear the river through the phone now, beneath too many voices speaking at once.

The volunteer was shouting for people to stand still on the walkway. Someone nearer the far side was calling for a mother not to lift her child above the rail.

Then a woman's voice reached the receiver clearly.

"Is taraf se wapas nahi aa sakte. Udhar jaane nahi de rahe."

Veer sat forward and stopped when the knee caught.

The woman asked again, louder, as though the man holding the phone might be able to give her a piece of bank.

"Hum utrein kahan?"
