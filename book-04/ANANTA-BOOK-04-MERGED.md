# ANANTA — BOOK 04 — MERGED

# CHAPTER SIXTY-ONE — THE FIRST BARRICADE

---

> *"The first boundary crossed a road we had used to carry the wounded.*
> *By noon, men on both sides were calling it protection."*
>
> *— relief driver's deposition, collected after the first road closures*

---

The first relief van to return to the hospital that morning brought back all its food.

Rudra saw the covered pots before he saw the driver. They were being lifted down beneath the corridor window, their lids still tied with the kitchen's strips of cloth. An attendant touched one with the back of his fingers and called somebody over. Steam escaped when the knot came loose.

The food was still warm.

The man who should have delivered it sat on the low wall beside the ramp, pressing a gamchha against his cheek.

"Neeche kya hua?" Rudra asked.

The nurse finished securing his ankle dressing before looking towards the window. His chair stood well back from the glass, the foot raised. She had brought him there while the observation bed was being changed. It was not a place from which he could lean out and direct the people below.

"Pata kar rahe hain. Pair yahin."

He left the foot where she had put it.

Downstairs, a security guard took the driver towards the treatment entrance. Another stayed with the van. The kitchen attendant carried one pot away instead of letting it sit in the sun.

Behind Rudra, a trolley wheel squeaked. The smell of fresh phenyl came through the open bay door.

The hospital had gone on being a hospital after they stopped laughing in the courtyard.

---

Ram had needed oxygen through the night. The chest drain remained in place. There had been no new collapse in pressure, but the doctor wanted another examination and another day of close watching before saying more.

Rudra had heard the update with his mother. The doctor had waited for their questions before returning to the unit.

Rukmini was back in her bay now, Dadi sitting beside her. The left shoulder stayed supported; the dark pressure marks below her jaw had spread into colours Rudra disliked looking at. She had asked whether Ram's breathing hurt less. The doctor had answered about the treatment for pain, not promised that the injury had stopped hurting.

Rudra had brought that distinction back with him.

Now he watched the second pot being carried away and wondered how many people would have been waiting for it at the other end.

Vedant arrived with the answer to the smaller question first.

"Gaadi roki thi. Hospital ke peechhe, anaaj-mandi ke chauraha pe."

"Aegis?"

"Driver se sunte hain. Naam hum nahi bharte."

The words were quiet, without rebuke. Vedant pulled a chair across, leaving room for the trolley to pass.

The driver came after his cheek had been cleaned. A small dressing covered the cut. He held a hospital slip in one hand and two other pieces of paper in the other. The security guard stayed at the bay entrance; the nurse gave them a few minutes while she entered the dressing change.

"Rasoi se nikle the?" Vedant asked.

"Subah. Ghat waale parivaar school mein hain na. Wahin khaana."

At the grain-market crossing, men with a handcart across the road had stopped him. They wanted the pots unloaded under their supervision. He had shown the kitchen's delivery note.

"Bole, parchi badlo. Rakshak suraksha mein jaayega. Maine kaha, rasoi ka hai. Sabke liye."

The slap had knocked his cheek against the edge of the van's door. He touched the dressing, then lowered his hand when the nurse looked up.

"Thappad kisne maara?" she asked.

"Jo cart ke paas tha. Naam nahi pata."

She wrote down his answer. Vedant did not interrupt the medical account to ask about the man's strength.

The driver had reversed into the adjoining lane. There another group had stopped him, beside a shuttered seed shop. A woman there had read the first slip and torn off its corner.

"Boli, Prakriti ke raaste pe unki parchi nahi chalegi. Apni di. Maine poochha, school toh ek hi hai. Khaana kahaan utaaroon?"

"Kya kaha?" Rudra asked.

"Pehle tay karo kis taraf se aaye ho."

Through the window, the van's rear doors stood open. An attendant was passing down the last pot.

The driver laid both slips on the empty chair. One had a thumbprint beside a demand for supervised passage. The other carried a signature and the name of the seed-shop lane. Neither bore a hospital order or the kitchen's agreement.

"Inhone apna Kul bataya," Vedant said. "Itna pakka hai. Poora gharana kya kar raha hai, abhi nahi pata."

Rudra nodded, though his eyes had gone to the road beyond the ramp.

When the wounded first reached that road, they had needed every pace of it. This morning somebody had decided the way belonged to him.

---

Aadhya heard about the food while a porter moved her chair back from the hand clinic. Both hands rested on the pillow again. The right thumb and index finger had not begun to feel the cloth beneath them.

Ira was waiting outside the adjoining eye room, turned away from the window. The covering over her left eye was clean. She listened while Vedant repeated what the driver had actually seen.

"School ko phone hua?" she asked.

The kitchen had reached its volunteer there. Breakfast had not arrived. They had some dry food left; they needed to know whether to keep waiting or use it.

"Use karne ko bolo," Ira said. "Aur yeh slips rakhna. Inke kehne pe rasoi ki parchi mat phenk dena."

She asked for the route names to be read once more: the two turns where a driver had been stopped, and which way he had finally come back.

Vedant called the kitchen. The hospital desk had already informed the police about the obstruction and the assault.

Before he finished, Veer's mother came along the corridor beside her son's chair, bringing him back from the dressing desk. Veer was in yesterday's sling. Sleep had not made his knee fit to carry him up a hospital ramp.

His mother stopped beside Vedant.

"Bahar ek aadmi milne aaya hai," she said. "Veer se. Keh raha hai, apne Kul ke hain."

---

Veer looked down at the sling before answering.

Its edge had begun to cut into the back of his neck. His mother loosened the fastening while he held still, then folded a small clean cloth beneath the strap. She had tied his wrist-thread for journeys before. This knot she checked with two fingers underneath it.

"Dheela ho gaya?" he asked.

"Jitna chahiye. Haath ko sahaara mil raha hai na?"

He started to touch the cloth, then stopped when the movement tugged at the dressing on his right elbow.

"Haan."

"Bas. Zyada kasne se achha nahi hota."

She smoothed the back of his collar. Waited for his answer about the visitor.

Veer looked towards Vedant.

"Paanch minute. Yahin. Maa saath rahengi."

The nurse pointed them towards the recessed visitors' seats beyond the clinical door. No blocking the passage; no one going into the bays to find a patient. The guard brought the man there and stayed within sight.

He wore a pale kurta with one cuff rolled higher than the other. A row of small stitches ran along the exposed forearm. When he sat opposite Veer, he settled the injured arm carefully on his thigh.

"Kal se dhoondh rahe the," he said. "Ghat pe tumne jo kiya—"

"Abhi uski baat nahi," Veer's mother said. "Kya kaam hai?"

He accepted the interruption with a nod.

He said his group had come to guard the grain-market crossing. Rakshak families had lost men and property in the siege; other households had begun moving stores before the rubble was cold. There were medicines being taken from one godown and seed sacks from another. If no one held the road, everything would vanish.

"Kiski dawa?" Vedant asked.

"Hisaab hoga. Pehle rokna padega."

"Khaane ki gaadi bhi?"

The man glanced at the two slips in Vedant's hand.

"Baantne ka zimma lenge toh gin ke lenge. Kal phir kami padi toh hamaare darwaaze pe hi aayenge."

The food was not his, Vedant said. The school had asked the kitchen for it. The hospital had given the van a place to turn and load.

"Hospital se jhagda nahi hai," the man replied. He looked back at Veer. "Tum saamne aa jaao. Baithkar hi bolna hai. Itna ki apne logon ka raasta hum rakhenge. Log tumhari sunenge."

Veer's good hand lay flat on the chair arm.

"Aur doosre log?"

"Unke apne hain."

Aadhya's chair had stopped a little behind his, on the shaded side. The man's gaze reached her pillow, the bandages, then her face.

"Tumhare liye bhi gaadi aayegi. Prakriti waale apne ilaake mein le jaayenge. Yahan saath rehne se baat aur uljhegi."

"Kaun le jaayega?" she asked.

He named the seed-shop lane, not a relative she knew.

"Wahan jo khade hain, unse poochhna. Hamaare saath unki nahi banti. Par tum unki ho, baat maanenge."

Aadhya kept her hands where they were.

"Mere doctor ki baat abhi sun lenge? Yeh haath lekar kahin nahi jaana hai."

"Main ilaaj chhodne ko nahi—"

"Toh gaadi mat bhejna."

The man leaned back. His injured forearm slid; he caught it with the other hand and drew breath through his teeth.

For a moment nobody spoke.

Veer's mother asked whether the stitches had been checked that morning. He shook his head. She pointed out the dressing desk before looking back at her son.

"Tumne jawaab nahi diya," the man said to him.

Veer watched the careful way he was holding the arm.

"Raasta bachane aaye ho toh raasta kholo."

The man's mouth tightened.

"Ek baar bolkar khul jaata toh main tumhare paas nahi aata. Udhar jo baithe hain, woh bhi Kul ka naam le rahe hain. Tumhare doston ka."

"Mere naam se inka raasta band nahi hoga," Veer said.

Rudra saw Aadhya turn towards him. Not in surprise. As if a thing which should not have needed saying had nevertheless needed to be heard.

From inside the clinical door, Ira asked for the nurse. She had heard enough; the uncovered eye was watering in the brighter light. The nurse took her chair back first.

The guard checked the time and stepped nearer.

Vedant's phone rang before the visitor could try again.

He listened, then stood.

"Mandi ke chauraha pe? Gaadi ka engine band karwaao. Kisi ko pahiye ke neeche mat bhejna. Police ko wahi location do. Main aa raha hoon."

The visitor had risen too.

"Kya hua?"

"Gaadi kheench rahe hain. Aadmi phans gaya."

For the first time the man looked towards the hospital entrance without looking back at Veer. He went with the guard, moving quickly despite the arm.

Vedant asked the nurse to tell Sitara where he was going. She would stay with the patients. Two constables had reached the gate to take the driver's account; he would meet them downstairs.

"Main—" Rudra began, and stopped.

Vedant waited.

Rudra looked towards the observation bay, where the driver had left the delivery note.

"School ko bata dena. Doosri gaadi usi raste mat bhejein."

Vedant nodded. "Abhi."

He took his shallow basin from the bag beside the wall and left carrying it low beside his knee.

---

Two turns from the hospital, a sack of flour burst against a shutter.

White dust hung across the crossing. Through it, Vedant saw the nose of a small goods carrier, a handcart tipped towards the gutter, and a man lying on his side beside its trapped wheel.

The cart's long shaft lay across his lower leg.

"Engine band!" one of the constables shouted.

The carrier's engine stopped. Someone kept sounding its horn until the other constable reached the cab and struck the door with his palm.

"Band matlab band! Peechhe log hain!"

The horn stopped too.

Beyond the cart, a woman stood at the seed-shop corner with one hand against the trunk of a roadside tree. Roots had broken through the old mortar below it. One ran along the gutter, thickened round the cart's axle, and disappeared beneath a fallen sack.

Opposite her, a broad man held the cart's front rail. A pale edge stood along his other forearm. It caught the constable's raised lathi and turned it sideways when he tried to get between the two groups.

The constable staggered into a bystander. Both stayed upright.

"Prakriti ka maal chhod!" the woman called.

"Tere godaam se nahi liya!" somebody shouted back.

The injured loader tried to pull his knee towards his chest. The cart shifted with him. He stopped with a sound which made Vedant put the basin down at once.

"Isko chhodo pehle," Vedant said.

Neither side answered him. The woman pressed her hand harder against the trunk. The root drew tight; the axle grated across the gutter stone.

The loader screamed.

The man from the hospital came out of the turning behind Vedant.

"Haath hatao!" he shouted at the broad man. "Neeche aadmi hai!"

"Dikh raha hai. Udhar se kheenchegi toh—"

"Toh chhod!"

He caught the cart's rail with his good arm. The other man did not release it.

Vedant went down on one knee beside the loader, keeping outside the space under the shaft.

"Naam?"

The answer came broken by breath.

"Theek. Meri taraf dekho. Pair mat kheenchna. Abhi nahi."

The loader's hand closed on his sleeve.

At the goods carrier, the second constable was getting the driver out on the side away from the struggle. The first had moved the bystanders behind a parked delivery truck and was calling for another vehicle, giving the junction and warning that people were using powers in the road.

An upended drinking-water drum lay against the seed-shop step. Water continued to leak through its broken tap, finding a course towards the flour-clogged gutter.

Vedant set the basin beneath the run. The driver, following his pointing hand, dragged a length of discarded packing wood off the carrier's bed.

"Pahiye ke peechhe. Upar se nahi. Side se."

The driver pushed the wood along the ground until it met the rim. A chock, not a lever. If the root let go, the wheel would not roll farther into the gutter.

Vedant looked towards the woman.

"Tumhari jad ke neeche phansa hai. Dekho idhar."

"Inko bolo gaadi se hato."

"Main tumse iske pair ki baat kar raha hoon."

She looked down.

For a moment her fingers stayed spread against the tree. Then two lifted. The root loosened a little round the axle.

"Abhi kheenchna nahi," Vedant told the loader.

The hospital visitor set his feet apart and put both hands under the rail. His stitched forearm shook. The tipped cart rose just enough for daylight to show beneath the shaft.

"Nikaalo."

Vedant and the driver reached for the loader's shoulders.

The broad man jerked the rail towards his side of the crossing.

"Hamaara maal—"

The wooden chock cracked.

The axle turned inside the root's loosening coil. A sideboard sprang free, swinging down towards the loader's head with one iron corner leading.

Vedant lifted his free hand from the basin.

The gathered water rose in a shallow curve. It met the board across its broad face, not at the corner. Water flattened, sprayed. The board turned enough to strike Vedant's forearm instead of the man beneath it.

He grunted. Kept the curve there.

"Ab!"

The driver pulled. The visitor lifted again. Blood showed along the stitches above his rolled cuff.

The loader's leg came clear.

Vedant let the water fall. The board struck the road. A sack rolled off the cart and split into the wet flour already pasted across the gutter.

For one breath all the shouting stopped.

The loader was crying. The driver knelt behind him, keeping his shoulders raised away from the wheels. The woman took her hand off the tree. The root stopped moving; it did not sink tidily back into the paving it had broken.

Then the broad man shoved the visitor away from the rail.

The first constable stepped between them again, this time with two men from the delivery truck beside him. The second had a chair from the seed shop. They brought it under the loader rather than trying to make him stand; the driver folded a sack under the injured leg and stayed crouched beside it.

A siren sounded from the main road.

"Sab peechhe!" the constable shouted. "Ab koi gaadi haath nahi lagaayega!"

The broad man backed towards his companions, the pale edge still raised. The woman remained beside the tree. Neither had a clear path to the flour. Between them lay the broken cart and a strip of water turning grey.

Vedant stepped out of it. His forearm had begun to swell where the iron corner had struck. The basin rattled against his knee when he picked it up.

The visitor looked at the blood crossing his wrist.

"Yeh rasoi jaa raha tha?" he asked the driver.

"Parchi teen baar dikhaayi," the driver said.

No one asked to see it a fourth time.

The police vehicle reached the turning. One constable directed it across the junction to hold the two groups apart; the other went with the loader towards the hospital. The injured visitor followed. His companions called after him. He did not turn until the chair was clear of the broken paving.

On the carrier's bed, the sacks which had not been taken down remained tied in place.

They would have to wait for another road.

---

By the time Vedant returned upstairs, the four had been taken back inside. Veer's morning assessment was not finished; the nurse had put him on an observation bed with the injured leg raised.

A porter had met the loader at the entrance with a trolley. The man from the visitors' seats was at another treatment desk, his reopened wound wrapped in fresh gauze. The constable had stayed to take accounts after the injured were examined.

"Dono ek hi gate se aaye," Veer's mother said.

She was speaking to her son, but Aadhya heard from the next bed. The curtains had been drawn back between them while the nurse checked their dressings.

"Unhone kya kaha?" Veer asked.

"Abhi kuch nahi. Ilaaj ho raha hai."

He nodded. The cloth his mother had tucked under the sling had worked slightly sideways. He asked her to straighten it rather than reaching across the injured arm.

Vedant came in with Sitara beside him. A cold pack lay against his forearm.

Rudra looked from it to the basin hanging from his other hand. Dried flour marked the rim.

"Aapka?"

"Laga hai. Dekh liya doctor ne. Ungliyaan chal rahi hain. Thanda rakhoon, phir dobara dekhenge."

Sitara took the basin before he could find a place to balance it. She did not take away the cold pack.

Vedant gave them the junction in order: the goods carrier stopped on its way to the kitchen, sacks being taken onto the cart, the root round the axle. The driver and loader had both said the flour was for the relief meals. The two groups disputed who had the right to move it. Neither had shown him an order from the kitchen.

"Unko rok diya?" Rudra asked.

"Us chauraha pe police hai. Log nikal rahe hain. Tooti zameen pe gaadi nahi jaayegi abhi."

"Aur woh log?"

"Wahin. Apni-apni taraf."

Outside the window an ambulance crossed the ramp. Its siren gave one short warning to a reversing car, then stopped.

Rudra listened until it had passed.

There was no lever at that crossing which could be broken and make this end. The men guarding the road called the woman a thief. The woman called the sacks stolen. Neither accusation had lifted the cart off the loader.

He looked at the cold pack. The swelling beneath it was the part of the answer Vedant had brought back on his own body.

"Maal kisne diya tha?" Ira asked from the shaded bed.

Vedant had the driver's number. The kitchen would check its purchase and donation entries against the load. The police had asked the same question.

"Dono slips ke saath rasoi ki parchi ki copy bhi dena," she said. "Sirf unka daava pahuncha toh baad mein wahi sach bana denge."

Sitara found a loose sheet in her bag. Ira dictated the two route names, the assault on the first driver, and which accounts still needed checking. She left the exact ownership of the flour blank. Beside it she asked Sitara to write *verification pending*.

Her fingers had begun trembling against the coverlet. She rested one hand over the other and closed the uncovered eye while Sitara finished the line.

"Bas?" Sitara asked.

Ira listened to the short list read back.

"Haan. Ab bas."

The sheet went into Vedant's bag. It was enough for the next call. No one asked her to open the register and turn a morning's injury into a finished history.

---

Aadhya had been staring at the door.

"Woh jad rok sakti thi," she said.

Vedant put the cold pack down long enough to adjust the cloth beneath it.

"Usne roki. Der se. Phir doosre ne cart kheencha."

Aadhya looked at her numb fingers.

She remembered the care with which the elder had put three paper twists of seed before her. Mustard. Rice. White peas. The lesson had begun with looking at what was already alive.

At the crossing a woman had put her hand against a tree and kept pulling after a man cried out.

For a few seconds Aadhya could not make those two hands belong in the same thought.

Veer shifted on the next bed. His mother's hand stayed near his supported knee until he settled.

"Hum bhi toh wahi hain," he said.

Aadhya raised her head.

He looked towards his sling, then at her pillow.

"Unke apne. Tumhare apne. Bol aise rahe the jaise humne pehle hi haan kar di."

"Maine nahi ki."

"Maine bhi nahi."

Neither smiled at the agreement.

Rudra watched them across the small distance between the beds. At the ghat, that distance had been crossed with a warning, a hand, the turn of a shield. Somebody outside was trying to stretch it across a street and put a different duty on either side.

He wanted to say they would not let it happen. The visitor had not needed permission to say *your people*. Veer had refused him, and the flour still lay in the gutter.

"Jo bola hai, woh ghar pe bhi bata do," Rudra said. "Kisi aur se na sunein."

Veer's mother was already reaching for her phone.

---

The porter took Rudra past his mother's bay on the way back to his own observation space. Sitara walked beside the chair. He asked her to stop at the open curtain.

Rukmini had heard there was trouble on the road. Dadi had not tried to hide the sound of the trolley or the constable asking directions.

Her good hand rested across the wrist of her supported arm. As Rudra came into view, two fingers moved once at the pulse, then lay still.

He showed her the raised foot without trying to stand.

"Yahin tha," he said. "Andar."

She looked at Sitara, who nodded.

Rukmini's hand settled. The left shoulder remained exactly where the nurses had supported it. Dadi drew the sheet clear of the shoulder dressing and sat down again.

Rudra let the porter take him on.

He did not need his mother to thank him for staying in his bed.

---

Dhruv came before noon.

He had gone back to the ghat after sleeping, checked the relief watch and then followed the account of the grain-market fighting to the hospital. The angle-ring stayed in its pouch. A clean dressing crossed his palm; he used the other hand to bring the chair close to Rudra's bed.

"Neeche?" Rudra asked.

"Jo band hua tha, band hai. Gharon mein abhi bhi kisi ko nahi jaane de rahe."

The broken device had not come back to life. The people who had lost their rooms had not got them back either.

Dhruv had seen them leaving the school in pairs to find water, a charge for a phone, news of a missing relative. They had begun asking at the relief post which lanes they were allowed to use.

"Kaun rok raha hai?" Rudra asked.

"Jis mod pe jo khada hai. Naam alag bata rahe hain. Hamaare watch waale har jagah nahi hain. Police ko jagah bheji hai."

Some of the people calling themselves guards had helped carry stretchers during the siege. Dhruv knew two by face. He had told them to move their barricade. They had moved it far enough for him, then drawn it back across the lane behind him.

"Aapko jaane diya," Rudra said.

"Mujhe. Gaadi ko nahi."

Dhruv laid his dressed hand on his knee. The ring made no sound inside its pouch.

Beyond the curtain, a nurse was explaining to a frightened family that no one would be transferred merely because a visitor had offered another vehicle. The treating doctor would decide whether a patient could move. The family could ask the doctor themselves.

Rudra listened to the relief in the woman's answer.

It lasted until she asked how the rest of them were supposed to get home.

---

The school had used its dry stores. The kitchen's first returned meal had been served to people waiting at the hospital before it spoiled. A volunteer was checking another approach on foot before a fresh load went out.

These small pieces of news reached the bay through Vedant. He made the calls sitting down, the cold pack balanced on a folded towel. When he needed to read a number from the sheet, Sitara held it at an angle which did not force his bruised arm to turn.

Rudra watched the two of them work without calling it a plan for the whole city.

There was a flour load to establish ownership for. A school to feed. A junction the police had partly secured but which still could not take traffic. Beyond it were roads none of them had seen that morning.

"Aap sabko bol sakte hain na," he asked Dhruv, "hospital aane waalon ko mat roko?"

"Bol raha hoon. Aur bolenge. Sunenge ya nahi, uske liye log chahiye. Sirf naam nahi."

Rudra looked down at his hands.

The wrist support held its shape when he relaxed. His knuckles still pulled beneath the dressings. The seam at his neck caught against the clean collar if he turned too quickly.

For a little while at Manikarnika, he had been able to stand inside a force great enough to break the frame. He remembered it with a frightening clarity. He also remembered needing someone else to press a dressing against his neck afterwards.

He had not learnt how to call that force again. Even if he had, he could not stand at every turning in Varanasi.

The thought did not make the people waiting at those turnings less real.

"Jo raste check ho gaye," he said, "unke naam desk pe de do. Time ke saath. Puraana jawaab aage na bhejte rahein."

Dhruv nodded. He would give them what his watch had actually checked, and the number for a fresh call before any vehicle left.

He stood, leaving the chair close enough for the next person who came to sit.

---

The nurse brought Rudra his prescribed medicines and waited while he took them. Then she lowered the light on his side and told him to rest.

From the adjoining bay came Veer's voice, low on a family call. He was repeating the words *maine nahi kaha*. Aadhya had asked Sitara to reach her mother directly. Ira was asleep, the loose sheet already put away.

Rudra lay back.

Outside, a porter argued gently with a wheel which wanted to turn left. The hospital's lunch trolley went past. Somewhere behind the next wall, somebody who had been silent all morning began asking for tea.

He shut his eyes and let the ordinary sounds remain ordinary.

Then the phone at the corridor desk rang.

The nurse answered. Said the hospital's name. Listened.

"Kaunsi gaadi?"

A pause.

She called the duty doctor, then asked someone to find the constable still taking statements downstairs. Dhruv's voice answered from farther along the passage.

Rudra opened his eyes.

The ambulance had gone to the school for an injured man whose breathing had worsened. It had avoided the grain-market crossing. The northern approach had been clear when the driver checked. Now someone had pulled a barrier across it.

The nurse put the call on speaker for the doctor.

"Peechhe patient hai," the driver was saying. "Hospital ko baat kara raha hoon. Doctor hain."

A fist struck the ambulance's body. Not the quick knock of a family member telling a driver to go.

"Naam bata diya," the driver said to the desk. "Maan nahi rahe."

The doctor moved nearer the phone.

"Phone dijiye. Main baat karta hoon."

Another voice came through, close now, almost courteous.

"Doctor-saab, naam nahi poochh rahe. Patient kis Kul ka hai?"

# CHAPTER SIXTY-TWO — THE PATIENT IN THE BACK

---

> *"They called it the road of no Kul after the first ambulance got through.*
> *The man in the back remembered only the ceiling."*
>
> *— ambulance dutybook, account added after the northern closures*

---

The doctor asked the man at the barrier to give the phone back to the driver.

"Pehle aap bataaiye—"

"Mujhe patient ki saans sunni hai. Driver ko dijiye."

His voice did not rise. Beside the desk, the nurse had already written the location on a slip and put it in Dhruv's uninjured hand.

A scrape sounded through the speaker. The ambulance driver returned, breathing hard with anger rather than effort.

"Doctor-saab."

"Medic paas hain? Unko dijiye. Gaadi se patient ko abhi utaarna nahi."

The next voice was a woman's. She gave the patient's condition, the oxygen she was using, the change since leaving the school. The doctor listened. Asked two short questions. Told her what he wanted checked while they waited for a way through.

Rudra could not see the numbers she read from the monitor. He could hear somebody behind her trying to finish a sentence on too little air.

Then another woman said, "Bhaiya, inko pahunchaa do. Jo naam likhna hai likh lo."

The driver moved the phone closer to the medic. The plea went on, quieter now, beyond her account.

Rudra turned his head towards the curtain.

The seam caught against his collar.

---

The constable came up from the statement desk with his notebook still open. Dhruv gave him the location. He read it back, called the vehicle outside, and went down before the call ended.

Vedant rose from the chair beside the bay. The cold pack slid off its towel. He caught it against his thigh without closing the bruised arm round it.

"Tum raho," Dhruv said.

"Chal sakta hoon."

"Pata hai. Hospital waalon ko yahan bhi ek aadmi chahiye jo doosri taraf ki khabar sun chuka ho. Main pahunchkar phone karta hoon."

Vedant looked towards the desk. The nurse was asking that the main gate remain clear for the returning ambulance, not filled by people going out to watch.

He sat again.

Sitara stood at the open end of the bay until Dhruv saw her. She nodded once: the patients would not be left without someone they knew.

He checked the knot of the pouch at his waist. The angle-ring stayed inside it. Beneath the fresh dressing, his palm had begun to ache simply from the morning's walking and questions.

At the stairs he paused for a porter bringing a cylinder up. The porter thanked him, scarcely looking at his face. Dhruv waited for the load to cross the landing, then went down.

---

Veer had heard the call from the next bay.

"Kis taraf ke hain?" he asked when Vedant came back within hearing.

"Barrier waale? Abhi unse sunenge."

"Patient."

Vedant looked at him.

Veer's face changed before he answered his own question.

"Nahi. Mera matlab—"

"Pata hai," Vedant said.

He put the cold pack back over the towel. Veer stared at the ceiling for a moment, then turned his good hand palm-up on the sheet.

"Bas wahi poochh diya."

Nobody reassured him that a habit could not enter through words used by someone he hated. Aadhya, sitting across the bay while her hands were checked, asked whether the family had somebody at the school who could meet the ambulance if it had to reverse.

Vedant called the volunteer there. They needed the road behind the vehicle kept clear as well as the road in front. The school sent two people out to turn approaching handcarts back before they joined the queue.

It was a small useful task, finished before any of them had found something brave to say.

---

Outside, the afternoon had the flat white glare which makes every shutter seem hotter than the wall around it.

Dhruv followed the constable past the pharmacy and along the northern approach. The police vehicle had gone ahead by the wider road. He could hear its siren starting and stopping beyond the next row of houses.

At the tea stall a man pointed without being asked. A woman coming the other way held a schoolbag against her chest and said the van was still there.

"Aage mat jaiye," the constable told her.

"Mere pati hain usmein. Mujhe bahar bhej diya."

He stopped. Asked her name and whether she had come with the patient.

She had. The medic had needed space; men at the barrier had begun asking her questions through the open door. She had stepped down to answer them, thinking that would make the delay shorter.

Now she was on the wrong side of the crowd.

Dhruv turned his body to give her room beside him.

"Hum gaadi tak jaa rahe hain. Aap saath rahiye. Jahan bolenge wahin rukna, theek?"

She nodded, still clutching the schoolbag.

"Kapde hain," she said, seeing his glance. "Do jode."

He did not tell her she would not need them.

---

The ambulance stood behind a low farm trailer slewed across the lane. Its towbar pointed into a brick stack. A rope ran from its other end to the iron post outside a closed shop.

Enough room for a person on either side. Not enough for the van.

Three men stood by the rope. Behind the ambulance, a motorcycle and two carts had become a second obstruction. The driver had left a few feet between his bumper and the trailer, as though the small visible distance could persuade someone to clear it.

A young man at the post was writing on a board balanced against his stomach.

Across its top, in thick blue letters, was the word **PROTECTION**.

---

The constable went to the post first.

"Kiski gaadi hai? Hataao. Ambulance nikaalni hai."

The young man lowered his board. Beside him, an older man put a folded towel on the head of a boy sitting in the shade. The boy's nose was bleeding. He wore the same narrow cloth band round his upper arm as the men beside the rope.

"Hamaara bhi aadmi ghayal hai," the older man said.

"Usko bhi bhejo. Pehle rasta khol."

"Udhar Prakriti waale gaadi rok ke baithe hain. Unko khulwaaiye pehle. Rakshak ke aadmi yahan se hat gaye toh yeh poori line unki."

The constable gave the ambulance one look, took his radio out and repeated the request for the vehicle to come to the post, not stop at the wider junction.

Dhruv moved along the van's side with the patient's wife. He did not ask her to argue for the road.

The driver opened the sliding side door far enough to let him hear the medic. The air from inside smelled of vinyl, sweat and the sharp clean edge of spirit.

"Kitna time?" she asked.

"Police aa rahi hai. Main dekh raha hoon."

"Mujhe andaaza nahi chahiye. Agar gaadi nahi niklegi toh doosra tareeka dekhiye. Inko pahunchna hai."

Dhruv nodded. She let the wife speak to her husband for a few seconds, from the step, without climbing across the equipment.

"Aa rahe hain," the woman said. "Hum aa rahe hain."

The patient could not turn towards her. His eyes found the roof light, lost it, found it again.

---

At the trailer, the constable had untied the rope from the post.

One of the men caught the free end. A pale curve flashed above his wrist and stood between the constable and the trailer's handle.

"Haath peechhe," the constable said.

The curve held.

A phone rose above the crowd. Another followed it. The man with the towel looked towards the lenses, then pushed one down. Its owner recoiled; the screen struck the closed shop's shutter and cracked.

The sound brought more people to their doors.

Dhruv looked past the post. The trailer was loaded with paving blocks. Without it, the lane was wide enough. Beside it, the gap narrowed at a jutting stone doorstep, not much wider than his shoulders.

He could send a person through there. Not a stretcher kept level.

The constable spoke into his radio again. No flourish with the lathi this time. His free hand held the phones and the shield-man apart.

Behind the ambulance, a cart owner began backing his empty vehicle. The school volunteers had reached the rear of the queue. They turned the motorcycle sideways into a doorway and made another space behind it.

Dhruv went back along the van, looking at the buildings rather than the men.

"Us taraf se?" he asked the wife.

He pointed to the narrow iron gate beside the shut shop.

"Kapde dhote hain andar," she said. "Peechhe gali hai. School se aate the hum. Ab gate band hai."

"Kiske paas chaabi?"

She named the woman who lived upstairs. Someone in the watching crowd called up to her before Dhruv could ask.

A window opened. A grey head appeared above the clothesline.

"Kya?"

"Dono gate kholna hai! Mareez hai!"

The woman looked down at the trailer, at the ambulance, then at the man whose pale curve still held a policeman away from a public road.

"Aa rahi hoon," she said.

---

The medic came down to inspect the passage as soon as the first gate opened. The driver stayed with the patient. Dhruv and the woman led her through a washing courtyard striped with wet sheets.

A second iron gate stood at the far end. Beyond it, a service alley ran past the back of the pharmacy towards the hospital. The building owner unlocked it. She took two buckets out of the path, then went back for the clothesline which sagged across the centre.

"Yeh utaar do," the medic said.

"Saaf kapde hain. Upar kar dete—"

"Sir pe lagega. Poora utaariye."

The woman unknotted it without another word. Wet cotton settled in a heap on her verandah, touching the floor she had just washed.

Dhruv walked the route again, this time looking at the thresholds. At the back gate a broken drain cover left one dark opening along the side. He pointed it out to the medic. She measured the usable strip with her eyes and asked whether the hospital could meet them at the alley mouth with a trolley.

He called Vedant. Gave the exact gate and the drain, not merely the name of the road.

"Wheels ke liye nahi hai," he said. "Stretcher uthaake. Seedha."

The medic made the decision. They would transfer with the portable oxygen and a carrying team, if the hospital sent bearers to the back entrance. Not the wife, not a few excited men grabbing whichever end they reached first.

Dhruv relayed that too.

Outside the first gate the man with the board had stopped writing.

"Yeh kiska ghar hai?" he asked.

The woman came back from dropping the clothesline.

"Mera."

"Aise khol dogi toh sab aayenge."

She took the empty buckets off the step so nobody could trip on them.

"Abhi ek aayega," she said. "Usko jagah do."

From the ambulance came three quick taps. The driver wanted the medic back inside.

She went at once.

Dhruv followed her as far as the open door. The wife had stopped repeating that they were coming. Both her hands were pressed flat against the outside of the van.

---

The porters arrived through the back alley with a carrying stretcher and another attendant. They left the trolley on the hospital side of the broken cover. Dhruv went ahead of them, pointing out the opening before anyone could step beside it with a load.

At the van, the medic told the wife to wait inside the courtyard.

"Main saath—"

"Aap saath hi hain. Bas abhi haath lagaane ki jagah chahiye."

The woman went through the gate. The building owner took her schoolbag before its strap could catch the latch.

They brought the ambulance stretcher level with the carrying one. The medic counted for the transfer. Staff hands moved together. The patient's mouth opened; no full cry came out. She checked the oxygen line and put two fingers where she wanted the leading bearers to slow.

Dhruv stood back. The lifting was not his work to improve by adding an injured hand.

The man with the pale curve left the rope.

"Ruko."

The constable moved across his path.

"Mareez jaayega. Tum yahin raho."

"Yeh doosra raasta kisne khola?"

The rear bearers had already turned towards the courtyard. One had to take a half-step back to keep the stretcher level through the first gate.

The man lunged for the iron leaf.

Dhruv caught it with his left shoulder before it struck the bearer. His dressed palm hit the latch plate. Pain shot across the old rope burns.

He did not reach for the pouch.

"Andar," he said to the carrying team.

The constable caught the man's elbow. The pale curve scraped the gate, taking a thin white shaving off the paint. A second man came forward from the rope.

The building owner seized the inside handle with both hands.

"Mera gate chhod!"

A neighbour reached her from the verandah. Together they pulled the leaf towards the wall, not shut across the patient. Dhruv got his shoulder out of the pinch and stood between the hinge and the passing stretcher.

At the street's end the police vehicle turned in. Its front wheel mounted the edge of the paving to get round the waiting carts. Two more constables got out before the driver had straightened it.

The men by the post stopped advancing.

The stretcher crossed the first threshold.

---

The courtyard was not long. Under a load, every object in it acquired an edge.

A bucket hook at the height of a sleeve. A patch of soap-water near the tap. The bent foot of a washing stand. Dhruv named them as the bearers approached. The neighbour moved the stand against the wall; the building owner kicked a folded sack over the wet patch and warned the men not to put a heel on its loose end.

The medic walked at the patient's head. She did not look back at the shouting.

"Seedha. Ab dheere."

The wife went behind the oxygen, close enough to hear her husband breathe. She was no longer promising him arrival. She named the things passing above him instead.

"Kapde hain upar. Ab gate. Roshni aayegi. Aankh band kar lo."

At the rear threshold the leading porter stopped.

The drain opening lay exactly where Dhruv had said. The route had space for the stretcher only if its left edge stayed close to the gatepost. He put his uninjured hand flat against that post.

"Meri taraf. Thoda. Bas."

The porter placed his foot on sound stone. The man behind him waited until the weight settled before stepping through. Cloth whispered against brick. The oxygen cylinder cleared the latch by the width of two fingers.

Then they were in the alley.

The trolley waited at the far end. A nurse had come with it. The medic handed over while the porters made the next transfer, giving the delay along with the condition in which the man had left the school.

The wife tried to follow when the trolley began moving. The courtyard owner had come after them with her schoolbag.

Dhruv took it with his left and held it where she could reach.

She caught the strap. Went after the trolley.

There had been no moment in which the patient rose and thanked the people who had carried him. The doors took him while his wife was still walking quickly enough to lose a sandal.

A porter brought it back from the ramp.

---

By the time Dhruv returned to the first gate, the shield-man had been put beside the police vehicle. His pale edge was gone. One constable watched his hands while another asked the shopkeeper to describe what had happened.

The older man still held the towel against the boy's bleeding nose. He looked towards Dhruv's dressing, where a dark line had appeared.

"Hamaare ladke ko bhi—"

"Le chalo," Dhruv said. "Yahin hospital hai. Kisi ki parchi nahi chahiye."

The man opened his mouth, then shut it. The boy got up slowly. His cloth band stayed on the arm; no one made him remove it to be examined.

At the iron post the writing board lay face down. The cracked phone's owner stood beside the constable, angry enough for his account to need repeating. The constable let him finish before asking for the sequence again.

The police were bringing a towing vehicle for the loaded trailer. Until then, the opened courtyard could carry people in a single controlled direction, with someone at each gate.

The owner looked at the wet clothes on her floor.

"Mere bachche upar hain," she told the constable. "Aap chale gaye toh?"

He asked his senior to leave a man at the gate. When permission came, he gave her the post number and showed her which vehicle would stay.

Only then did she bend to pick up the clothes.

Dhruv helped with the part he could do one-handed. His palm dressing would need changing. The gate had a bright scrape through its paint. Neither had been the largest possible cost.

That did not make either imaginary.

---

Rudra knew the ambulance patient had arrived because the doctor left the desk in the middle of a sentence.

Not running. Moving with the particular speed which made everybody else get out of the way.

The nurse who remained brought the man's wife to a bench and asked her to wait while they examined him. She had the schoolbag again. One sandal was on crookedly.

Rudra saw her through the opening between the curtain and its rail.

He had once believed the worst part of bringing someone to a hospital was being stopped outside a door. He could picture another door now, in a washing courtyard, which a woman had needed permission from nobody to open and protection from somebody to keep open afterwards.

Vedant came to his bedside.

"Aa gaye. Treatment shuru hai."

"Saans?"

"Oxygen pe hain. Doctor andar hain."

Rudra let the answer be as small as it was. Across the passage the wife folded and unfolded the loose end of her bag's strap.

"Unko pata hai?" he asked.

"Wahi bataaya hai."

The woman looked towards the treatment doors. Rudra looked away before she had to acknowledge another stranger watching her wait.

---

Dhruv came back with his palm redressed and a warning not to lean on it again. The angle-ring had stayed in the pouch throughout. He set the pouch on the chair beside him rather than wearing its cord against the new padding.

Veer studied the thicker dressing.

"Gate ne kaata?"

"Latch ne."

"Complaint gate ko deni padegi. Latch toh employee hai."

Dhruv looked at him until the corner of his mouth moved.

"Tumhara review kitne din chalega?"

"Jab tak discharge na kar dein."

Veer's mother, returning with water, said she could arrange an early end to the commentary without troubling any doctor. He accepted the glass in a chastened silence which lasted until she had turned away.

Aadhya smiled. Then her right hand slipped a little on the pillow, and she had to ask for it to be placed back properly. The numb fingers had not noticed the edge.

The smile faded while she watched the nurse protect them from a pressure she could not feel.

"Kal bhi yeh covered rahega?" she asked.

"Doctor dekhenge. Feeling aa jaaye tab bhi jaldi kaam mat shuru kar dena."

Her mother was coming for the evening review. Aadhya had asked for an ordinary change of clothes, loose sleeves, nothing which had to be pulled hard over the hands.

Not a uniform. Not clothes in which somebody could photograph a champion leaving for her own territory.

She asked Vedant to tell the desk that only her family should be told where she would rest after the doctor permitted her to leave. Anyone claiming to have sent a vehicle could wait outside.

He took the request to the nurse rather than promising to guard every gate himself.

---

The recorder-lady arrived near the end of the afternoon.

Ira heard her before opening her eye: the familiar small clearing of the throat, never quite a cough, with which she had announced herself at a desk when she thought its occupant had been sitting too long.

"Kya laayi?" Ira asked.

"Khaana."

"Aur?"

"Chamach."

Ira opened the uncovered eye. The older woman stood between her and the strongest light until Sitara adjusted the curtain.

"Kaam nahi laayi?"

"Tumhe dekhne aayi hoon. Office kholne nahi."

The food went on the little table. Sitara had already checked what Ira could eat. The recorder-lady placed the cup on her right, described where it stood, and waited for her to find it herself.

Ira's fingers trembled round the handle. She put the cup down before lifting it.

The older woman slid a folded towel beneath it so it would not scrape away while Ira changed her grip.

"Main de doon?"

"Nahi. Bas paas rakhna."

"Yahin hoon."

For a while their conversation concerned the thickness of dal. Too thick to drink, too thin to accuse anyone of having cooked a proper meal. Ira argued that the hospital had probably commissioned it to prevent patients from staying for the food. The recorder-lady told her to complain softly; the cook might take a complaint as an invitation to add more water.

Ira laughed once. It hurt behind the dressing. She let the older woman move the bowl closer and stopped trying to turn her head towards every sound.

Rudra heard none of the joke clearly from his bed. He heard the change in her voice.

There had been a chair at Rajghat which she had chosen to stand beside. There were people who came when she was hurt. No one at a checkpoint had the authority to make those things temporary.

---

The school called before evening.

A small delivery had reached it by the checked approach, with a police escort for the last stretch. Not the full flour consignment. Enough food for the people who had begun stretching their dry stores into another meal.

Vedant gave that news to the group without calling the roads safe.

The northern trailer had been moved. A constable stayed at the courtyard gate until the owner could secure it for the night. Elsewhere, another driver had been turned away from a lane whose entrance had been open an hour before.

Rudra asked for a fresh update to go to the critical-care desk as well. His father did not need to be moved; the people going home after duty did.

He was thinking of the nurse who had waited while he cleaned his hands before a visit. She would have to leave this building eventually. So would the man who had taken Ram's chest image. So would the porter still bringing cylinders up a stair.

The hospital was not an island merely because he had learned to remain inside it.

---

The man with the reopened stitches returned to the visitors' seats after his statement.

He did not ask for Veer to be brought out again. He gave the guard a folded receipt and asked that Vedant look at it.

"Flour waale ka hai," he said. "Jo gaadi mein bacha hai, woh bhi chala gaya toh kal mujhe jawaab dena padega."

Vedant met him at the recess, leaving the bays quiet. Sitara stayed where she could hear the patients if they called. The recorder-lady came to the clinical door with Ira's permission to hear anything concerning the kitchen records.

The receipt had been kept in a plastic sleeve. Its corners were worn, but the amount and the words *paid in full* were clear. There was a dispatch number beneath them and a promised collection at the grain-market warehouse.

"Kisne paise diye?" Vedant asked.

"Humne jama kiye. Chaar ghar se. Rasad ke liye. Hamaare log hain school mein bhi."

"Naam ki jagah Kul kyun poochh rahe the phir?"

The man looked towards his freshly wrapped forearm.

"Pehle chori hui thi. Bola tha, maal uth gaya. Doosri taraf pahunchaa diya."

"Kisne bola?"

"Jahan se khareeda."

He tapped the seller's line on the receipt.

Vedant asked the constable to hear the account. A piece of paper presented after an assault could be evidence of a transaction; it could not turn the assault into an official duty.

The constable wrote the man's statement in his own notebook and asked him to identify the men who had stood at the cart. The visitor named two. When asked who had struck the first relief driver, he said he had not seen that blow.

His account stopped there. Nobody filled the gap for him.

---

At the clinical door the recorder-lady asked whether the school kitchen had kept its purchase note.

Vedant showed her the copy sent by the cook. The original was with the driver, who had been told to keep it dry. The surviving flour sacks were still awaiting a documented move from the carrier; police would not let another group simply take them while arguing about yesterday's losses.

She studied the note without announcing a verdict.

"Transport waale ki kitaab dekhni padegi. Aur bechne waale ki. Yeh alag kaagaz hain."

Ira called softly from the bay. The older woman went back at once.

"Kya hua?"

"Aawaaz aa rahi thi."

"Isliye khadi thi darwaaze pe. Tumhe uthna na pade."

Ira asked what the receipt actually said. The recorder-lady read only the relevant lines. No one put the small print in front of an injured eye and asked for a miracle.

"Maal diya, yeh likha hai?" Ira asked.

"Abhi is copy mein paise liye likha hai. Dispatch number bhi hai."

"Toh number ka matlab poochhna. Apne aap gaadi mat maan lena."

The older woman looked at her over the paper.

"Wahi karungi."

Ira lay back. Her right eye shut before she finished asking when the doctor would come again.

"Shaam ka review abhi hai," Sitara said. "Kal ka kal dekhenge."

---

Aadhya's mother brought the loose-sleeved clothes. She also brought a comb and asked which way her daughter wanted her hair tied.

Aadhya said the usual way.

Halfway through, she asked for it to be looser.

Her mother undid the tie without saying it had always been comfortable before. The hands which normally did it were lying on the pillow between them.

The doctor examined those hands again that evening. There was no sudden return of sensation. He described how the dressings would be managed outside observation if the next check allowed it, what help she would need, when to return. Permission to rest elsewhere would not mean permission to put the injured fingers to work.

Ira's eye team would examine her again in the morning. Rudra still had no weight-bearing clearance. Veer was to leave only with family help after his remaining checks, not walk out because a better rumour was waiting outside.

The ordinary future returned in pieces: a place to sleep, a lift to arrange, clothes with sleeves which would pass over a dressing.

Rudra heard his mother's bay settling for the evening. Ram remained in critical care. The new patient from the school was still being treated; his wife had been given an update and somewhere to sit.

A whole day had passed without making either man's next day certain.

---

Just before the visiting queue thinned, the guard brought another envelope to Vedant.

A woman from the seed-shop group had left it. The recorder-lady opened it with him at the visitors' recess, beside the constable who had returned for the first receipt's details.

Inside was a second paid claim for flour.

Different purchaser. Another collection amount. The same warehouse seller's stamp.

Vedant put the plastic-sleeved receipt beside it.

The recorder-lady ran one finger down each page, stopping below the words *paid in full*.

"Number ek hai," she said.

The constable turned his notebook back to the visitor's account.

From inside the bay came the small sound of Ira asking for the light to be lowered. The older woman waited for Sitara to answer, then looked again at the two receipts.

Both sides were claiming to have paid for the same promised flour.

Only one load had reached the crossing.

By morning, the loader whose leg had been trapped beneath the cart was being called a thief.

His sister heard it outside the hospital gate. She came back through security carrying breakfast, put the tiffin down too hard, and asked whether the police had come to arrest him.

The nurse fetched the constable who had taken his account. He told her that her brother was a witness to the loading and an injured complainant. Nobody had asked her to bring him out for an arrest.

"Bahar naam le rahe hain," she said.

He asked who. She looked towards the gate, as though the men might have followed her in with the word still in their mouths.

Ira heard the exchange while waiting for her morning eye examination.

The recorder-lady sat beside her, holding the closed food bag in both hands. She had not brought the receipts into the examination room.

"Uska employer?" Ira asked.

"Bahar jo keh rahe hain, abhi unka poochh rahe hain."

"Nahi. Kaam kisne diya tha usko. Usse bhi poochhna."

The older woman nodded. A constable writing the allegation down was better than a crowd repeating it. It was not yet an answer to where the flour had come from.

The eye-room door opened. Ira went in with a nurse's arm offered on her uncovered side.

---

Permission to leave observation came with instructions which filled more time than the act of signing the discharge paper.

The left eye would stay covered as directed. Bright light, strain, reaching through her other sight: all to be avoided. There would be another examination the next day, earlier if the specified changes appeared. She repeated the important parts back before the doctor let the paper go to the recorder-lady.

"Ghar pe akeli?"

"Nahi," the older woman said. "Mere saath. Neeche ka kamra hai. Seedhi nahi. Wapas laane ki gaadi bhi rahegi."

Ira would rest in the Trust caretaker's ground-floor guest room, with the older woman staying nearby. The records office off the verandah could remain someone else's work for the morning. Being allowed out of a ward did not improve the depth in her uncovered eye.

At the door she reached for the handle and closed her fingers too early.

The nurse opened it. Ira let her.

---

Aadhya's review ended with a similar distinction.

She could go with her mother. She could not use the hands simply because her legs worked. The thumb and index finger were still numb; dressings and follow-up would continue. For the ride, both hands needed support clear of door edges and impatient grips.

Her mother was taking her to rest near the recorder-lady while the family's usual route was being checked. Sitara would accompany them. There would be room to lie down, food, help washing and a vehicle for the next dressing visit. No one had asked a Kul escort to choose the address.

Aadhya looked at the discharge slip held in her mother's hand.

"Doctor ne bola hai, main chal sakti hoon."

"Kamre tak. Sabko dikhane nahi. Gaadi se utarke chair hai."

"Maine sabko dikhane ko nahi bola."

"Maine bhi tumhe kamzor nahi bola."

Aadhya stopped arguing. Her mother tucked the pillow under the dressed hands without touching the numb fingertips.

---

Rudra remained for wound review and the ankle. He still could not put weight on it. Ram was still in critical care; the drain had not been removed, and the oxygen remained. Rukmini's doctor was considering what support she would need outside the bay, not sending her to an unexamined house that morning.

Vedant stayed with them. Dadi sat beside Rukmini while Sitara went to the vehicle with the two young women.

Veer's family had taken him back to the nearby room after his checks the previous evening. He called before the girls left and complained that people were holding a transport meeting without consulting the man most qualified to criticize its chairs.

"Review khatam nahi hua?" Aadhya asked.

"Extension mila hai."

He wanted to know who was driving. Which road had been checked. Who would call if the approach changed. Practical questions borrowed the space usually occupied by his jokes.

Ira gave him the recorder-lady's contact through Sitara, then told him not to sit with his knee hanging down while he interrogated anyone else about care.

His mother answered from near the phone.

"Sun liya."

The call ended on Veer protesting that he had only changed position for a minute.

---

The journey to Rajghat took longer than a rumour said it would.

Their driver stopped at the police post to ask about the actual approach. Twice he let people walking with bundles cross before moving the car. A whitewashed wall bore the names of two different protection groups, one painted across the other's last word.

Aadhya watched it pass. Ira kept her uncovered side out of the sun.

At the Trust, the caretaker had put a chair beside the vehicle's stopping place. Sitara guided Ira from the car; Aadhya stood on her own feet, then accepted the chair for the longer verandah rather than making her mother argue in public.

The neem-lined vault below the satsang room remained closed. Nobody was bringing out the fourteen boxes to solve a dispute over a flour truck.

In the office off the verandah, two paid claims lay under a paperweight.

Beside them, the recorder-lady had left a blank sheet.

At its top she had written one question: **WHAT ACTUALLY LEFT?**

---

The carrier driver did not come to the Trust. His vehicle was still in a police holding yard, and he would not abandon it while people were saying its surviving load belonged to them.

The recorder-lady went there with the constable. Ira stayed in the darkened room; Sitara put the discharge instructions where Aadhya's mother could find them without waking either patient.

In the yard, flour had settled into the driver's shirt seams. He stood when the constable arrived, then saw the older woman and reached into the tin box beneath his seat.

"Phir parchi?"

"Is baar shuru se," she said. "Uthaaya kahan se?"

He laid the school kitchen's note on the bonnet. Beneath it he put his loading sheet.

The note asked for flour and gave the relief kitchen's destination. It did not record a payment to the warehouse named on the two receipts.

The loading sheet had eighteen sacks entered against a small mill beyond the main market. The driver had signed for them there. The loader had countersigned. Below, under two further collections, were the warehouse seller's address and an empty space where weights should have gone.

"Teen jagah jaana tha?" the constable asked.

"Pehle chakki. Phir yeh godaam. Phir rasoi. Godaam ka shutter nahi khula. Phone kiya toh bola, baad mein aana. Rasoi waale do baar poochh chuke the. Main chakki ka maal leke chala."

The trip number printed at the top of that sheet was the one on both paid receipts.

It belonged to a route booking. It did not say whose stock had been loaded.

"Maal kisne gin ke diya?" the recorder-lady asked.

The driver gave her a name and a number. The constable wrote them down before anybody called.

---

The miller's daughter came with a flour-dusted notebook and her father on the phone. The constable spoke to him first, then to the carrier's owner on the business number painted on the vehicle. The loader worked for that carrier, not for either protection group.

The daughter opened the notebook on her knee.

Her father had offered eighteen sacks to the common kitchen after the siege. His entry named the kitchen. The weighing slip had the same date, the carrier's registration and the loader's signature. She had watched the sacks go up the ramp.

The notebook alone was her family's account. The driver alone could have been defending his own load. Together with the mill's retained weighing slip, the kitchen's earlier request and the carrier's collection record, their accounts described one journey without requiring either private purchaser's flour to have travelled on it.

The constable asked the daughter whether anyone had paid her father on behalf of the warehouse.

"Nahi. Daana unka hota toh daan hum kaise karte?"

She looked at the two paid receipts.

"Unse hamara khareedna bhi nahi hua. Inki baat inke bechne waale se poochhiye."

The driver pulled the tarpaulin back far enough for the police count. Fifteen sacks remained usable. Three had been torn or spoiled at the crossing. The damaged sacks were kept apart; no one tried sweeping food out of the gutter and calling it a full delivery.

The police allowed the fifteen to go to the kitchen with a recorded handover and an escort. The ownership allegations and assault statements stayed open. The driver would have to return for further questions.

He folded the original note along its old creases.

"Mere ladke ko chor mat likhna," he said.

"Tumhare ladke ko?"

"Saath kaam karta hai. Hospital mein hai."

The recorder-lady put that sentence on her blank sheet too, below the eighteen and the fifteen.

---

Near noon, Ira woke to the sound of a cup being set down outside.

She knew that careful contact: the recorder-lady placing something on a surface without letting its base ring. There was more than tea to tell.

"Kitni der?" Ira asked.

"Pehle khaana."

"Uske baad?"

"Chhe minute. Tum sunogi. Main padhoongi. Phir bas."

Ira agreed before the older woman could shorten the allowance.

They sat in the guest-room doorway, Ira with the uncovered eye closed. Aadhya listened from her cot across the room. Her mother held a bowl while she ate; they had stopped pretending the arrangement could be made invisible.

The recorder-lady read the findings without asking Ira to identify a mark. No inner light moved behind the covering. A pencil scratched beside the older woman's own voice.

"Do paid orders," Ira said. "Kitna?"

"Baarah bore ek ke. Aath doosre ke."

"Aur chakki ke athaarah. Seller ne kis baat pe kaha ki bhej diye?"

"Abhi usse poochhna hai. School mein. Dono khareedne waale wahin milenge. Police bhi."

"Pehle alag-alag jawaab lena. Ek doosre ka sunkar na badlein."

The recorder-lady marked the point, then closed the notebook.

Ira wanted to hear the rest after the meeting. She also wanted to go and say the questions herself. She had imagined leaving hospital as a return to the person who could do those things.

Aadhya's mother offered her water. Ira reached too quickly and missed the cup's side.

She waited for it to be placed in her hand.

"Baad mein bataa dena," she said.

The recorder-lady rose without making her ask twice.

---

At the school, children were being told not to run past the flour.

The carrier stood in the forecourt. Fifteen sacks came down under police supervision, one at a time, into a dry classroom. The cook signed for the usable count; the miller's daughter took a copy for her father.

Beyond the forecourt stood the long teaching block. One hall held bedding. A side corridor led through to the play-yard, where the cooking shed had been working since dawn. At the far wall, a narrow maintenance gate opened on the municipal pump passage. The teacher kept its key with the others at her waist.

These were ordinary school precautions. She explained them because the two constables had asked how people could leave if the front approach filled again.

"Peechhe gaadi nahi aa sakti," she said. "Chalne ki jagah hai. Pump ke baad chaudi gali."

One constable went to see it rather than taking her word for the width.

The recorder-lady waited on the front verandah, under a drawing of a mango with its English name rubbed half away. The seller arrived between two men from the warehouse. The constable asked them to wait while he spoke to him separately.

The two buyers had already given their accounts apart.

---

When they sat together, the injured visitor kept his wrapped forearm off the table. Across from him, the woman who had sent the second receipt put eight household contributions in a little exercise book beside it.

She was not the woman who had gripped the tree. She had spent that morning looking for the sacks her neighbours believed she had purchased.

"Main ab unko kya boloon?" she asked.

The seller looked at the doorway. The driver stood there with the loading sheet.

"Gaadi ka number diya tha," the seller said. "Gaadi gayi hai. Aapko toh dikh rahi hai."

The constable asked him where the twelve sacks and the eight had been weighed.

"Booking ke baad bhejna tha. Phir jo hua sheher mein—"

"Bheje the?"

"Driver ko godaam jaana tha."

"Gaya tha," the driver said. "Shutter band. Phone pe aapne bola baad mein."

The seller's right hand covered the margin of his own order book.

The recorder-lady waited until he moved it. Two paid orders had the trip number written beside them. Nothing recorded a completed loading there. A booking had been made before the road trouble. The flour for those two orders had not been handed to this driver.

The constable asked the seller to say that in his own words.

He did.

The injured visitor leaned forward, then stopped when his dressing touched the table edge.

"Mujhe bola udhar pahunch gaya. In logon ne le liya."

The woman opposite him opened her book to the page where she had written down the seller's message.

"Mujhe bola Rakshak waalon ne rok liya. Paisa humne diya, hifaazat woh kar rahe. Aapke hi muh se suna."

The seller shook his head.

"Maine kaha tha raste mein—"

"Aapka maal raste mein tha hi nahi," the recorder-lady said.

From outside came the scrape of the final sack across the store-room threshold.

The seller looked towards the sound as though it might offer another account of the journey.

It did not.

---

There was no single quiet instant in which everybody became reasonable.

The woman demanded the flour or the money. The injured visitor demanded to know whether stock still existed behind the warehouse shutter. The driver asked who would pay for the three ruined donated sacks. The seller complained that he had lost goods during the siege too, as though a real loss elsewhere could make these deliveries happen afterwards.

The constable separated the questions.

The payment receipts were genuine. The orders remained unfulfilled. The donor's eighteen were not a substitute for the seller's twenty. The seller would have to account for his stock and the money in the investigation; no schoolchild's meal would be handed over to close his debt.

"Chori kisne ki phir?" a man outside asked.

"Is gaadi se tumhara maal gaya hi nahi," the driver said. "Mere saathi ka pair gaya kaam se. Uska hisaab kaun dega?"

The visitor looked down at the darkening edge of his own dressing.

He had helped bring that man out. Before the rescue, he had asked for Veer's voice to make the road his group's to control. The reopened wound and the account already given to police were both still there.

"Usko chor kisne bola?" he asked.

The man at the door looked away.

The visitor asked the constable for a copy of the corrected account. He would take it to the men who had collected the money with him.

"Sirf yahan mat bolna," the miller's daughter said. "Jahan chor bola, wahin kehna galti thi."

He nodded once. It did not look comfortable.

---

By the time the meeting ended, the cook had begun serving fresh bread on the verandah.

At the front of the serving queue stood a man with the narrow arm-band of the northern checkpoint. Behind him, a woman whose family had fled the opposite lane held two bowls nested together. The cook gave both the same instruction: leave room for the people carrying the hot pot.

The broad man from the market crossing watched them from the school gate.

"Yeh sab yahin rahenge?" he asked the teacher.

"Jab tak ghar jaane laayak nahi."

"Aur rakhega kaun? Tum?"

The constable turned towards his voice. The man's hands were empty. Men behind him carried rolled sleeping mats and a length of blue cloth.

"Hum pehra denge," he said. "Main hall hamaare parivaaron ka kar do. Baaki ke liye doosri jagah hai."

The teacher looked past him at the children eating on the verandah.

She did not ask which children he meant.

"Yahan jagah naam se nahi milti," she said.

---

The constable did not let the mats enter the school.

He told the broad man to move away from the gate. The teacher had not asked his group to take charge. The other constable called for more men while the seller was taken outside to continue his statement.

Across the street, the blue cloth went up between two shutter hooks.

The men sat beneath it. Not far enough away for the families on the verandah to stop watching them.

The recorder-lady asked the teacher how many people needed help moving.

"Abhi?"

"Agar karna pada toh."

They went through the hall together. A woman with a sleeping infant refused to leave until her husband returned from looking for their older son. Two men with leg injuries needed a vehicle. An old woman could walk but could not carry the bundle which contained what remained of her home.

The teacher did not cross anybody off merely for being difficult to move.

A municipal worker reached by telephone could open the dispensary waiting room beyond the pump passage. It was smaller than the hall, but it had a clear approach for a van. Police checked that route. The teacher began sending the easiest escorted groups there while a vehicle was arranged for the men on the floor.

The cook looked at the fifteen flour sacks and then at the people still waiting for a meal.

"Aag band kar doon?"

"Ek pot abhi utaar lo," the teacher said. "Log niklenge toh khaake niklein."

The cooker lids rattled across the play-yard. On the front verandah, children were putting their sandals on with bread still in their hands.

---

Sitara took the call at the Trust, outside the guest room.

She arranged a vehicle through the caretaker and gave the checked pump-passage approach, not the school gate now gathering watchers. Aadhya's mother stayed with the girls. The vehicle which had brought them would remain available for medical care.

"Kya hua?" Aadhya asked when Sitara came back.

"School mein jagah ko leke dabaav hai. Kuch parivaar shift kar rahe hain."

Ira lay still. Her uncovered eye had begun to ache after lunch even without a page to read. She asked whether the paid-claim correction had reached the loader's family.

"Constable ne phone kiya hai. Seller ne delivery na hone ki baat maani. Baaki enquiry mein."

Ira nodded. A receipt had been corrected, not the injured leg. She asked Sitara whether the loader would still have wages while he recovered. The carrier's owner had not answered that question yet. Today they had taken one untrue word off a wounded worker, if the people saying it agreed to stop.

She turned her face away from the light and let the adults make the next call.

Aadhya asked Sitara to hold the phone for a message to her family group. She did not want somebody to photograph her dressings and say Prakriti had summoned her to the school.

"Main wahin hoon jahan Maa hain," she said. "Ilaaj chal raha hai. Mere naam pe kisi ko hataana mat."

She heard the recording played back. Her voice sounded thinner than she expected.

Her mother waited until Sitara sent it, then straightened the pillow.

"Ghar waale samajh jaayenge," she said.

"Sab nahi."

"Sabko samjhaana aaj ka kaam nahi hai."

Aadhya looked at her mother's hands. Flour had caught in the skin beside one nail from helping with lunch. There was no band, no proof to display. When the bowl tilted, those hands steadied it before either of them mentioned lineage again.

---

At the hospital, the corrected account reached the loader's sister before it reached Rudra.

She listened twice. Asked whether someone could give her brother the same news without telling him to get up and sign anything. The nurse said he was resting; the copy could wait beside his bed.

Vedant relayed the school situation to the hospital's police desk. Dhruv had finished handing over his ghat watch. He would meet the municipal transport worker at the school, not bring the injured students to it.

Rudra asked if another vehicle was needed.

"Ek mil gayi. Doosri ka poochh rahe hain."

"Aur log?"

"Police maangi hai. Dhruv jaa rahe hain."

Rudra's hand tightened on the bed rail. The wrist support stopped the movement before it could become useful.

Across the passage Rukmini asked Dadi for the water, waited while it was put within reach, and drank from the side she could manage. Then she looked at her son.

He loosened his hand.

"Maa, school se log nikaal rahe hain."

"Suna."

"Humne unko wahaan khaana bheja tha."

Rukmini watched him for a moment.

"Isliye ab unko wahin rehna padega?"

He shook his head.

"Nahi."

There was something he had been trying to demand from a rescue: that it stay rescued, that the first effort buy the right not to fear for the same people again. His mother was holding a cup with the arm which still worked. His father was beyond a door no promise could open safely.

Rudra asked Vedant to tell them when the last school family reached the dispensary.

"Naam poore gin lena," he said. "Sirf gaadiyaan nahi."

---

The injured visitor returned before the next vehicle reached the school.

He had taken the corrected account to the collection point. Two men had come back with him to hear it from the constable. A third had refused to leave the blue cloth across the road.

"Maine delivery ki baat galat maani," the visitor said, standing where the driver could hear him. "Is gaadi mein hamaare bore nahi the. Loader ne nahi liye. Yeh baat aage bolna."

A few people listened. One asked whether his apology would bring their money back.

"Nahi," he said.

The recorder-lady saw him look at the school store before lowering the hand which held the paper. This time he did not point anyone towards the flour.

The driver shut the carrier's tailboard. His exhausted relief did not look like forgiveness.

The constable gave the teacher the number for the approaching reinforcement. They were held at another confrontation on the main road. She asked that they meet the next vehicle from the pump side if the front street could not be kept clear.

Then she went back to the hall. Eighteen residents still needed to leave: the two men who could not walk, the mother with the infant, the old woman with the bundle, children, adults waiting to keep those children together.

The older son had been found at the dispensary with his father. A worker came back with the news. The mother began gathering her baby's things at once.

It should have been an easier last group than the ones before it. The destination was open. A vehicle was coming. The food had arrived and, at last, been accounted for.

---

At the front gate the broad man put one hand on the upright and asked who had given the teacher permission to empty the hall.

"Abhi toh tumhare logon ko dene bol rahe the," the injured visitor said.

"Toh school chhod do. Hum dekh lenge."

"Andar log hain."

"Nikaal rahe ho na."

Behind the broad man, someone began untying the blue cloth from its shutter hook. The other end stayed fixed across the street, pulling the strip diagonal as he carried it towards the gate.

The constable told him to stop. There was no change in control of the building to announce. A second school worker closed the gate leaf until only a person's width remained for people leaving.

A pale edge rose beside the broad man's wrist.

The recorder-lady was close enough to hear the teacher's keys strike one another when she turned.

"Peechhe se," the teacher said to her. "Ab sab peechhe se."

The older woman took the residents' sheet and went through the side corridor. At the play-yard the cook was covering the remaining pot. Two helpers stacked the empty utensils against the wall, leaving the route to the maintenance gate clear.

She told them not to stack anything else. The vehicle was to stop beyond the pump passage; the men who needed lifting would be brought through together.

The teacher came behind her with the key. She opened the maintenance gate and tied it back. A worker went down the passage to warn the approaching driver.

For a few minutes the school had two different sounds: the roughening voices at the front, and people at the back explaining patiently which bundle belonged with which child.

---

Dhruv reached the pump passage as its worker came out.

He had the newly dressed hand held clear of his side. The ring was tied away again. He asked where the vehicle could stand without blocking the lane, then followed the worker towards the open gate.

The recorder-lady saw him from the play-yard and lifted the residents' sheet.

"Athaarah abhi. Do uthaane padenge."

"Madad aa rahi hai," he said. "Raasta khula rakho."

The words were followed by a hard metallic blow from the front of the school.

Then another.

A child at the hall's rear door began to cry. His mother moved him away from the frame; she had heard something strike the other end of the corridor.

The constable's whistle sounded twice, shrill and close together. Feet crossed the verandah above its echo. The school worker who had held the front gate came stumbling out into the play-yard with blood on his chin.

"Andar mat aane do—"

The cook dropped his empty ladle.

Beyond the rear doorway, someone pulled a hanging cotton screen sideways. For an instant the recorder-lady saw a man's forearm, a burning twist of cloth held low beside it, and the terrified face of the old woman on the floor behind him.

The screen brightened along its hem.

The man was gone before Dhruv crossed the yard.

Smoke came out through the doorway where the last eighteen people were waiting to leave.

The cook struck the burning screen with the lid of his largest pot.

The cloth fell. Fire ran along a fold on the floor before his helper emptied the washing bucket over it. For a moment the doorway cleared enough to show another flame farther inside, where bedding had been pushed against a wall.

Then smoke lowered across the room.

"Sab bahar!" the teacher shouted.

A woman came through with her infant pressed to her chest. An older boy tried to turn back for his shoes. The recorder-lady caught his sleeve and moved with him towards the maintenance gate.

Dhruv reached the hall door. The air stung before he had taken a second breath. He saw the old woman beside her bundle and offered his uninjured arm.

"Yeh rehne do. Mere saath."

"Ismein—"

"Aap pehle."

She left it. He brought her to the open yard, bent double when his next breath became a cough, and let a helper take her farther.

Inside, two men were still on the bedding. Others had started trying to lift them by their arms. Dhruv stopped that from the threshold.

"Pair ghaseeto mat. Chair laao."

The cook's second helper ran for the school chairs. The municipal worker was already returning through the maintenance gate with the van driver behind him. Between them and the able residents, there were hands to help without adding Dhruv's damaged palm.

The teacher pointed them towards the men. She could still see where they lay. She stayed at the door and called them by name while the others brought the chairs in low beneath the smoke.

The recorder-lady called the fire service from the yard. Gave the school, the street and the rear approach. Said that people were still inside. She did not waste the first clear words describing who had started it.

At the cooking shed, the cook shut down the stove. His helper went with the teacher to cut the building's electricity at the outside switch box. The loose wire over the rear door stopped humming.

The yard light went out. The flame inside the doorway was already brighter.

---

The maintenance gate was open. People still jammed it.

The mother with the infant had stopped to see whether the boy behind her belonged to the woman following. The old woman was trying to turn, calling for the bundle. An elbow caught the tied-back gate leaf and loosened its cord.

The recorder-lady set her phone down on the yard wall, still connected to the dispatcher. She took the gate leaf clear with both hands.

"Aage jagah hai," she called. "Pump ke baad rukna. Darwaaze mein nahi."

The municipal worker went ahead to receive them. The driver and kitchen helpers brought one seated man out of the hall, keeping the injured leg supported and clear of the frame. The second man waited while another chair was brought level with his bedding.

Dhruv stayed outside the smoke and kept the yard clear. When the woman with the infant faltered, he asked a helper to take her elbow instead of trying to hold her with the wounded hand. His cough had begun to hurt beneath his ribs.

The gate worker with the bleeding chin sat against the kitchen wall. He was trying to rise and go to the front.

"Wahan police hai," Dhruv told him. "Tum yahin raho. Naam bataao apna. Ambulance ko bataana hai."

The man gave it. Speaking held him in place for another few seconds. Dhruv passed the name to the dispatcher through the recorder-lady's phone, then asked for medical help at the wide lane beyond the pump.

Across the yard the teacher called for a child. Two adults answered from different directions. Neither had the child by the hand.

She called again.

A little figure crossed behind the smoke at the doorway. Then the first seated man needed the threshold, and for a few moments there was no view into the hall at all.

---

At the front, the constable's radio lay beneath an overturned school bench.

He had kept hold of his lathi. His other arm was pinned between the gatepost and the pale edge at the broad man's wrist. Blood ran from his hairline into one eyebrow.

"Peechhe hato!" he shouted.

The man looked over his shoulder towards the hall.

Smoke had begun to come through the high windows. One of the men carrying the blue cloth dropped his end. It sagged across the forecourt, still attached to the shutter on the other side of the road.

The injured visitor caught the broad man's sleeve with his good hand.

"Andar aag hai."

"Dikh raha hai. Kisne—"

"Haath hata."

The pale edge held another heartbeat. From inside the hall a child screamed for her mother.

The visitor dropped his papers and hooked his good arm under the man's elbow. He pulled sideways, away from the post. The broad man turned on him; the pale edge turned with him. The constable wrenched himself free.

The second constable came down the verandah with the flour driver beside him. Together they got the injured officer through the open side of the gate. The miller's daughter cleared people out of their way.

Behind them, the broad man reached for the blue cloth instead of the fallen radio.

---

Fourteen residents reached the dispensary in the first movement through the passage. Once a helper had retied the gate leaf securely, the recorder-lady had followed them with the sheet and her phone.

The recorder-lady counted the infant separately. She asked each adult who had come with them. A boy answered for his mother until she asked him to let her hear the mother's voice too.

The woman had believed her daughter was ahead with her brother. The brother had believed she was beside their mother.

The recorder-lady kept the place beside the girl's name empty.

Behind them, the first injured man arrived in his chair. The second followed with his adult nephew beside him. Seventeen.

The teacher reached the dispensary doorway after the last chair. She took the sheet and read the names herself, touching no mark until she saw the person it described. The child did not appear between one count and the next.

"Chhoti?" her mother said.

The boy looked back towards the pump passage. He had a yellow bag in mind, a rabbit on its zip, and the place beside their bedding where his sister had left it.

The teacher called the municipal worker. At the school, he put his phone into Dhruv's uninjured hand.

"Ek bachchi nahi mili. Naam bolti hoon. Fire waalon ko bataao. Maa ko yahin roka hai."

The woman tried to go past the dispensary nurse. The teacher moved in front of her, not seizing her, asking about the girl's clothes.

Giving the description made the mother stay long enough to finish it. It did not make staying easy.

---

At the school, the worker called fire control again. Dhruv passed the child's description to the dispatcher.

The rear hall door no longer showed a room. Smoke came out beneath its lintel and rolled along the wall before lifting into the yard. The cook and his helper stood clear, coughing. The empty chairs which had carried the men were coming back through the passage; Dhruv told the worker not to bring anyone nearer with them.

"Andar nahi," the voice on the phone said. "Team pahunch rahi hai. Jo location pata hai woh unko dijiye."

He went along the outside wall instead.

The hall had two rear windows, with iron grilles and a narrow strip of gravel beneath them. At the first he saw only the reflection of flame. At the second, the glass had cracked above desk height.

He called the child's name.

Nothing answered. From the front came shouting and the repeated whistle of a policeman trying to clear a lane for the fire engine.

Dhruv took a step away from the window, drew air that still carried smoke, and called again.

Something yellow moved below the nearest desk.

It could have been cloth settling in the draught. He did not tell the mother her child was safe.

"Doosri khidki!" he called to the worker. "Yahan kuch hila hai! Team ko yahin laao."

He put his good hand against the grille. The iron was warm. Behind the desk, something struck wood twice.

The girl's voice was too small to reach the yard as a word.

---

The fire engine stopped in the wider lane. Its crew carried a hose line through the pump passage with their breathing gear and tools. The municipal worker walked them past the open gate, then got out of their way.

Dhruv pointed out the window. Named the child. Described the yellow bag and the movement, without adding what he hoped they meant.

The firefighter nearest him looked through the lower pane, then told him to stand back.

Dhruv did not move far enough the first time. The man repeated himself more sharply.

He went to the kitchen wall. His fingers had closed round the pouch at his waist. The ring inside pressed against the padding through the cloth.

He released it.

There was no angle he could safely draw with a torn hand which would let him breathe another person's air for her. The men at the window had equipment for the danger actually there.

He told a helper to carry the girl's location back to the dispensary. Only the location. Not an invented rescue.

---

At the front gate the pale edge was gone.

Four more constables had reached the school in the police vehicle. Two held the broad man beside the wall while their senior ordered the watchers away from the fire route. The injured visitor stood apart with his good hand raised, identifying himself as a witness before anyone could make him part of another charge forward.

The flour driver helped the wounded constable sit in the vehicle. The miller's daughter pressed a clean cloth beside his eyebrow while he tried to give his radio call sign to a man who had already heard it.

The senior asked who had carried fire into the school.

Three men spoke at once. None gave an account which could yet be checked.

The broad man turned towards the blue cloth hanging across the entrance.

"Woh neeche—"

"Haath wahin," the constable beside him said.

A school worker pulled the cloth down to free the route. Its far end came off the shutter hook and dropped into the street.

Nobody stopped to fold it.

---

The first sound from the window was metal being forced away from brick.

Dhruv watched from the kitchen wall. A firefighter worked at the frame while another kept the hose ready. Smoke pressed out through the opening they made, hiding their hands and showing them again.

The man in breathing gear went through.

A piece of plaster fell inside. Someone at the maintenance gate started forward; the cook blocked him with an arm and pointed him towards the wider lane instead. Help was not measured by how many bodies could crowd the same window.

Dhruv called the girl's name once more. The firefighter outside raised one gloved hand: quiet.

He stopped.

There was the rasp of breathing gear, water striking something out of sight, a short exchange between the two men. Beyond the wall, the siren of a second emergency vehicle approached and cut off.

Then a small hand appeared below the broken frame.

The firefighter outside took the child while the man inside supported her through. Her yellow bag came with her because she would not release the strap. One shoe was missing. Soot had gathered above her lip; her eyes were shut, but her mouth opened to cough.

The worker stepped forward to carry her. The firefighter directed him clear and took her to the medical team now coming into the yard.

Dhruv went after them for three steps, then stopped to cough against his sleeve. When he straightened, a paramedic was looking at him as well.

"Aapne smoke liya?"

He nodded.

"Bahar. Saath aaiye."

"Bachchi—"

"Uske paas log hain. Aap bhi bahar."

He went.

---

The girl crossed the maintenance gate in a responder's arms.

At the dispensary the teacher heard the news from the municipal worker, then saw her coming along the passage. She kept the mother from running into the medical team's way and moved with her instead.

The child was breathing. She needed assessment and treatment, not a crowd pulling her from the man's arms to prove a happy ending.

Her brother stood close enough for her to hear him. He said he had her other shoe.

It was not true. He had seen the bare foot and said the first repair he could imagine.

The girl opened her eyes long enough to find him. Her cough broke whatever answer she tried to give.

The ambulance crew took mother and child to the vehicle in the wider lane. The brother stayed with the teacher after his mother asked her to keep him close. Police had cleared the hospital approach; another officer went ahead to keep it that way.

The teacher put the last mark beside the girl's name.

Eighteen residents found alive: sixteen remaining at the dispensary, the girl and her mother going to the hospital. The teacher wrote the destination beside both their names.

Then she asked about her staff.

The cook. His two helpers. The gate worker, now being examined for the cut under his chin. The people who had carried the chairs. Each answer had a face and a location.

The recorder-lady took the sheet while the teacher sat down on the dispensary step. Her hands had begun shaking too much to hold it flat.

No one asked her to make a statement yet.

---

At the school, water ran down the side corridor and spilled into the forecourt.

A firefighter called for the second supply line. The local hydrant's pressure had fallen; the engine's tank could not be the night's only water. Another tanker had been requested from the wider road.

The senior constable sent two men to keep its approach clear. He did not ask the fire crew to keep working while he settled the competing stories about the blue cloth.

The broad man was taken away for the assault and obstruction witnessed at the gate. The men who had come with him gave names, some willingly, others only after the constables separated them. The arson account remained what the recorder-lady had actually seen: a forearm, burning cloth, the screen. She would not turn an unclear face into an identification merely because there was an angry man ready to be blamed for everything.

His witnessed violence had enough witnesses of its own.

The injured visitor gave his account at the dispensary after his dressing was checked. When asked why he had been at the school, he handed over the corrected flour statement first.

"Yeh bhi lagaana," he said. "Pehle ki baat chhupaana nahi hai."

The constable read the page before taking the next account.

---

By the time the second tanker came, the hall windows were black all the way to their lintels.

The fire crew would not let anyone back in. The building would need inspection; even the rooms which had not burned had smoke and water through them. The flour classroom was closed with the rest. Some sacks might still be dry inside. Nobody could call them fit to eat from the street.

The old woman asked for her bundle.

The recorder-lady went down on her knees so she would not have to raise her voice.

"Abhi andar nahi jaa sakte. Aap bataao kya tha. Jo abhi chahiye uska intezaam karte hain."

The woman named clothes, a tin of tablets, a small steel box. Then she shook her head. She did not want a new box.

The older woman stayed there until the next answer came.

---

Dhruv was not allowed to walk back to the ghat.

The clinician at the dispensary listened to his chest, checked him again after the first treatment, and sent him on for further observation. The cough had not settled enough for a man to call himself useful and disappear into another watch.

His palm was checked too. Soot marked the outside of the dressing; the fresh padding underneath had stayed in place. The ring remained wrapped. It went to the hospital with him, not onto his hand.

The recorder-lady rode as far as the hospital to finish her arson statement and make sure the child's mother had somebody she knew from the school. A municipal worker stayed at the dispensary with the teacher and the other residents. Names and destinations went with the people, not in a pile left on the school table.

When Vedant met them at the receiving desk, he looked first at Dhruv's face.

"Saans?"

"Check kar rahe hain."

"Toh baitho."

Dhruv sat.

Vedant's own bruised arm was still resting in its support between reviews. He used the other to move a chair for the recorder-lady. Neither asked the other to prove he could stand.

---

The girl from the window was in treatment. Her mother had been told what the team was watching and when someone would speak to her again. Her son was safe with the teacher; a worker would bring him when the medical staff said it was sensible.

Dadi sent food to the mother through the desk. The woman accepted it, then forgot it beside her bag until a nurse reminded her.

Ram remained beyond another set of doors, with his drain and oxygen. Rukmini had been helped into a more comfortable position; her supported shoulder had not become a spare arm for every new arrival. Rudra's ankle was still raised when Vedant brought the news.

"Athaarah mil gaye. Bachchi yahan hai. Maa saath hai. School band rahega."

Rudra nodded at the first sentence. The rest reached him more slowly.

He pictured the children receiving the flour, the doors which had finally let it in. He had asked for the relief delivery to get there and had been glad when it did. There was nothing false about being glad. There was nothing in that gladness which made the later fire his to explain away.

"Unko raat kahan milegi?" he asked.

"Abhi dispensary. Agli jagah municipal waale dekh rahe hain. Teacher saath hai."

"Khaana?"

"Jo bahar nikal gaya tha woh hai. Doosri rasoi se aur maanga hai. School ka stock abhi use nahi hoga."

Rudra looked towards his mother. She was listening with her eyes closed. Dadi tucked the sheet round her uninjured side and went to ask about the next dressing time.

For once he did not make his mother open her eyes to tell him that staying in the bed was not abandonment.

He asked Vedant whether the hospital could keep the teacher's number with the patients' family contacts, where the next shift could find it too.

---

At the Trust, Aadhya heard *all eighteen* and began to reach for the phone.

Her mother lifted it before the bandaged fingers touched the table.

"Sun sakti ho. Pakadna nahi."

Sitara repeated the account on speaker, keeping it short. One child had needed a window rescue. The school was lost as shelter for the night. Both injured men had reached the dispensary with their damaged legs supported.

Ira asked after the recorder-lady.

"Hospital gayi hain. Statement dekar yahin aayengi."

She closed her uncovered eye again. The familiar voice would be late, not missing. That was enough to let the pillow take her head.

Aadhya's mother asked the caretaker what clean bedding the Trust could spare. They had no room for a whole school population, but there were things they could send. The caretaker counted them himself and arranged for collection. The medical car stayed at the guest-room side.

Aadhya said she knew where her family's spare sheets were kept. Her mother phoned a relative to bring them. They did not put the injured daughter in a car to prove the offer was sincere.

---

Veer heard through Sitara later.

He had spent the evening trying not to keep calling. His mother had set his phone within reach and told him that moving it away would not make him sleep. He could wait with it there.

Now he asked about the man with the stitches.

Sitara told him what the constable had confirmed: the visitor had corrected the theft claim, helped pull the officer free, and given his account. Earlier coercion remained in the record. No one had made him a hero in exchange for silence about the rest.

Veer said nothing for a while.

"Usko bolna kal mujhe dikhaane mat aaye," he said at last.

His mother's hand paused on the sling strap.

"Chot khul jaayegi ghoomta rahega," he added. "Jo kehna hai phone pe kahe. Aur yeh bhi—"

He stopped, found a plainer sentence.

"Main Rakshak hoon. Madad lene ke liye kisi ko mere Kul ka hone ki zaroorat nahi."

His mother settled the cloth under the strap. This time he did not ask whether she was tying it tight enough.

---

The teacher did not go home when the residents began leaving the dispensary for the next temporary rooms.

She went with the first vehicle to see the place herself, then came back for the others. It was a municipal community building with toilets and a side entrance wide enough for the chairs. There were fewer partitions than the families wanted. A worker was finding screens; for that night, the teacher asked people to keep the same neighbours beside them so nobody would wake without knowing a face.

The old woman took a borrowed blanket. She asked once more about the bundle and was told the school would be checked only when the fire crew allowed it.

The child's brother sat near the dispensary desk until his mother called. He put the phone to his ear with both hands.

He did not tell her that he had found the shoe again. He told her the teacher was there.

---

Outside the school, the last of the blue cloth lay where traffic had pressed it into wet grit.

A man who had carried a mat wanted it collected before someone photographed it beside the burnt doorway. The constable made him wait. The cloth, the damaged gate, the radios, the witnesses: none would be cleaned out of the account because a group disliked how its evening looked.

The fire crew's report would describe what had burned. The injured people would give their own accounts when they could. Private flour orders still needed repayment or delivery. The ruined donation needed replacing. None of those tasks became smaller because the child had come through the window alive.

At the dispensary, the visitor with the stitches offered to pay for part of the next food purchase from his own pocket. The teacher told him to make it to the common kitchen, with a receipt, and not through a guard at a road.

"Naam likhwaana ho toh?" one of the men beside him asked.

"Dene waale ki jagah hai," she said. "Lene waale ka Kul nahi poochhenge."

The visitor took the kitchen's contact. He did not get a photograph with the rescued families.

---

The recorder-lady reached the Trust after dark.

Aadhya's mother opened the guest-room door quietly. Ira had been awake, listening for footsteps and pretending not to. The older woman washed, changed the smoke-smelling shawl outside the room, and sat beside the cot before saying anything about the school.

Ira found her sleeve with the hand which shook least.

"Aapko kuch?"

"Check karwaaya. Ab baithungi."

"Achha."

The older woman waited. Ira's grip loosened, not because she had lost it, but because the sleeve was staying where she had found it.

Aadhya watched from the other cot. Her mother set water within reach, then remembered and held it for her instead. They were all tired enough to make small mistakes. They corrected them without turning each one into a lesson.

Sitara checked the next morning's medical transport with the driver. Eye review, hand dressings, the people still in hospital. The plan took account of the roads as they were at night and would have to be checked again before anyone left.

There was no victory procession to schedule between the appointments.

---

At the hospital, the nurse asked Rudra whether the quieter light was enough.

He said yes. His ankle remained supported, his wrist still bound. The news of eighteen living residents had not taken either injury away, and the closed critical-care door had not opened for him because other people had been rescued.

He listened while Vedant called the night-shift desk to confirm the receiving contacts. Then he let the phone be put away.

Ram's treatment continued. Rukmini slept. Dhruv had been kept for observation instead of being permitted to return to watch duty. Veer's mother had sent word that he too was finally resting.

For a few minutes, nobody they knew was travelling towards another danger.

---

The man from the ward pumping station arrived at the dispensary before the final vehicle left.

He wore his work card outside his shirt so the constable would see it before asking him to stop. The teacher recognized him from the pump passage. He had carried drinking water to the school before the trouble reached its gate.

Now he asked for the municipal supervisor.

The nurse called the supervisor's number and put the phone on the desk. The man described the station's two approaches. Rakshak-claiming guards held one. Prakriti-claiming guards held the other. Each had given him a different set of lanes to supply first, and told him not to open the other branch.

He had not been allowed into the switch room to inspect the valves.

"Aaj pressure isliye gaya?" the constable asked.

"Andar dekhe bina kaise boloon? Main abhi jo dekha wahi bol raha hoon."

The supervisor told him to remain at the dispensary while an escort was requested through the control room. The hospital's water requisition was already among the pending calls. Stored water would carry the night shift; the morning's pumping still had to happen.

The operator looked at the nurse filling a small jug from the tap behind her.

The stream thinned while he watched.

He put his work keys on the desk.

"Chaar baje start karna hai," he said. "Dono taraf bola hai doosron ke liye kholunga toh wapas nahi aane denge."

The nurse turned the tap farther. It coughed once, then stopped.

The operator looked from the keys to the constable.

"Mere saath kaun aayega?"

# CHAPTER SIXTY-FIVE — THE HOUR BEFORE WATER

---

The constable looked at the keys on the dispensary desk.

"Main aaunga," he said. "Par akele nahi. Control room se gaadi aa rahi hai. Municipal team bhi."

The operator did not pick them up.

"Mere ghar ka rasta bhi unhi ke paas se hai."

The nurse left the empty jug beneath the dead tap. The teacher had been holding the door for the final shelter vehicle. She let it settle against her shoulder and waited.

The constable asked who was at the operator's house. Wife, mother. A son working the night shift elsewhere. The operator had told his wife he was going to ask for help; she had wanted him to stay in the house until daylight.

"Unko phone kijiye," the constable said. "Kahan rehna hai, unse poochhenge. Aapke duty pe jaane ki sazaa unko nahi milni chahiye."

The operator looked at him for a moment, then took out his phone.

No one made the call on speaker.

---

The municipal supervisor arrived in a vehicle carrying two fitters, a torch and the station's duty assistant. The assistant had been turned away from the other approach. He had thought the operator was inside.

They stood facing one another in the dispensary light.

"Tum nahi pahunche?"

"Peechhe se gaya tha. Bola saamne se aa. Saamne waale bole peechhe jaa."

The supervisor stopped them before the conversation became an apology for having believed somebody in an armband.

He checked what each had actually seen. Who stood at which gate. Whether any municipal worker had entered the switch room. Whether a valve position had been checked, or merely reported by a man pointing through a fence.

Neither worker had inspected the equipment. Neither could certify the plant safe to start.

The supervisor called the control room again. He needed access for both men and the fitters, a protected working space, and a public distribution decision from his department. Not an escort which took them to the gate and left.

The nurse showed him the dispensary's remaining containers. Then she gave him the hospital's contact rather than guessing its reserve.

The teacher finally stepped outside to the waiting residents.

"Main saath aa rahi hoon," she said. "Bas ek phone."

She confirmed that the girl and her mother were still in treatment and that the brother would remain with her until his mother asked for him. The boy had curled up on a chair beside the old woman's borrowed blanket.

She woke him gently for the ride. He asked whether they were going back to school.

"Aaj nahi."

"Kal?"

"Kal dekhkar bataayenge."

He accepted the answer without liking it. She took his hand and went with the last vehicle.

The operator watched it leave. His own keys were still on the desk.

---

At the hospital, water became a task before it became a rumour.

The night supervisor checked the storage with maintenance staff. Non-urgent use was reduced. Drinking water and the clinical supply were handled separately; no relative was sent to dip a household bucket into a tank serving the wards.

Rudra woke when the nurse moved the wash basin.

"Kya hua?"

"Supply kam hai. Reserve hai. Abhi isi se chal raha hai."

He looked towards the critical-care passage.

"Udhar?"

"Unka intezaam alag se check hua hai. Aap rest kijiye."

She gave the answer she had, not a promise about the whole morning.

Vedant sat beyond the curtain, speaking quietly to the municipal supervisor. His bruised forearm lay on a folded towel. He passed on the hospital desk number and asked that any public notice distinguish an inspection from an actual restart.

"Switch tak pahunchna paani aana nahi hai," he said.

He finished the call before Rudra could ask him to go.

"Team jaa rahi hai. Engineer aur police."

"Aap?"

Vedant shifted the towel under the swollen place.

"Meri zaroorat yahan pade toh main yahan milunga."

Rudra listened to the movement in the next bay. Dhruv was still being observed after the smoke. His cough had interrupted sleep twice. Across another passage Ram's treatment continued behind the closed doors.

There was no uninjured version of the room waiting to stand up on command.

Rudra asked whether the school teacher had reached the next shelter. Vedant showed him the brief message confirming arrival, then turned the screen away when the nurse came to check the ankle.

---

At three-ten, the operator put the work keys into his pocket.

His wife and mother had chosen to spend the remaining night with his sister. A police vehicle had taken them by a checked approach; the address stayed with the duty officer, not with the men outside the pumping station.

The operator had spoken to his wife after they arrived.

Now he climbed into the escort vehicle beside the duty assistant. The supervisor and fitters followed in the municipal van.

At the turn towards the station, the first protection board caught the headlights.

A man stepped into the road and lifted his palm.

---

The escort stopped short of the man, leaving room for the municipal van behind it.

A sub-inspector got out. The operator recognized the guard's jacket; he had seen it beside the road two hours earlier. In daylight he might have recognized the face without the jacket too.

"Station kholna hai," the officer said. "Gaadi side karo."

"Kholne se pehle baat toh suno."

"Supervisor sunenge. Road pe nahi. Hathiyaar andar nahi."

Behind the guard, three men rose from a bench. One had a child asleep across his lap. Another moved a motorcycle out of the way. The guard with the raised palm stayed where he was until the officer walked towards him.

Then he stepped aside.

The vehicles crossed into the forecourt. Police cleared the strip between the station door and the open fence. The supervisor asked for the operator's account again in front of the duty assistant, so a warning given on the road could not later become an instruction nobody remembered giving.

The guard followed to the fence.

"Hamaare mohalle mein kal se nahi aaya. Udhar tank bhar rahe hain."

"Kaunsa tank?" the supervisor asked.

The man named a lane. The assistant knew it. The supervisor sent a fitter to check the corresponding readings with him once access was safe.

A real complaint went into the work list. It did not give the complainant the switch room.

---

The back approach had not emptied because the front one had opened.

Two women and four men stood beyond the service gate, under the station's broken outside light. One woman held the paper she had given the operator earlier. A Prakriti mark had been drawn at its top. Below were lanes to be supplied before the opposite side received anything.

The operator stayed behind the police line while the officer at the gate asked them to step back from the hinge.

"Wahan se aaye hain toh unka kholenge," the woman said, pointing towards the forecourt.

The supervisor came to where both approaches could hear him.

"Yeh municipal station hai. Pehle plant check hoga. Hospital aur public points ki supply restore hogi. Baaki pressure dekhkar. Kis ghar mein kaun paida hua, uska valve nahi laga hai."

A man behind the woman laughed once, without amusement.

"Kaagaz pe sabka hai. Paani unka jaata hai."

"Reading dekhenge. Shikayat likhwaaiye. Duty band karwaake reading bhi nahi milegi."

He asked each group to name one unarmed resident who could wait outside the working enclosure and hear the findings. They would not choose the valve positions. Other households could send their own witnesses when the notice went out.

The guard at the front objected to the woman being allowed to wait at all.

She objected to his jacket.

The supervisor looked at the police officer, not between their faces for permission.

The officer pointed out the line beyond which neither would come. A constable stayed at each entrance. The others went with the workers to the station door.

---

The operator put the first key into the lock and found his fingers shaking.

The duty assistant stood beside him. He did not take the keys away. He held the torch where it lit the lock rather than the operator's face.

Behind them the guard called, "Hamaare logon ko pehle nahi mila toh tum jawaab doge."

The operator left the key still for a moment.

He looked towards the supervisor.

"Yeh bhi statement mein lena. Naam se bola hai mujhe."

The sub-inspector asked the guard to repeat himself for the account.

The man pulled at his jacket's zip.

"Matlab duty ki zimmedaari—"

"Duty ki zimmedaari main loonga," the operator said. "Tumhaari pasand ki nahi."

The key turned.

Inside, the air smelled of warm metal, damp floor and a room shut longer than its shift allowed. The assistant checked the immediate working area before the fitters came through. No one reached straight for a switch to satisfy the men watching.

The supervisor told the operator to begin the inspection.

---

There were two faults, not one story.

A supply isolation which should have been open was partly shut. The department's previous shift sheet recorded it open; its seal had been disturbed. That established an unauthorized change since the check, not whose fingers had made it.

Farther along, a worn joint was leaking into the drain. The fitter showed the old staining below the fresh wet patch. It had not begun leaking because a protection group had arrived that night. It still needed repair before normal pressure could be restored.

The supervisor had both recorded. He did not let the seal become an excuse to ignore the joint, or the joint become an excuse to erase the interference.

He went back to the witnesses and explained the difference. The department would preserve the disturbed seal and logs for investigation. His crew would decide what could safely run now and what needed an isolated repair.

The woman at the service gate asked whether her lane would get water.

"Standpost pe pehle milega. Gharon mein abhi poora pressure nahi doonga. Test bhi hoga. Peene ka tanker alag aa raha hai."

The guard at the front leaned against the fence.

"Toh itna police leke bhi itna hi?"

The supervisor looked towards the men still kneeling beside the leak.

"Haan," he said. "Jitna sach mein ho sakta hai."

---

At three-forty-seven, a stone broke the station's side pane.

The fitter beside the wall jerked back with a cut along his cheek. Glass fell across the tool roll. His partner caught his sleeve before he could wipe his face with the dirty glove.

The operator took his hand away from the controls.

Outside, a constable caught the young man whose arm was still drawn back. Two people pointed towards him. A third tried to pull the constable away, shouting that nobody had been hurt badly enough for an arrest.

The supervisor went to the door.

"Kaam rok diya hai," he said. "Andar aadmi ko chot lagi hai. Jagah khaali karaaiye."

The officer moved the witnesses farther from the broken window. The young man was taken to the vehicle. His companions' complaints followed him; the officer asked for individual names and accounts, not a list of everyone wearing the same band.

The fitter's cut was covered by the first-aid attendant travelling with the escort. He would be taken for assessment. His partner checked the tools for glass and moved the roll clear. The damaged working area had to be made safe before anybody resumed.

Four o'clock kept approaching while nothing ran.

---

The operator's phone vibrated in his pocket.

His wife asked whether he had reached.

"Haan. Andar hoon."

"Chal gaya?"

"Abhi nahi."

She was quiet long enough for him to hear a chair scrape on her side. His mother was asking for the phone.

He could have told them the line was bad. Instead he said someone had thrown a stone, the police had taken him, and the workers were checking the room again.

"Main wapas aa sakta hoon," he said.

He looked towards the duty assistant as he said it. The assistant looked away, giving the conversation a little privacy inside a room with a broken pane.

His wife did not tell him to be brave.

"Abhi bahar nikloge toh akele mat nikalna," she said. "Aur duty khatam ho toh seedha yahan. Ghar pe subah bhi pehle phone karenge."

He promised that much.

When the call ended he asked the supervisor who would remain after the pump started. The threat did not end at a running motor. Somebody would have to stop the guards coming back to the door once the escort had its success to report.

The supervisor called the control room in his hearing. A relief police detail was assigned to the working site. The next municipal shift would arrive under escort too. Repairs and public supply times would be posted through the department, with a complaint number which did not belong to either group.

Nobody promised that every walk home had become safe.

The operator put his phone away.

---

By then, people with empty containers had gathered beyond the forecourt.

A few had come because a pump was being opened. Others had followed the police lights. The guard in the jacket told them to form a line beside his men, where their household names could be checked.

An older woman moved her bucket to the other side of the road.

"Tere paas naam likhwaaya toh udhar waale nahi jaane denge," she said.

"Toh udhar likhwaa lo."

"Mujhe paani bharna hai. Dono ki naukri nahi karni."

Two women joined her. Then a man carrying containers for neighbours who could not come out themselves. He asked the constable where the public queue was supposed to begin.

The officer marked a clear waiting space away from the vehicle entrance. He asked for two residents to help keep it clear for workers. The guard's bench remained behind the fence; nobody had to sit at it to receive a turn.

At the service gate, the woman with the priority paper watched the new line form.

"Hamaara bhi number?" she asked the supervisor.

"Public point pe. Baaki sabke saath."

She looked down at her paper. Folded it once. Put it in her bag.

It was not an apology. She stayed to hear whether the water actually came.

---

The crew finished the immediate safety checks. The leaking section would remain isolated for repair; a reduced service could run without pretending the whole network was ready.

The supervisor gave the instruction. The operator and assistant began the start under the remaining fitter's watch.

The motor's first sound was smaller than the voices outside it.

A tremor passed through the floor, then settled. Readings began to change. The fitter watched the joint they had isolated; the assistant checked the return report from the first downstream point.

The operator did not leave his position to announce anything.

At four-eleven, the supervisor received confirmation of flow at the public standpost. Flushing and checks were still underway. Drinking water was being brought separately; the first new water from a long-interrupted pipe was not a festival drink.

He called the hospital maintenance desk.

"Flow aa raha hai," he said. "Aap apni receiving check kijiye. Main yahan se confirm nahi karunga."

The guard at the front looked at the public queue, then at his unused household list.

For the first time that night, people were waiting for water rather than for his answer.

---

The hospital confirmed its incoming supply twenty minutes later.

Maintenance staff checked the receiving side and the storage rise before the nurse told the wards. It was reduced service. There would still be careful use, separate drinking-water arrangements and another check when the repair crew changed shift.

Rudra heard only the last part of the message at first. He had fallen asleep while waiting for the first.

"Aa gaya?" he asked.

The nurse nodded.

He looked towards his father's doors and then at the basin beside his bed. Neither had changed shape. The men who had gone to the pumping station had not entered either room, but something they had done outside now let the people inside continue their ordinary work.

He put his head back before the nurse had to ask.

---

At Rajghat, the recorder-lady woke to Sitara speaking to the driver.

The day's medical appointments were still needed. The roads had to be checked again. A working tap did not remove the men from every crossing, and yesterday's checked approach could not be carried in a pocket as today's guarantee.

Aadhya's mother helped her wash and change. The cup was held where she could drink without trying to pinch its rim. Ira sat beside the door with her uncovered side turned away from the courtyard glare.

"Aaj bhi wahi kapde?" Aadhya asked.

"Saaf hain. Dheele hain."

"Photo mein repeat ho jaayega."

Her mother looked up sharply, then saw the small movement at her mouth.

"Photo ke liye alag haath laa doon?"

Aadhya laughed and immediately regretted how much she had needed to.

Ira asked whether those spare hands could sign the hospital forms too. Sitara said she would ask for two pairs, since everyone in the room had discovered a sudden need to delegate.

They went to the vehicle slowly. The driver opened both doors before anyone had to stand waiting in the light.

---

The eye examination took place behind a closed door, not in a queue of people wanting to see whether Ira had brought her sight back from the ghat.

The doctor checked the injured eye and replaced the covering as directed. Ordinary rest had not made second sight safe. Her uncovered eye still tired quickly, and the tremor remained when she reached for the examination chair's arm.

She was given the next review and the same specific changes which meant returning sooner. The recorder-lady repeated the plan back. Ira corrected the time of one appointment herself, then closed the eye which had done enough ordinary looking for that morning.

Aadhya's dressings were changed nearby. The doctor asked her to distinguish what she could feel from what she knew must be happening because she saw his hand.

She failed to feel the light contact at the same fingers.

He wrote that down without asking her to try harder.

Her mother watched him protect the skin again. The nails would not grow back to an audience's schedule. Permission to rest outside observation remained permission to rest.

Neither girl was sent to the pumping station to stand beside a working tap for a photograph.

---

Dadi met them with breakfast after the reviews.

She had found a space where chairs could stay without obstructing the passage. Veer arrived for his check with his mother and was told the chairs were not a public invitation to reopen his commentary.

"Main toh chup tha," he said.

"Isliye pehle bataa diya," Dadi replied.

Rudra could hear them from the bay. For a moment the sound was not a report of another road closing. Veer's mother loosened the fold which pressed beneath the sling and gave him food he could manage with his good hand. Aadhya asked for the bowl to be steadied. Ira listened without making herself turn towards each speaker.

Nobody pretended they were back at a training breakfast.

Dhruv was brought past for reassessment. He lifted his good hand in greeting, then stopped to cough before the smile had settled. The nurse went with him. Dadi kept his portion covered rather than calling him back to eat it before it cooled.

The fitter from the pumping station sat farther down the waiting area with a dressed cheek. The cut had been assessed and treated; he had been told when it needed checking again. His partner had sent word that the temporary repair was holding.

A volunteer recognized his municipal shirt and asked whether he was the man who had opened the water.

"Aur log bhi the," he said.

Dadi asked if he had eaten.

He said he would go home first.

She passed him a wrapped portion for the journey. It was an offer, not another job he had to finish before being allowed to leave.

---

Later, Rudra was helped to the chair near his mother's bay.

Rukmini's shoulder stayed supported. She had slept through the first water update. Now she watched him arrange the injured ankle on the support before he looked at her.

"Raat bhar jaage?"

"Beech mein so gaya."

"Achha hua."

He had been preparing to apologize for that part. She turned her good hand palm-up beside the sheet, and he rested his uninjured fingers against it instead.

Dadi had put a small bottle within her reach, the cap loosened so she did not need both hands. Rukmini asked Rudra to tighten it a little before the bottle was moved; she did not want it spilling on the bed.

He turned it until it held, then stopped.

For a while they discussed nothing larger than where the bottle should stand.

By noon, three different stories were being told about the water.

At the tea stall, four young people had marched to the station and made the guards kneel. Outside the pharmacy, a woman had turned the pipes with a tree root. In the actual duty room, a tired operator was handing over reduced readings, an open repair and the names of the men relieving him.

The sub-inspector recorded the threat against him as well as the stone thrown through the pane. A police detail remained at the station. The two private household lists had not become distribution orders.

At one public point, neighbours from opposing lanes stood in the same queue because there was only one working outlet and both needed it. At another, men still argued over whose containers had arrived first. The drinking-water tanker needed a constable at its stop. Nobody who worked that shift mistook running water for peace.

The operator went to his sister's house in the escort vehicle after handover.

His wife opened the door before he reached it. She asked whether he had eaten. He asked whether his mother had slept.

Only after those answers did he take the work card from round his neck.

---

At the school, inspection tape replaced the blue cloth across the entrance.

The teacher was permitted to stand outside while the responsible officers checked the rooms. She was not told to bring the families back because the flames were out. Damaged bedding and food would need assessment; the clean community rooms remained the night's shelter.

The old woman's steel box was still unaccounted for. The teacher recorded what she had described without promising it would be found intact.

The kitchen put in another request for flour. The miller's daughter said her father would help find sellers, but he could not donate eighteen more sacks every time armed men ruined a load. The teacher thanked her for the first eighteen instead of making his limit sound like a refusal of the city.

The two private buyers were given the contact for the continuing warehouse inquiry. They still had claims against a seller, not against a relief meal. The loader's employer was asked about wages during recovery. His sister did not have to withdraw the assault account before anybody would discuss help.

The work of the previous day had not disappeared beneath the new emergency.

---

The first call from outside Varanasi came through the common kitchen.

A relief organizer in Prayagraj wanted to know whether the road-control groups had dispersed or merely moved after losing the grain crossing. Their own vehicles were being divided into permitted and forbidden loads, and a neutral receiving post was under pressure to let one group choose who could work there.

The cook did not tell him that the same men must be responsible. He gave him the municipal contact handling the local obstructions.

An hour later, a Mirzapur cooperative reached the recorder-lady through a records volunteer she knew. Families lived beside the weaving sheds. Men claiming hereditary protection were demanding levies and declarations of belonging before letting the common loading court remain open.

The older woman asked for the speaker's name, the cooperative's registered contact and which authorities had already been approached. She rang back through a separate contact before passing the request on.

The two accounts did not establish one commander. They established two more places where people were being told that public work and private ancestry were the same thing.

The recorder-lady brought them to Vedant after the girls were resting again.

---

He did not answer either city with four names.

First he called the receiving posts, then the officials working with them. Local volunteers were already protecting deliveries, documenting threats, moving families when necessary. They were not sitting without help until a group of injured strangers could arrive from Varanasi.

What they lacked, the organizers said, were people who could speak across the lines without claiming the place for their own. The ghat survivors' refusal to be taken away by separate Kul escorts had travelled farther than any accurate account of that morning's water duty.

"Woh sab patient hain," Vedant said.

"Aaj nahi maang rahe," the Prayagraj organizer replied. "Jab aa sakein. Lekin baat abhi se karni hai. Yahan ke log roz yahin rahenge."

Vedant asked what work could be done with protected travel and no combat role. He asked about medical contacts, safe rooms, actual access, who would meet an arriving vehicle. Nobody could supply all the answers on the first call.

He told them to begin with those, not a banner welcoming champions.

---

Rudra heard the second conversation when Vedant came back to the bay.

The speaker was a woman from the Mirzapur cooperative. Machines clattered behind her, making her repeat some words. She said they were trying to keep the loom workers' complaint separate from a demand to declare war on a whole Kul.

"Hamaare ghar mein bhi sab alag hain," she said. "Kaun kis darwaaze se andar jaaye?"

Vedant asked when the next local meeting would be held. She gave a time and said the authorities had been informed; the cooperative would send the notice through the verified contact.

Rudra looked towards his mother's bay. She was speaking quietly to Dadi. His father remained in treatment. His friends had returned to rest after the reviews. Every road out of this room began with something which could not simply be left behind.

Then the woman on the phone asked whether the four young people who had refused to be separated could ever work in separate places.

"Dono shehron mein zaroorat hai," she said. "Aap logon mein se kaun aa sakega?"

# CHAPTER SIXTY-SIX — TWO ROADS OUT

Eighteen full days passed after the water returned before a doctor let Rudra put part of his weight on the right foot.

He expected the first step to feel like freedom.

It felt like finding the exact place pain had been waiting.

The physiotherapist steadied the support while he transferred the amount of weight she allowed. His wrist stayed protected; she had adjusted the aid so he was not asked to hang his whole body from the hand he had injured at the winch.

"Bas itna," she said.

He tried the second movement too quickly. His ankle answered before she did.

"Race nahi hai. Wapas chair pe."

He sat, angry with the foot for a moment, then with the idea that it owed him anything because eighteen days had passed.

The doctor checked the new examination against the earlier findings. The cuts had closed. Swelling had reduced enough for controlled loading, not running or a sudden pivot. Travel seated, with support and stops, was possible. A field fight was not part of that permission.

Rudra repeated the difference back.

Only then did the doctor sign the travel note.

---

The eighteen days had not belonged to him alone.

At the pumping station the worn joint had been repaired under the department's supervision. The disturbed seal remained part of the inquiry. Public supply improved, then failed in another lane when a damaged connection gave way. People learned which notices came from the department and which had acquired a private mark at the bottom.

The burnt school did not reopen for a coat of paint. Families stayed in the temporary rooms while the building was assessed. The girl from the window left hospital only after the team treating her permitted it; follow-up remained with her mother. Her brother stopped claiming he had the missing shoe.

The flour seller still owed two private orders. The loader had begun another stage of treatment; arguments over lost wages had not ended. His sister no longer had to answer a theft story every time she brought him food.

In Prayagraj, local volunteers kept the receiving post open through two attempts to impose a private roster. In Mirzapur, the cooperative went ahead with its complaint and meetings. Shipments went out late. Some came back. Nobody there had paused living until Varanasi's wounded could travel.

Vedant spoke to both places every few days. Requests became lists of actual work, rooms, contacts and risks. He refused invitations which meant standing under a faction's banner to make its protection respectable.

By the time medical travel was discussed seriously, the two cities knew who was coming and what the visitors could not do.

---

Ram was no longer in critical care.

The change had come after examinations and repeat imaging, not after a son asked often enough. The chest drain had been removed when his team was satisfied it could come out; the site was still dressed. He remained an inpatient. Oxygen was still needed at times, especially when the work of breathing tired him. Speech could run ahead of strength if nobody stopped it.

Rudra found him in the step-down ward that morning, sitting higher than before with the nurse's help.

Rukmini had come from the nearby ground-floor room where the family had arranged her rest. Her own discharge had followed a shoulder and neck review, with movement instructions and further appointments. The sling was still used for support on an outing. Dadi helped her settle without pulling the injured arm.

It was the first morning on which Rudra could see both parents without a critical-care door between them.

That did not make it an easy morning to ask about leaving.

---

Ram saw the travel paper on his lap.

"Doctor ne kya bola?"

Rudra gave the restrictions, not just the signature.

"Aur tum kya karne jaa rahe ho?"

"Relief post. Logon se milna, route ki baat sunna. Vedant saath rahenge. Wahan doctor ko papers bheje hain."

"Ladne?"

"Permission nahi hai."

"Maine permission nahi poochhi. Iraada poochha."

Rudra looked at the foot on its support.

He had imagined arriving in another city without anybody remembering how he fell after the machine broke. There would be people who knew only the outline of light, the thing which had stood when he could not. For a few days he had wanted that version of himself to go first and spare the rest of him the journey.

"Agar laga zaroorat hai toh khud ko zyada samajh loonga," he said. "Isliye akela nahi jaa raha."

Ram rested his hand against the blanket. The fingers had more strength than on the night Rudra could not make them close. They still did not look like the hand which had lifted iron at the ghat.

"Yeh baat unko bhi bolna. Sirf mujhe nahi."

Rudra nodded.

The nurse came to check the breathing support. Ram waited until she finished before asking for the bag beside the chair. Rukmini reached the bag for him and took out the ordinary phone charger he had asked her to bring.

"Tumhara taar phir toot raha tha," Ram said.

Rudra looked at the coiled lead in his mother's good hand.

"Naya liya?"

"Phone karna hai toh charge bhi chahiye."

Rukmini put it in the outer pocket where he could find it without standing.

---

At the Trust, the route map lay on a table low enough for nobody to have to stand over it.

Aadhya's nail beds had closed slowly during the eighteen days. New growth at the edges was still fragile. The right thumb and index finger remained numb. She could manage a light object with her left hand, sometimes, but could not safely judge a grip by wanting it to be steady.

Her doctor had agreed to supported travel with dressing supplies, scheduled review and no force through the injured fingers. The Mirzapur clinic had the instructions. A local clinician had accepted the handover and knew when the original team wanted her brought back.

Ira had a separate plan. The left eye still needed its covering and further examination; the right could do short periods of ordinary work with rests. The ophthalmology appointment in Mirzapur was booked, not a name someone hoped to find on arrival. No second sight or Darpan was part of that clearance.

Veer brought his travel instructions in a clear sleeve.

His shoulder still needed protection; the sling was to be used for the journey and prescribed movements continued afterward. The elbow wound had closed. Knee pain returned if he stood too long, and a deep laugh could still remind him of the ribs.

"Saare kaagaz mere hain," he said as Sitara sorted the medical sleeve from the route sleeve. "Abhi tak kisi ne mere ticket ka nahi poochha."

"Seat tumhari bhi hai," she replied. "Kaagazon ke upar nahi bithaayenge."

He nodded gravely, as though an important negotiation had concluded.

---

Vedant's forearm had settled enough for ordinary work after reassessment. He still avoided testing it against an unnecessary load. Dhruv's chest examination had improved after treatment and rest; the smoke cough had subsided. His palm was closed but tender. The ring remained wrapped until he could use it without opening the skin again.

Dhruv was staying in Varanasi.

The hospital knew his contact. Dadi would remain with the family. Rukmini had transport for her appointments; Ram's ward could reach Rudra through more than one number. The local volunteers and police detail had their own shifts, not a promise that one mentor would stand at every gate.

Sitara would travel with Aadhya and Ira to Mirzapur. Vedant would take Rudra and Veer to Prayagraj.

The pairings had been worked out with the receiving posts and the four people who would make the journeys. Ira could help the cooperative keep a usable complaint without forcing frightened families to publish private details. Aadhya had asked to work where protection claims were being made in her lineage's name. Neither reason made them the owners of the workers' decisions.

At the Prayagraj post, Veer's refusal to turn shared aid into a Rakshak entitlement had been quoted back to the volunteers. They wanted him to say it where his admirers could hear. Rudra would listen to the deliveries and blocked routes with Vedant, not arrive as the man who could break every obstruction.

The two mentors named the limits plainly. No solo inspections. No following a threat into another neighbourhood. Local authorities would handle arrests and armed confrontations. A changed approach meant a call and a pause, not a challenge to prove the travel papers wrong.

Each destination had a ground-floor rest space and a vehicle for medical return. The boys' next review in Prayagraj had been arranged through the hospital. The route would be checked before each movement, not blessed once in Varanasi and considered safe thereafter.

The four listened. Each had a place to say no before anyone put a bag in a vehicle.

None did.

---

While Vedant checked a call, Rudra reached for the water cup too quickly.

Pain caught at the ankle when he shifted. Gold showed briefly at his collar, beside the closed edge of the old neck wound. He stopped moving and let the tension fall without trying to bring the rest of the outline up.

Sitara waited until he was comfortable, then held the small mirror where he could see.

The broken line had not shortened with the healing skin.

He had expected it to, without admitting the expectation. Scabs were gone. Bruises had faded. The damage in that other outline remained where the battle had left it.

"Zakhm bharna aur yeh judna ek baat nahi," he said.

"Ab itna farq tum khud dekh sakte ho," Sitara replied.

She did not offer a name for what lay beneath it. He did not ask her to find one by making it hurt again.

The nine-breath memory was still there, along with the other count which had crossed it. A single sustained form at the ghat had not settled their disagreement. Eighteen days of sleep had not done it for him either.

He let her lower the mirror.

---

Aadhya's mother put two small cloth-wrapped bowls into the Mirzapur bag.

"Wahan bartan nahi honge?" Aadhya asked.

"Honge. Inka size tumhe pata hai."

Ira recognized the practical mercy before the argument began: one less object to misjudge, one less edge a numb finger would have to discover.

Aadhya moved her good hand away from the bag's knot.

"Main aapko phone karungi."

"Khaana khaake."

"Pehle bhi kar sakti hoon."

Her mother tucked the loose cloth inside so it would not catch in a door.

"Pehle bhi kar sakti ho."

---

The recorder-lady brought Ira a plain notebook with wide lines.

"Ismein toh aadha page do vaakya mein bhar jaayega," Ira said.

"Toh do vaakya likhna. Baaki kisi aur se likhwaana."

"Aap nahi hongi wahan."

The older woman kept her hand on the cover until Ira had found its edge.

"Isi liye toh khaali diya hai. Kisi aur ki writing ke liye jagah rahe."

Ira nodded, looking at the ruled space with her uncovered eye. She put it away before the print on the label began to swim. The book did not have to be filled before it counted as work.

The recorder-lady would stay in Varanasi, helping the ongoing complaints and keeping contact with the cooperative through the established volunteer. She was not being replaced because a younger woman could travel. Ira was not leaving her behind as a stage she had outgrown.

At the car, Ira took the offered arm and let herself be guided down the shallow step. The older woman named the edge before she reached it. Sitara took over at the open door.

For a moment Ira kept hold of both sleeves.

Then she let go of one.

---

Rudra had said goodbye to his parents before coming to the Trust.

Ram had not tried to sit without help to make the departure easier. The nurse adjusted the pillows; he let her. Rukmini stayed beside the bed while Dadi went with Rudra to the lift.

Before he left, his mother had drawn the edge of his cuff smooth with her good fingers. They paused on the unbandaged skin above the wrist support, where she could feel the small living beat without pressing an injury.

She said nothing about the gold at his collar.

He did not promise her a body which could never be hurt again.

"Phone lagaao toh pehle jagah bolna," she said at last. "Aawaaz toot gayi toh bhi pata rahe."

He repeated the address of the Prayagraj receiving post.

She corrected the pronunciation of the lane from the paper, making him say it once more. Then she let the cuff go.

---

The Mirzapur car left first.

Aadhya's mother stood clear while Sitara checked the seat belt lay away from the injured hands. A soft support kept them from knocking against the door. Ira sat on the shaded side with her covering in place; she could rest without being asked to navigate.

The driver repeated the receiving contact and the planned stop. There would be a call before entering any changed approach. A local volunteer was meeting them outside the cooperative district, not expecting the car to find its way through a crowd.

Aadhya watched her mother through the open door.

"Aap khud time pe khaana khaana."

"Haan."

"Sirf phone pe haan mat bolna."

Her mother looked towards Sitara.

"Ab yeh mujhe bol rahi hai."

Sitara waited until the door was shut before smiling. The car moved away slowly enough for no hand to need a sudden grip.

From the next vehicle Veer lifted his good hand. Aadhya saw it and nodded rather than trying to wave with the one she could not feel properly.

Ira kept her eye closed. She knew who would be standing outside without making herself look until they disappeared.

---

The boys' van had room for the leg support and a seat which did not force Veer's knee into a tight bend.

Vedant checked the medical sleeve stayed accessible. The rest of the load was relief material and ordinary bags, tied so nothing could slide forward when the van braked. The driver had already agreed to stops; an extra half-hour on a safe journey was not to be treated as failure.

Dadi put food into the space left for it.

"Itna kaun khaayega?" Veer asked.

"Tum dono. Driver. Vedant."

"Prayagraj mein bhi log khila denge."

"Toh khaali dabbe wapas aa jaayenge. Main unko bhi ginoongi."

He looked at Rudra.

"Duty double ho gayi."

Rudra smiled before he could decide whether a departure required a solemn face. Dadi noticed and did not rescue him from it.

Dhruv stood beside her with the hometown contact list. His hand no longer needed the thick dressing, but the skin had not been toughened by anybody's impatience. He took the van's number, gave the driver his own and confirmed who would answer at night if the first phone failed.

"Ghar ki khabar main bhejoonga," he told Rudra. "Har aadhe ghante check mat karna. Koi badlaav hoga toh call aayega."

Rudra nodded. He knew waiting for a call would not make him calm. It gave the waiting somewhere to go besides another unanswered ring to the ward.

---

At the first police check, the driver showed the route information and medical travel papers when asked. They were not a pass to order everyone else aside. Another vehicle had arrived before them, carrying a man returning for treatment, and it went through first.

Veer settled his knee rather than complaining.

The city receded in small ordinary pieces: a repair crew at a trench, a row of opened shutters, a protection board still leaning beside a wall where nobody had yet dared remove it.

Rudra kept his foot where the physiotherapist had placed it.

He had left Varanasi. He had not left the part of himself which needed help to stand.

---

They stopped twice before Prayagraj.

At the first stop Rudra wanted to remain seated rather than admit the ankle had begun to throb. Vedant asked the driver for the planned break anyway. Nobody had to make a confession before the support could be checked.

Veer came down slowly with his good hand on the offered arm. He did not help Rudra out by pulling with the shoulder he was supposed to protect. The driver and Vedant handled the transfer while he kept the clear space clear.

A man at the roadside stall recognized the boys.

"Ghat waale?"

Veer nodded.

The man looked at the sling, then at Rudra's supported foot.

"Abhi bhi?"

"Haan," Rudra said.

He had expected to hate saying it. The word did not change when he said it without an apology.

The man brought water to the table instead of asking for a demonstration. When the van left, he moved a chair out of the path so the driver could take the wider turn.

At the second stop no one recognized them. Their needs were the same.

---

Prayagraj arrived in a lengthening evening, wider roads narrowing into lanes busy with return traffic and handcarts.

The receiving volunteer met the van at the agreed point. He checked with the post before taking them down the final approach. A group had gathered near another entrance that afternoon; they had moved on, but nobody sent an arriving vehicle there merely because it had been the printed route.

The post occupied the ground floor of a former lodging house. A public relief board stood beside the entrance. Inside, volunteers worked at a long table, matching incoming supplies to requests which could be checked. A pot of tea had gone cold beside three unanswered calls.

The organizer who had spoken to Vedant came out to meet them.

He offered Rudra a hand, saw the support, and changed the greeting into a nod without making the adjustment everybody's business. Veer sat before the knee began shaking.

"Kal subah baat karenge," the organizer said. "Aaj room dikha deta hoon."

Vedant asked to hear any immediate safety change first.

The pressure was still outside the post, the man said. Two regular drivers had been told their loads would not pass unless the workers' roster was approved by private guards. One had refused and returned. The other had called the police and waited for an escort. Local households had kept the post supplied while the route was checked.

"Hum aapko yahan ka incharge banaane nahi laaye," he told the boys. "Jo chal raha hai, usko saath khade hoke chalne dena hai."

Rudra looked at the people who were still making calls behind him.

He had come to defend a place which already had defenders. There was room in that fact for relief as well as shame.

---

The rest room was accessible from the same courtyard, with a separate wash area and enough space to turn the chair.

The receiving clinician checked the travel notes and examined the things the journey had aggravated. Rudra's ankle needed elevation and rest. Veer's knee had stiffened; no one sent him immediately to stand at a public meeting. Their appointments for the next review were confirmed before the clinician left.

Vedant called Varanasi.

Rudra gave the place first, as his mother had asked. Then the room, the journey, the ordinary details which let her picture where the body behind the voice had gone.

Dadi asked about the boxes of food.

"Abhi tak sambhaale hain," Veer answered from the bed.

"Khaaye bhi?"

He looked at the closed packet beside him.

"Abhi khaata hoon."

Rukmini could be heard telling Dadi not to keep them talking until everything cooled. The call ended without a warning from critical care hidden behind it.

Rudra ate, let the ankle stay raised, and asked for no briefing beyond the one already given.

Outside, the receiving post kept working.

---

The Mirzapur car had reached its volunteer earlier, before the last of the light left the loading roofs.

From the safe approach, Aadhya could hear looms before she could see the cooperative. Their repeated clack came between houses where dyed thread hung above the street. Finished cloth did not look like a battlefield resource until a man explained that whoever controlled the common court could stop every piece leaving it.

The receiving woman worked at the cooperative. She had spent the eighteen days keeping the complaint moving while the shifts continued. There were families in the residential strip who wanted the guards removed and others who feared losing the only men who said they would protect them.

"Aapko bhi sab ek jaise nahi milenge," she told Sitara.

"Humein sabse ek jaisa jawaab nahi chahiye," Sitara said. "Pehle ladkiyon ko rest. Phir jo tay hua hai."

Their room was beside the community clinic, one lane outside the working compound. The clinician on duty checked that the planned eye appointment and hand review were in place, and that neither girl had arrived with a new complaint being concealed as travel fatigue.

The car remained outside, ready to return to care if it was needed.

For the first hour in the new city, they unpacked bowls, medicines and a notebook. Nobody hung a flag above their door.

---

The cooperative's public inspection was set for the following morning.

A department representative was coming to hear the complaint with the local officers already handling the threats. The workers had asked for witnesses who would not turn the meeting into another Kul rally. That was the short, seated duty the girls had agreed to attend, with rest before and after, not a night patrol through the sheds.

Sitara went over the plan with the receiving woman at the clinic door. Ira listened for a few minutes, then asked that the rest be told to Sitara while she rested her eye. Aadhya let the clinician look once more at the hand which had knocked lightly against the seat belt during the last turn.

The skin was intact. The numbness had made the knock feel less important than it was.

Her mother's bowl sat beside the bed, its familiar rim within the reach she knew.

---

When the four finally spoke to one another, they were in two different rooms in two different cities.

Veer asked whether Mirzapur had accepted the delegation's medical papers or was still looking for somewhere to seat them.

"Tumko chair mil gayi?" Ira asked.

"Bed. Senior delegation."

"Knee pe pillow rakha?"

There was a small silence.

Sitara heard Vedant say something on the other end. Cloth moved. Veer announced that the infrastructure problem had been resolved.

Aadhya laughed, then settled her hands again.

Rudra asked about the journey rather than the assignment. She told him about the thread drying above the lane and the way the machines could be heard before the roofs appeared.

"Yahan post ke bahar ek bada courtyard hai," he said. "Kal dekhunga. Abhi pair upar hai."

She said good. Neither made the other's rest sound like a command which they alone had invented.

Vedant reminded them that a local emergency went first to the people already there. The other pair could listen and help make calls, not arrive between one breath and the next. Distance had to change their habits before an emergency forced it to.

They agreed on the next check-in. A missed call would go to the receiving contact, then the mentor, not become proof of catastrophe after one unanswered ring.

When the call ended, Ira said the room sounded different without Veer in it.

"Shaant?" Aadhya asked.

"Bohot. Shak ho raha hai."

They smiled without needing to keep the call open to prove they were still together.

The receiving woman came back after dinner with a fresh notice.

It had been pushed under the cooperative office door, not issued by the department coming in the morning. The men demanding protection payments wanted each working household to place a thumbprint beside a hereditary affiliation and accept the levy before the inspection began.

Families who refused were to lose access to the common loading court. Finished cloth would be held against the payment. The notice called that *voluntary protection*.

Aadhya looked at the page from where she sat. Ira asked the woman to read the wording aloud; she did not lean into the small print.

"Jis ghar mein alag-alag hain?" she asked.

"Ek naam chunwaayenge. Nahi chuna toh bahar waale."

"Aur kiraye ka, mazdoori ka jo haq hai?"

"Woh keh rahe hum dilwaayenge. Pehle inka maan lo."

Sitara asked whether the local officer had the notice. A copy had been sent. The cooperative was keeping the original and contacting the inspection team as well. Several families had refused already; others were afraid to be the first household without a mark on its door.

"Koi aaj nikal raha hai?" Sitara asked.

"Do parivaar bachchon ko rishtedaar ke paas bhej rahe. Kaam chhodenge toh paisa nahi milega. Rukenge toh sign."

She set the notice down.

Ira asked her to keep names out of the general phone message. Specific threats could go to the officer with the affected person's consent. The families did not need to become a public list for whichever men arrived at the gate first.

It was one question, not an evening of work. She stopped when the eye began to ache.

At Prayagraj, Rudra was almost asleep when Vedant returned from the post table.

"Mirzapur se notice aaya hai," he said. "Sitara ne local officer se baat kar li. Subah ka plan police confirm karegi."

Rudra's hand moved towards the phone, then stopped. He asked whether either girl had been asked to leave the clinic.

"Nahi. Dono rest mein hain."

"Theek. Koi badlaav ho toh—"

"Pata chalega."

Rudra let the phone stay where it was. Beside him Veer's breathing had finally steadied into sleep.

In Mirzapur, the loom noise went on beyond the clinic's closed window.

The receiving woman's phone rang as she was leaving. She listened, went still, and came back into the light.

At the cooperative's front, men were unloading a chain and a set of padlocks. A goods vehicle had been parked across part of the loading court. The night workers had been told their cloth would not leave without the signed household list.

The woman called the police contact again. Sitara stayed beside her.

From the phone came a second voice, close to the caller.

"Subah inspection karwa lena," the man said. "Aaj raat gate hamaara hai."

# CHAPTER SIXTY-SEVEN — A DOOR WIDE ENOUGH

By morning, the chain was in police custody. The marks it had made were still on the gate.

The officers had come during the night. They had made the goods vehicle clear the access, stopped the new padlocks being fitted and stayed until the night workers could leave. The complaint about the levy remained open. Nobody had given either claimant a title to the court because he arrived with a chain.

The local officer confirmed the morning's arrangements before Sitara agreed to leave the clinic. The public inspection would go ahead. Two constables would be present while the department representative heard the workers. The girls would sit in the welfare office, take part briefly and return to rest.

Aadhya had slept badly. Ira had slept with the notebook still unopened beside her bag. Neither was sent out to finish the argument the police had interrupted overnight.

At the cooperative, the receiving woman showed them where the chain had scraped the paint.

"Nikaal diya, phir bhi log sign karke aaye hain," she said.

The first working household to arrive had brought a thumbprinted acceptance of the protection levy. The man who carried it asked whether he could take the acceptance back.

His wife stood beside him, holding their rent receipts.

"Raat ko bachche andar the," she said. "Tab haan bola."

Ira asked the representative to keep those words with the paper. A thumbprint did not describe the room in which a frightened person had given it.

The man lowered the document onto the table as carefully as if it might break.

---

The loading gate opened from the street into a rectangular court.

Along the left side stood the long weaving shed. Finished cloth was kept in a separate store at the back. The welfare office ran along the right wall, ending short of the store. A low ramp faced the court. Its rear door opened beside the store into a service path which continued along the back wall to the residential strip.

The receiving woman took Sitara along that path before the meeting began; Ira listened while the way out was described, then walked the short distance with help rather than trying to memorize a place she had never seen.

Against the office's rear wall stood a folding timber drying frame, hung with lengths brought in from the open court before the night's threat. A woman moved it nearer the wall to widen the service path. Beside its foot, a creeper grew from a strip of soil where drain water had kept the ground damp. Its lower stem had been tied loosely to the frame to keep it off the walking line.

Aadhya noticed the knot because the stem had room to thicken inside it.

The woman who tended it put her watering tin down and went back to the meeting.

---

They were given chairs near the office's rear door, out of the central crush of people coming to speak.

Sitara stayed beside them. The receiving woman kept the front entrance clear. In the weaving shed, the morning shift was finishing work which could not be abandoned without losing another day's pay. The machine operator knew the inspection was underway; representatives came from the floor in turns.

The department representative began with the tenancy and work arrangements. Who paid rent. Who received wages. Who had authority to collect which charge. None of those questions required a family to choose a hereditary side.

The men demanding the levy arrived after the first two workers had spoken.

One wore a narrow pale band at his wrist. Another had a green thread tied above his elbow. They came together, bringing a ruled payment book and an empty handcart.

"Humse bhi poochh lo," the man with the book said. "Raat ko ek taraf ki sunke gaadi hata di."

The officer told him he could give his account, not move goods while the dispute was heard.

He sat on the front bench without being invited nearer the girls. Put the book on his knee. When a worker asked about the compulsory affiliation, he turned the page as though a different charge might be easier to defend.

---

Ira listened until the ordinary effort began to pull behind her uncovered eye.

She asked the receiving woman to read back one sentence from the account she was taking: the workers had asked for access to their homes and work, not for one protection group to replace another.

The woman read it. Two workers agreed. A third asked to have his family's specific threat given privately, not spoken in the room.

Ira nodded and let the notebook stay with the woman. The wide lines had space for another hand, exactly as the recorder-lady had intended.

Beside her, Aadhya kept both hands supported. She could hear the looms through the open front door and the representative asking for proof of a levy which the men had brought only themselves to authorize.

The man with the payment book shut it.

"Proof store mein hai," he said. "Maal ka bhugtaan nahi hua. Hum uthaa lete hain, baat baad mein kar lena."

Outside, the handcart's wheels began to move.

---

The constable at the office door moved before the handcart reached the store.

"Roko. Abhi kuch nahi niklega."

"Hifaazat ka paisa baaki hai," the collector said. "Maal yahin toh hai."

The department representative closed his file.

"Toh aap levy ke badle kaam rok rahe hain. Koi maal khareedne ki delivery nahi lene aaye."

The collector stood up.

One of the men at the cart shoved it forward anyway.

A worker caught the front rail. Another reached for a bale which had begun sliding. The constable got between them, palm out, telling everyone to let go. His radio crackled while he asked for the waiting support vehicle to come to the entrance.

Behind him, the collector said the inspection could continue after the store was emptied.

The representative told him to remain where he was.

The man with the pale wrist band stepped across the office's front ramp. A curved edge brightened along his forearm.

People near the door recoiled. The bench scraped backwards. A woman who had come to give her account stumbled against the table and lost her grip on the rent receipts.

Sitara rose beside the girls.

"Peechhe se chalenge," she said. "Saath."

---

The loom operator heard the warning from the court and stopped the machines.

Their clatter broke into separate dying movements. A worker at the shed door kept people from reaching into equipment which had not yet stopped. He sent them along the open side of the court, away from the cart.

No one had to keep working for a photograph of order.

The second constable moved towards the bright forearm. The officer at the table ordered the man to lower it, then stepped between him and the residents trying to leave. The curve caught the officer's lathi with a sharp hard sound.

The man did not lower it.

At the back, the cart lurched when the worker trying to protect the cloth released the rail. A loose bale fell sideways. Its wrapping split. Lengths dragged across the ground, taking dust and dirty water into the weave.

The driver who had brought the empty cart shouted for room to pull it straight.

Another guard told him to keep loading.

---

Ira saw the pale curve through the office doorway.

It was a live source. She recognized the low gap at the wrist before she could stop thinking about it. She had once placed such a reflection where a machine could break itself against it.

Pain stirred behind the eye covering. The visible curve was not a whole wall, and her injured sight would not become a good receiving angle because people needed one.

She turned towards Sitara's voice instead.

"Rear door kitna?"

"Do kadam. Phir chhota edge. Mera haath lo."

Ira took it. Aadhya rose with the receiving woman's help, keeping the numb fingers away from the chair's arm. They moved towards the exit they had walked before the meeting.

The woman who had dropped the receipts came with them. She bent once for a page; Ira asked her to leave it until people had room. The department representative had heard her account. The page did not have to be rescued ahead of her.

At the front, the officer's radio called the location again, louder than before.

---

The collector reached for the representative's file.

The representative pulled it back. A cooperative worker stood beside him, holding the table steady instead of running after the men with the cloth.

"Naam chahiye toh police ko apna do," the worker said.

The collector struck the table with his palm. The officer caught his wrist and took him away from the residents. He kept shouting to the men outside, but his words no longer controlled where the file went.

The bright-edged man backed towards the court. The constable followed at an angle, not putting another resident between the curve and his lathi. From the street came the sound of the support vehicle stopping.

At the store, the cart driver tried to turn away from the arriving officers.

The court had space. The people beside the office did not.

The cart's rear corner struck the splayed foot of the timber drying frame.

---

At the service path, the receiving woman had just stepped out ahead of Aadhya.

Sitara was guiding Ira over the low rear threshold. Two residents were behind them. The narrow path would take people in single file, not three shoulders abreast.

Aadhya heard the timber scrape. She turned towards it.

The frame's upper rail leaned away from the wall. The stem loosely tied to its foot drew tight. Cloth slid along the rail, bringing the weight towards the door where people were coming out.

"Frame!" the receiving woman shouted.

Sitara pulled Ira close to the wall. A resident behind her stopped. The woman at the front tried to move back into the court and found the cart across her way.

The frame kept tipping.

Aadhya could not take its weight in the hands which had only just closed over their wounds. The right thumb gave no warning even when she knocked it against the doorpost.

She dropped to one knee beside the strip of soil.

Her left palm heel touched the damp ground below the creeper. The thin stem was already straining against its knot.

There was no time to ask it to become a wall.

---

Aadhya did not try to lift the frame.

She reached along the living stem into the roots already holding its small patch of earth. The nearest timber foot was touching the loose end of that growth.

Hold here.

The thin roots gathered. One curled across the foot nearest the office door and pulled tight.

The nearer leg stopped. The far one kept sliding towards the court under the cart's shove.

The frame turned.

Its upper rail scraped along the wall instead of falling straight across the doorway. Cloth dragged from it in a long dark fold. Sitara moved the residents through the gap while the receiving woman pulled Ira clear of the crosspiece.

"Cart peechhe!" the woman shouted.

The driver finally obeyed the people nearest him. A worker caught the far rail, grunted under it, and called for another hand. Two more came from the shed side.

For one breath Aadhya felt the roots take the strain without knowing where the next demand would end.

For the second, pain split through a nail bed on her left hand.

She did not give it a third because the first two had worked.

"Pakad liya?" she asked.

"Haan! Chhod!"

She let go.

The roots slackened. A strip of bark peeled from the creeper's lower stem. The workers lowered the frame onto the now-clear edge of the court, letting the cloth slide away rather than trying to save its clean folds with people beneath it.

One timber joint broke as it landed.

The sound was small after all that waiting for the whole frame to strike.

Aadhya lifted her hand from the soil and saw blood at the edge of the new nail.

---

Sitara knelt beside her as soon as Ira was with the receiving woman.

She did not praise the work before looking at the hand.

"Aur kahin laga?"

"Right hand door pe. Pata nahi kitna."

The numb fingers had not acquired sensation because the left hand hurt. Sitara checked where the knock had landed without squeezing them to see whether Aadhya could bear it.

"Clinic. Abhi."

Aadhya looked towards the frame. The woman who tended the creeper was crouching beside its stripped stem. A worker helped her loosen the original cloth tie so it would not bite into the damaged part.

"Bach jaayega?" Aadhya asked.

The woman shook her head a little.

"Dekhenge. Pehle apna haath."

Sitara helped her up. No one asked for a second root to repair the first.

---

In the court, the extra officers had reached the cart.

The man with the pale curve lowered it when he found an officer behind him and the residents already out of reach. The constable in front kept his lathi raised until the forearm was dark again. Then the man was moved away from the workers, hands in view.

The collector was being taken to the police vehicle. His payment book stayed with the officer who had heard him call the levy a right to seize cloth.

The department representative asked the machine operator whether the shed was clear and the equipment stopped. He checked with the people from each work section before anybody was allowed to resume moving about the machines.

The driver said he had been hired only to carry goods.

The cooperative worker who had caught the rail showed him the split wrapping, the broken timber and the office door from which the residents had come.

"Aaj yeh bhi bolna statement mein," he said. "Kya uthaa rahe the, woh bhi."

The driver stayed.

---

At the clinic, Aadhya was put back in the chair she had left that morning.

The clinician cleaned and covered the reopened nail bed, examined the right-hand knock and checked the existing numbness against the handover. Further assessment was arranged for the injury which could not be judged by pain alone. She would not return to the cooperative that day.

The next forty-eight hours of rest and reassessment were not optional because the intervention had helped.

Ira sat on the other bed with her uncovered eye shut. She had used no second sight. Ordinary glare, fear and sustained attention had still brought the ache back. Sitara called the eye contact about the symptoms rather than deciding that an injury must be harmless if no power had caused it.

The receiving woman stayed until the clinician said what help was needed. She would take the account back to the inspection team herself.

Aadhya asked whether everyone had come out of the office.

"Sab jinhe humne nikalte dekha, court mein hain," the woman said. "Officer alag-alag section check kar rahe. Puri khabar aayegi toh bataaoongi."

Ira nodded without opening her eye. An incomplete answer was still better than giving a frightened person a certainty which would have to be taken away later.

Aadhya looked at the new white dressing.

She had held a timber foot for two breaths. Her hands would be paying for it after the people in the doorway had gone home.

---

The account which came back from the court contained losses as well as arrests.

Two workers needed examination after the shove and the falling rail. A constable's wrist had been struck against the cart. Cloth was torn and stained; the wrapping could not be changed to make it clean again. The stopped shift would have to be paid for somehow, and the cooperative could not recover an order's deadline by pointing at a police complaint.

No missing participant had been reported after the work sections and residential passage were checked. The machinery would remain stopped until the operator had checked it properly. Those were the limits of the first update, and Sitara passed them on without adding a triumph.

The inspection continued after the immediate danger was cleared. No one was asked to sign an affiliation to get back to a rented room. Questions of rent, work and legitimate dues would be handled through the appropriate process; the compulsory protection levy had supplied no authority for that morning's seizure.

A police detail stayed while the cooperative arranged its own access watch. The people who had keys before the men arrived kept them. They did not hand the keys to the visitors from Varanasi instead.

---

The man who had brought the thumbprinted acceptance came to the clinic door with his wife.

The representative had kept their account of the threat with the paper and recorded that they disputed the supposed consent. Their names would go to the officers handling it, not onto a public list of families to be admired or punished.

"Humein bola tha maan gaye toh apne ho," the woman said.

Aadhya looked at her from the chair.

"Ab?"

"Ab keh rahe inki baat mein aa gaye."

She meant the visiting girls, the inspector, anyone who had made the household's first frightened answer reversible.

The husband turned his rent receipts over in his hands.

"Ghar se nikalna nahi chaahte. Kisi ka dushman bhi nahi banna."

"Wahi toh aapki baat likhi hai," Ira said, her eye still closed. "Dono baat ek saath sach reh sakti hain."

The woman sat for a minute without asking what either girl had been born as. Her husband went to tell the relative keeping their children that the family could come back to its own room that evening.

The fear would go back with them. So would the account they had finally been allowed to give.

---

Sitara asked Aadhya to describe the intervention for the clinician.

Not the legend version. The turn, the knock, the pressure through the left palm, the two breaths, where the first pain had appeared. She did not let gratitude replace a history the doctor needed.

Aadhya gave it. Her right fingers still could not tell her how hard they had struck the post. The left nail bed was the new wound; the rest of the slow healing had not vanished, but neither could it protect that thin fresh skin from strain.

She had wanted, for a moment, to call the work small.

The dressing made the word inadequate. Small had meant a timber foot rather than a wall. It had not meant free.

The clinician arranged the further hand check and told Sitara what changes required urgent return. Ira's symptoms were passed to the eye clinician, with her actual work and rest described. She was to keep the planned review and seek earlier assessment if the specified warning changes came. Neither girl was promised that being brave had shortened the recovery.

Aadhya asked after the creeper once more.

The woman who tended it had supported the damaged stem and stopped anyone pulling the broken frame across its roots. It might survive. There would be no attempt to force new growth to prove that it would.

Aadhya nodded and let the nurse place her hands where they could rest.

---

In Prayagraj, the boys' first morning had been quieter.

They sat with the receiving-post volunteers for a short meeting, then stopped when the agreed time ended. Veer listened to a driver explain how one private guard had tried to turn a worker's surname into a transport permit. Rudra asked which part he had seen himself and which had come through a caller.

The driver drew the approaches on a scrap of packaging.

A temporary foot crossing over a river channel was still used by families coming to the post from the far side. Supplies went around by road when the crossing was unsuitable. The volunteers checked with the workers managing it before directing anyone there. Rival claimants had begun demanding that the two banks keep separate queues.

The post had notified the local officer. Two volunteers were at the near landing, helping people who had already set out. The boys were not being asked to inspect it on foot.

Vedant took the route information to the official contact while Rudra and Veer returned to the rest room. The post would need an actual safe arrival route, not another paper certifying the wish for one.

The Mirzapur update reached them there.

Rudra waited until Vedant had spoken to Sitara before asking to hear Aadhya himself. The first message had already confirmed she was at the clinic and under care. Fear did not have to invent an empty interval while the actual facts were being gathered.

---

Aadhya called her mother before the cooperative could send a grateful version home.

She gave the clinic's name first. Then the hand, the treatment, what the clinician was checking next. She said the right fingers were still numb and the new bleeding had been on the left.

Her mother asked whether Sitara was there.

"Haan."

"Unko do. Phir tumse baat karti hoon."

Aadhya handed the phone over with help. She could hear the questions, not all the answers. Her mother wanted to know whether she should come, whether the return vehicle was still available, who would call if the hand changed during the night.

Nobody told her not to worry because everybody was alive.

When Aadhya spoke again, her mother asked whether she had eaten.

"Abhi nahi."

"Pehle khaa lo. Main yahin hoon."

There was no punishment hidden after it. Aadhya let the bowl be placed where she could use it without finding out how much the new dressing would bear.

---

Ira's call to the recorder-lady was shorter.

She described the injured workers, the file staying with the representative, the families not being forced to choose one hereditary mark. The older woman asked whether Ira had used her sight.

"Nahi. Bas dekha zyada."

"Doctor ko bola?"

"Haan. Sitara ne bhi."

The recorder-lady waited until Ira stopped trying to make the answer sound lighter than it was.

"Notebook?" she asked.

"Doosri writing aa gayi."

"Achha."

Ira could hear the small pleased breath after the word. Someone else had taken an account in the room where she could not keep reading. The work had not ended at the edge of her injured eye.

She put the phone down before the older woman had to tell her to rest.

---

The receiving woman returned with the office keys and the plain notebook.

The keys were going back to the cooperative caretaker. The book had notes in two hands now; one worker had dictated his own correction when a sentence made his household sound more willing than it had been.

Ira asked that the notes stay available to the people who had given them, not disappear into the visitors' bags. Copies for the complaint would go through the representative with the contributors' agreement.

The woman said she would arrange that. Then she asked who would sit with them at the next meeting if the girls had to stay at the clinic.

Sitara named the local people already doing the work and offered to attend only after care was covered. The inspection did not require Aadhya's blood to remain valid. It did not require Ira to read through pain so the accounts could be believed.

"Aap dono nahi aaye toh bhi hum bolenge," the woman told them.

Aadhya looked up from the bowl.

"Wahi toh chahiye."

The woman took the keys to their owner. The loading court was open when she went back, with a constable at its entrance and workers arguing over the order in which the damaged cloth should be assessed.

The argument belonged to their work again.

---

In Prayagraj, the call between the two pairs lasted only a few minutes.

Rudra asked after Aadhya's new wound. She told him what she had told her mother, without reducing it to a scratch. Veer listened to Ira describe the live curve she had not tried to borrow.

"Achha kiya," he said.

"Source ki burai kar rahe ho?"

"Source bhi galat aadmi ke haath mein tha."

She smiled with the uncovered eye closed. The joke did not need a demonstration to finish it.

They ended the call so the girls could rest. Sitara stayed clinic-side until the care arrangements were settled; nobody in Mirzapur was waiting for the boys to cross the distance and make the room safe.

Vedant went back to the receiving table. Rudra kept the supported foot up. Veer adjusted the cushion beneath his knee before anyone asked.

Outside, volunteers were setting aside space for families expected from the river channel.

---

Late in the afternoon, the near landing called again.

The receiving-post volunteer there called the official contact, then the post. A group had blocked the approach and begun turning people into separate household queues. Families already on the temporary crossing were trying to go back rather than choose a side at its end.

At the opposite approach, a section joining the walkway to the bank had come loose under the crowd. The volunteer could see the gap. He could not yet say who had moved what before it failed.

The police and water-rescue team were being called. The near landing had to be cleared for them, not packed with more spectators going to see whether the report was true.

Vedant asked the post to keep its arriving families off that route. The driver reached for the official contact number instead of starting the van on a guess.

Rudra could hear the river through the phone now, beneath too many voices speaking at once.

The volunteer was shouting for people to stand still on the walkway. Someone nearer the far side was calling for a mother not to lift her child above the rail.

Then a woman's voice reached the receiver clearly.

"Is taraf se wapas nahi aa sakte. Udhar jaane nahi de rahe."

Veer sat forward and stopped when the knee caught.

The woman asked again, louder, as though the man holding the phone might be able to give her a piece of bank.

"Hum utrein kahan?"

The woman was still asking where to step when the volunteer began repeating the rescue team's instruction across the water.

Stay on the walkway. Stop pushing towards the broken join. The boats were coming.

At the receiving post, Vedant asked the driver to put the official contact on the other phone. The two calls gave him different pieces of the same place: a visible gap at the far bank, a crowd at the near one, families turning between them because neither end offered a way off.

"Jo abhi udhar nahi pahunche, unko mat bhejna," he told the organizer. "Alternate road pe bhi pehle police se check."

The organizer went to stop the next group before it left the courtyard.

Veer had swung his good leg off the bed. The other stayed on its cushion until he saw Vedant look at it.

"Hum?"

"Yahin. Receiving mein zaroorat padegi. Clinician ko bula liya hai."

Rudra had not stood. His phone lay in his hand, the old fear of being left behind already making his wrist tense.

"Kitne log?"

"Abhi pakka nahi. Naam se poochho jab yahan aayen. Phone pe ginti banaake mat bhejna."

Vedant took his bag and went with the driver. A volunteer stayed with the boys while another cleared the post's shaded passage for people coming from the landing. The clinician was bringing supplies from the nearby room where the travel examinations had been done.

Nothing in the departure sounded like permission for a field fight. Veer put his knee back where it belonged and asked what could be moved without lifting.

The volunteer gave him the telephone.

---

The landing was two turns and a sloping lane from the post.

By the time the van reached the upper stopping place, an officer was clearing the lower stretch for the rescue vehicle. People coming to watch were sent back uphill. The van stayed above the steps so it would not take the space needed for a boat's passengers.

Vedant went down on foot. The driver carried the bag.

The temporary crossing ran across a narrow river channel towards a low opposite bank. Its walking boards rested on linked floats; handrails and bank lines held it in a shape which moved even when nobody walked. Near the far shore, the joining section had dropped away from its seat. One end lay slanting in the water.

At the near shore, people were still arguing about who could come off.

A length of rope had been put across the approach. Beside it, a man held a household list against a board.

"Yahan se udhar waale nahi—"

The officer took the rope off its post.

"Abhi sab utarenge. Peechhe."

The man caught the rope's loose end. A second constable took his elbow and moved him out of the approach. Neither the list nor the argument was allowed to remain between the rescue crew and the water.

The path was open.

The crossing was not safe to use merely because the man had let go.

---

The crowd on the floats began to move when it saw the rope fall.

Too many at once.

A rail dipped. Someone shouted that the bank was open and people behind pressed harder, not yet seeing how the joined boards twisted beneath those in front.

The rescue lead called through a loudhailer. A worker already on the crossing repeated him, spreading his arms to stop the next rush. Another worker made the people nearest the damaged join sit where they were instead of testing the loose section with a foot.

The near joining boards had slewed sideways under the change of weight. The crew checked them and refused to send a fresh crowd across the narrow contact that remained.

Boats would take the people off the steadier middle sections. Nobody was to walk out and prove the crossing strong with their own body.

Vedant listened to the boatmen describe the approach. The channel's current struck the upstream floats and came round underneath them. A boat needed to be held clear while people stepped down; it could not simply be tied to the rail and trusted to stay level.

He asked where a spare pair of hands would help.

The boatman looked at the forearm which Vedant still moved carefully.

"Uthaana mat. Baithe rehna. Jo bolo woh pehle bataana."

Vedant nodded. A crew member gave him a life jacket and checked its fastening before he boarded. The crew chose the route and the load. He would not be steering a boat by imagining a calmer river.

---

From the bank, the woman who had asked where to step looked smaller than she had sounded through the phone.

She stood beyond the crowd's centre, one arm round a child, the other holding the rail. An older man sat near her feet, holding out folded money towards the worker beside him.

The worker shook his head and pointed to the rescue jacket, then waited until the man looked at the boat.

Vedant could not hear the words over the motor.

He could see how long it took the older man to put the money away.

---

The boatman brought the bow beside the steadier section, not beneath the broken join.

A rescuer stepped onto the walkway in his safety gear. He spoke to the woman first, then to the older man. The crew fitted jackets and chose who would come down together. Nobody was told to jump for a hand reaching from a moving boat.

The older man showed the folded money again.

"Kisne bola paisa lagega?" the rescuer asked.

He pointed towards the near bank. The same bank which had finally let a rope be removed.

"Yeh rescue hai," the man told him. "Pehle aaiye."

The woman asked whether her father could go in the same boat. The crew made room for the three of them together. Her child would not be passed out over the rail to a stranger while she waited to be chosen later.

Vedant kept the landing space clear inside the boat. He left the lifting to the men with the position and strength for it.

The first movement went well. The child settled where the rescuer put him, angry at the jacket under his chin. His mother followed, talking quietly until he stopped reaching for the buckle.

Her father rose last.

---

The floats moved under a shift farther along the crowd.

A worker shouted. The boatman corrected. For a moment the boat and walkway were no longer moving together.

The older man's knee gave a little as he reached the edge. He caught the rescuer's shoulder. The rescuer took the weight, but his free foot had not found the boat's floor.

Water struck between the hull and the nearest float.

Vedant lowered his good hand towards the wash beside him.

Not the current out in the channel. The short tongue of water lifting between two hard surfaces, where another blow would close the gap against the man's leg.

He turned its face along the hull.

Water flattened, struck the float sideways and ran past. The boatman had time for one correction. The rescuer got his foot down and brought the older man onto the seat.

Vedant let the shape fall at once.

The next wash came normally. The crew had already moved clear.

His reassessed forearm had tightened with the effort of bracing himself. He kept it against his body instead of offering another hand to steady the man who was already seated.

"Bas?" the boatman asked.

"Bas."

The man looked at the water, then back at Vedant's face.

"Agli baar pehle bolna."

Vedant nodded. It was not a rebuke he had earned the right to ignore because the intervention had helped.

---

At the near landing, the woman refused to let her father step off first and vanish among the hands waiting for him.

The rescuer told the receiving volunteer where to keep the family together. Two people helped the older man up the lower steps. His daughter carried the child after the jacket was removed by the crew.

The man with the household list had been moved uphill beside an officer. He called to her as she passed.

"Naam likhwaake jaana. Baad mein ghar ka mat bolna."

She stopped for half a step.

The volunteer beside her said the receiving post was open and the officer would take any threat there as well. She did not have to go back to the list-holder.

She walked on. Her father had already turned his head at the familiar voice and needed reminding which way the path went.

Vedant stayed at the landing for the next arrivals. His arm would not help another boat as much as an accurate receiving report would.

The crew went back without him.

---

Across the water, people began believing the boats would return.

That belief made the worker's job easier and harder. Some sat where they were asked. Others stood too soon when a bow came near, trying not to be the last family visible against the darkening bank.

The rescue lead changed the pickup point when a float began riding badly under the weight. The crew did not argue with the water to preserve the first plan. They kept the crowd away from that section and worked from the next stable part.

One woman had a cut from the rail. A man needed help after the crush had left him unable to put weight through one foot. Medical staff at the landing were told before either arrived at the upper lane.

The empty section twisted farther once its people were off. A rail dropped into the channel and struck a float with a hollow clang.

Nobody ran back to catch it.

The crossing would not be reopened when the last boat left. Officials were already closing both approaches to new traffic while the landing remained open for rescue.

Vedant sent that distinction to the post. A closed crossing was not an instruction to turn away the people who had just escaped it.

One breath.

Another rhythm struck across it before he could settle the next.

The woman closed her hand over the key before her son could take it.

Behind the gate were their medicines, their clothes and the bed she had promised her son they would reach tonight.

Rudra woke anyway. He called from the room.

Vedant went to the door and gave him the actual report. Veer had opened his eyes beside him, his good hand already searching for the phone.

"Hum kya karein?" Rudra asked.

"Abhi rest. Nayi baat aayegi toh bataayenge. Jo calls ho rahe hain, unka ek hi account rahe."

The volunteer stayed in the doorway while Vedant returned to the table. Nobody needed the boys to invent a task to prove they had heard the danger.

---

The bus stand had not finished its day.

A late service waited in the passenger bay, its baggage door open while the conductor argued with a man trying to send an unaccompanied bundle. Beyond it, the warehouse service lane joined the stand's delivery approach. That approach narrowed before reaching the main-road exit.

The duty officer spoke to the dispatcher. Passengers were kept off the service strip. The late bus stayed in its allotted bay; it was not driven across a moving vehicle's path to make a trap.

The first police unit reached the exit from the main road.

A closed passenger van came down the delivery approach with its inside light on. The driver sounded his horn at the people already being moved out of the way.

From inside, the woman's relative saw a bus destination board through the window.

He read the first word aloud.

The hall worker passed it on. The dispatcher could see the same bus standing between his office and the van.

The officer at the exit raised a hand and signalled the driver into the clear stopping space beside the stand office.

---

The driver began to point towards the road.

The officer signalled again.

The van slowed. A man in the front passenger seat leaned across the driver and shouted that they were going on watch, not taking passengers. They should be allowed through.

The officer walked close enough to see the men packed behind them. One was half standing because another had been pushed onto the end of the seat. Work dust marked their shirts. There were no bags for a night away.

"Side mein lagao. Engine band."

The relative heard it through the van window and held the phone closer to his chest.

At the hall, the woman closed her eyes for the length of one breath. She did not end the call.

The front passenger put his hand on the side-door latch before the driver had finished stopping.

---

The side door slid open while the wheels were still turning.

A man near it put his foot down too soon. The relative caught the back of his shirt and pulled him onto the seat as the driver braked. A shoe scraped the paving outside. The man made a short, frightened sound and stayed inside.

"Engine band!" the officer repeated.

This time the driver took the key out.

The front passenger twisted round towards the men behind him.

"Baithe raho. Kaam ka matter hai."

The officer asked the men whether they had agreed to go on night watch.

Nobody answered at first.

The relative still held the phone against his chest. Through it, very faintly, he could hear the hall worker telling somebody that the vehicle had stopped.

He lifted his head.

"Unloading ka kaam liya tha. Khatam ho gaya."

The man in front reached back for his collar.

The officer caught the reaching arm before it got there. His partner came round the open door and told the crew to stay seated until they could step down safely.

The front passenger tried to pull free. He struck the door pillar with the heel of his hand, then pointed towards the men as though their presence were evidence of his authority over them.

"Apne log hain. Main leke aaya hoon."

"Abhi haath neeche," the officer said.

---

The conductor from the waiting bus had come to the edge of the clear strip.

The dispatcher sent him back to his own passengers. A woman with two bags had begun walking towards the open van, assuming the service had changed its loading place. He took her to the bus and kept the other travellers behind the bay marking.

The late service would leave late. The people inside were not asked to decide whether a frightened loading crew deserved those lost minutes.

At the van, the front passenger was taken aside. He continued talking over the officer's questions until the officer asked them somewhere the crew could no longer hear every answer challenged.

Only then did the first worker say he wanted to go home.

Then another did.

Two men said they had accepted a paid watch shift. They wanted to know whether they were being detained for taking it.

The officer spoke to them apart from the others. They could choose paid work. Their choice did not consent for everybody in the van, and they would not have to drag an unwilling co-worker along to keep their own arrangement.

The driver remained by the bonnet while his details were checked. The vehicle would not depart with the crew still inside merely because he had an appointment farther down the road.

---

When the men began stepping out, the worker who had nearly put his foot under the moving van could not make himself reach the ground.

His hands stayed fixed round the seat edge.

The officer gave him time. A bus-stand employee brought a low step from the baggage office. The relative came down first, then stood aside so two people in a better position could help the man.

On the paving, the worker sat at once. His chest hurt. The officer called for medical help instead of giving the pain a reassuring name because the vehicle had stopped.

The tea seller brought a chair into the lit space. He left it with the employee rather than pressing a cup into a hand which could not yet hold it.

The relative finally took the phone from his shirt.

"Utar gaya," he said.

At the hall, the woman sat down on the floor beside her father's mat.

"Sab?"

He looked back at the van, then at the men who had already been moved away from its door.

"Jo gaadi mein the. Godown ka police dekh rahi hai."

She let him say only that much. He did not owe her a complete rescue because he was the first person she could hear outside it.

---

The warehouse unit found the loading floor lit.

A supervisor brought out the shift book and began pointing to thumbprints. The officer asked for the workers first. The book could wait long enough to establish where the people it claimed to describe actually were.

Some had left before the watch demand began. One man had stayed by the rear wash place, vomiting after the work. A co-worker had refused to board without him and been told to wait inside until somebody from his household came.

The unit brought both into the open loading area and called for medical support. Their names and the van crew's accounts were compared privately, not shouted across the bus stand.

The employer's representative said there was no missing worker. Everybody had been at work, and work instructions had changed.

The officer asked who had given the instruction that men could not go home without a replacement from their families.

The supervisor looked down at the shift book.

Inside, below the unloading entries, a fresh heading had been drawn for the night watch.

Some thumbprints sat above it. Some sat below.

---

By the time the book reached the lit table, the representative had stopped calling every thumbprint the same agreement.

One worker said he had marked the page when he arrived for unloading. Another had done it after being told the meal was included in a longer shift. A third could not read the heading and asked the officer to say it aloud without the supervisor standing over him.

The officer recorded the circumstances separately. The order of marks on a page was not a recording of everything said beside it.

"Pehle se watch pe kaun aaya tha?" he asked.

There were people who had. Their wages, hours and willingness would be checked too. Nobody needed to pretend voluntary guards did not exist in order to establish that the others had tried to leave.

The supervisor fetched a shawl for the sick man and put it round his shoulders with unexpected care. Then he asked whether the crew could at least finish arranging the empty pallets before going.

"Abhi nahi," the officer said.

He kept a worker's hand off the first pallet as well. A man could agree too quickly when the person owing his wages was still beside the door.

---

The warehouse representative brought out the remaining loading money.

It was not enough to meet every amount the men said they had been promised. He wanted them to take it and put a thumbprint against full settlement.

The officer told him not to combine payment for completed work with the account of the night's incident. The wage dispute would need the proper worker-support contact and the actual entries. Leaving the premises would not be made conditional on signing either version as complete.

A worker touched the coins in his palm.

"Mazdoori li hai," he said. "Aadmi girvi nahi diya."

He took only what he was told was payment already due and asked for that amount to be written down. The unfinished claim stayed unfinished. His anger did not become a miraculous full wage in the officer's notebook.

Outside, the medical vehicle took the sick man. At the bus stand, the man with chest pain was being transferred for further examination. Their co-workers chose whom to contact at home. The hall was told about its own relative, not handed everybody's medical account as a reward for making the first call.

The van's driver and the man who had reached for the relative were questioned over their own acts and what they had been told. The warehouse's proposed night patrol would not leave with unwilling men while the inquiry went on.

---

The head count was finished through names and separate accounts, not the number of shoes visible beneath the van.

The warehouse unit confirmed the men who had stayed behind. The loading contact helped trace those who had left before the watch demand. Where a worker had gone straight home, somebody actually spoke to him before putting that movement down as confirmed.

A few calls took longer. A wife had no phone and had to be reached through a neighbour she knew. One man had gone with a cousin and could not remember the number of the vehicle. The officer kept those checks open until there was an answer.

Only after the crew's whereabouts were reconciled did the hall receive the limited all-clear: nobody from that loading shift was still being held in the warehouse or carried away in the stopped van. The men sent for examination had medical destinations. The two who had accepted paid watch work had been spoken to separately and could decide again without the van full of other men around them.

The rest would need their own safe way home.

---

The woman's relative returned to the hall with an officer's contact and a small treatment note.

He had bruised the upper arm where somebody had pushed him towards the van. The clinician had checked the strain from catching the other worker. He could rest there tonight. If the pain changed or movement worsened, the receiving worker knew which service to call.

He held out the note before sitting down, as though he had brought back a receipt for the time she had spent waiting.

She put it with his things.

Her father looked at his empty hands.

"Kanghi?"

The relative stared at him. Then he remembered the small errand and rubbed his face with the hand which did not hurt.

"Bhool gaya."

The old man made a tired, annoyed sound. The woman began to laugh and stopped almost at once, afraid it would hurt somebody. The hall attendant found a spare comb and brought it over. Her relative looked at it and laughed once too.

Nothing about it made the warehouse harmless. For a moment it merely made this a room in which an old man could be irritated about his hair.

---

At the post, Vedant passed on the confirmed return and ended the boys' questions before they became another watch lasting until dawn.

Rudra asked whether the worker-support contact had somebody to meet the men in the morning.

The organizer had made that call. The officer had the wage pages and accounts needed for the next step. The post would help people reach an appointment if they asked; it would not become the only place entitled to speak for them.

Veer let his phone be put beyond his good hand.

The late bus finally left the stand. Behind it, an employee swept the grit from the stopping space and put the borrowed step back in the baggage office.

---

In Mirzapur, Aadhya's reassessment began with an ordinary question.

Could she hold the small bowl without being told when her grip was changing?

With the left hand supported, for a short while, she could. When the clinician asked her to set it down, she did not argue for a longer trial. The recently reopened nail bed still needed protection. The right thumb and index finger could not reliably report the bowl's edge.

The minimum rest period was over. The injury was not.

The clinician allowed carefully limited light use of the supported left hand and brief seated activity with breaks. No force through the fingers, no lifting, no return to work which required catching something before she could judge its weight. The next review would decide what changed after that.

"Cooperative?" she asked.

"Aaj yahaan ka short session dekhte hain. Phir decide. Seedha kaam pe nahi."

She put the bowl where the helper could reach it.

Outside, the woman who had brought the food waited with a worker's pay slip. She had asked before bringing it in. A clinic appointment was not an open court for every dispute in the compound.

---

The worker came in after the examination, carrying her own unopened envelope.

A deduction had been taken for the private protection watch. Her name had been entered beside it as though she had volunteered. She had agreed to the loom shift. She had not agreed to give part of it away.

The cooperative's account worker had shown her the deduction and offered to put it in the complaint. The money itself was still short.

"Main complaint khaa loongi?" she asked.

Ira sat with the left covering in place. Her ordinary right-eye work had not been extended because a paper in the room seemed urgent. She asked the worker to read the amount and the words beside it.

The worker did. She read them angrily and accurately, then opened the envelope herself.

The two amounts did not match. They were not supposed to. One was the wage before the deduction; the other was what she had actually received.

Ira asked her to keep both facts in the account. Nobody should write that she had been paid nothing when the loss was a specific part she had not chosen to contribute.

"Mera hissa waapas kaise aayega?"

"Jisne kaata, uska jawaab chahiye," Ira said. "Mere likhne se tumhare envelope mein nahi aa jaayega."

The worker looked at the envelope, then at Ira's resting hand.

"Toh unko bulaao. Humse har baar kyun bulwaate hain?"

---

The cooperative's account worker came to the outer room with the visitor. The clinic would not fill the girls' room with another meeting, and the worker wanted her own pay discussed without every waiting patient listening.

Aadhya stayed where the hand could remain supported. Ira listened from beside her. Sitara kept the session within the short period they had agreed.

The account worker had not authorized the levy, he said. He had paid from what the cooperative could access while the disputed deduction was being challenged. He brought the relevant entry, not the full wage book of everybody in the shed.

The worker asked why the account had still labelled her contribution voluntary.

He crossed out that description in front of her, dated the correction and left the earlier word legible. The disputed amount remained visible. He would take the demand for its return to the people responsible for the deduction and give her the next response, not require her to walk between every desk again.

She did not thank him for correcting what should not have been written.

She asked when he would come back.

They agreed on a time. Sitara wrote it on the worker's own copy and read it aloud. If the money did not come, there would still be a next decision to make about the shift. Nobody asked her to keep weaving indefinitely because other families also needed wages.

---

After they left, Aadhya's hand was tired from doing almost nothing which would look like work from outside.

She let the helper settle it again.

"Woh sahi boli," she said.

Ira had closed the new notebook without using it. The worker's copy would go back with the worker; the account did not have to become another object in Ira's possession before it could be true.

"Kaunsi baat?"

"Har baar unko hi chal ke aana padta hai. Hum bas bolte hain, ek aur jawaab le aao."

Sitara moved a chair so the next visitor would not have to stand in the passage.

"Toh agli baar kiski baari hai chalne ki, woh bhi likho."

Aadhya looked at the short-session limit on the paper beside her bowl. It was not permission to go herself. It did not prevent the account worker from keeping the appointment he had just made.

She asked Sitara to confirm the time with the receiving woman, then rested.

---

The next morning's calls did not bring a single, tidy version of the night.

One worker wanted the warehouse named publicly. Another asked that his home not appear anywhere because his family still bought on credit from a man connected with the loading business. The two who had accepted paid watch work did not want to be called abducted men in a story they had not told.

The worker-support contact met them with the officer's account available. The post organizer gave the help he had promised and let them choose which personal details could be passed further.

Veer heard the summary during his short seated interval.

"Mera naam hataane mein sab lage the," he said. "Inka poochh ke likhna."

The volunteer nodded. This time the small distinction would go into the first account, not wait to be discovered under a headline.

Rudra asked whether the men had been paid enough to get home. Where a fare was needed, the post checked the actual journey and the relief money left. A man could accept that help without signing himself into the next public watch.

The balance in the drawer was smaller afterward.

---

Dhruv called from Varanasi after visiting the hospital.

Ram remained in step-down care. He had wanted to sit by the window and had managed a short supported period before tiring. Oxygen had been needed again. The drain site still had its dressing and review. None of those facts cancelled the others.

Rukmini had her own movement restrictions to follow. Dadi had gone with her for the appointment instead of staying in the ward as though worry were a duty which could never be handed over.

Dhruv listened to the account of the bus stand. He did not offer to drive to Prayagraj and leave the hometown arrangements uncovered. His palm was still tender; the ring remained wrapped.

"Yahaan subah ka supply nikal gaya," he said. "Agli gaadi ka check ab doosra volunteer karega."

Rudra repeated the name of that volunteer so the next call would not always go to Dhruv first.

Afterward he rang his mother at the agreed time. This time there was no new injury to report, and no excuse to hide the danger other people had faced. He told her he had stayed at the post while the response went through the people on the road.

She asked about the worker who had been sick. He said the medical team had him, and that was the limit of what he knew.

---

At the hall, the woman left her father and child with the attendant they knew and went to see the room in daylight.

Her relative came only as far as the lane. The bruise made carrying anything foolish, and she would not turn his return from one forced shift into another unpaid one. The caretaker had arranged a helper for the bedding she wanted to fetch.

The local officer met them at the common entrance while checking the complaint from the new hasp. Its holes remained in the wood. The old latch worked.

Her key opened her own room.

Inside, the cot stood where it had been when she left to collect her father. A cup had dried with tea at its bottom. The shelf above the cot had an empty place where the medicine tin had stood. She put her hand there before remembering that the tin was safely at the hall.

The room had not forgotten them. The people outside it had made returning difficult.

She asked the caretaker about the next night, the entrance contact and what would happen if the men came back. He showed her the arrangement he had made with the residents and the local police number. It was not a promise that an officer would sleep outside every household.

She decided her father would stay at the hall another night. She would bring him back after speaking to the clinician about the journey and seeing how the entrance arrangements worked through the evening.

Before leaving, she took the comb from the small shelf beside the mirror.

---

At the post, the organizer waited for the morning transport confirmations.

The foot-injured yard worker had a further assessment to reach. The kitchen had food packed for the hall. The dealer's point needed its first small delivery before opening. Separate destinations had kept the previous day from failing at one gate; each still needed somebody willing and able to make the journey.

The first driver cancelled by message.

The second answered the organizer's call, heard the route and said he would not take it.

"Raat ka sun liya," he said.

"Police contact saath rahega. Nikalne se pehle check—"

"Kal mere ghar se watch ka aadmi maang liya toh? Aap phone karte rahoge. Mere bachche kis se baat karenge?"

The organizer did not tell him the fear was an insult to the people who had answered last night's call.

He asked whether a different time or another checked route would help.

Not today.

The driver wanted his name taken off the available list. He had agreed to a paid run, not to become proof that nobody was frightened.

The organizer crossed the name out and called the medical transport service about the worker's appointment. The kitchen helper came to the door with the first covered pot on a trolley.

The food was ready.

Outside, the place reserved for the morning vehicle was empty.

The cook took the first pot back inside.

She did not leave it on the trolley to cool while the organizer tried to persuade a frightened man that a different route would change his mind. The hall had to be told what could not reach it. The food here could go to people here while it was still fit to serve.

The kitchen helper stopped with his hands on the trolley.

"Hall mein kya boloon?"

"Sach. Aur poochho unke paas abhi kitna hai."

At the other end of that call, the hall's cook opened the store with the receiving worker beside her. She could begin a smaller meal from the remaining dry stock. It would use what she had hoped to keep for evening.

The organizer wrote that loss of margin down before calling the next transport number.

Three collection places had survived one blocked gate. They had not become places which could survive forever without the people moving between them.

---

The medical service found a car for the foot-injured yard worker.

It would take him to the assessment and back with an attendant. It would not become a food van first because the kitchen's trolley stood nearer the door.

The worker's replacement asked whether the cost would come out of his injured colleague's wage. The organizer put the medical transport entry where both could see it, separate from the pay for completed work.

"Nahi. Iska alag hisaab hai."

The car arrived late enough that the receiving clinic had to be called about the appointment. The clinic adjusted the time. Nobody sent the worker home to return another day merely because a privately hired driver had withdrawn from a relief run.

By then, the hall's first smaller pot was on its stove.

---

The bus-stand dispatcher offered a possibility, not a rescue fleet.

Two regular services had approved space for small sealed consignments. There were limits on the weight, the storage and what could travel with passengers. The clean compartment had to be checked. The post's worker would pay the carriage and send a named receiver to the upper stop; a bus would not leave its route to turn into the hall's lane.

The organizer went with the kitchen worker to see the space before agreeing.

They sent dry stock, properly packed, not the cooked meal which had already been reassigned. Passengers kept their seats. A parcel which did not fit stayed behind. The dispatcher would not turn a public service into a different kind of commandeered vehicle because last night's passengers had watched the police stop one.

At the upper stop, two paid replacement porters met the consignment with the hall's trolley. The men hurt at the dealer's yard were still off that work.

The distance from the stop to the hall looked short on the route sheet. On the paving it took two journeys, a rest and help over a broken edge which the smaller wheels could not cross loaded.

A woman waiting at the hall came out to steady the empty trolley while the porters moved the first load in.

She offered that much. When her father called from inside, she went back to him. Nobody called after her that an extra pair of hands was still needed.

---

The first bus consignment reached the hall before the evening stock was gone.

The cook did not smile until she had opened it. She checked the contents, the quantity and what had been promised elsewhere. Then she began replacing the share she had borrowed from the later meal.

The carriage cost more than a full vehicle divided across its old planned load. The porters' time cost money. The broken paving had cost another half-hour, and the bus would not wait at the stop every morning while a receiver was found.

The kitchen worker stayed to write the actual route and timing for the next shift.

"Roz karenge?" one porter asked.

"Tum bolo pehle."

He would take two mornings. After that he had other paid work, and a child to collect on the third.

His partner could take one of those mornings and the following evening. The receiving worker wrote the times they offered, then left the rest of the page blank.

Nobody filled the empty spaces with the names of their brothers.

---

That afternoon a driver came to the post and asked about the run he had refused.

He had not changed his mind about today. He wanted to know whether refusing it had cost him every later job.

The organizer showed him the cancelled entry and the payment for the runs already done. Both remained ordinary records of work, not a list of loyal and disloyal households.

The driver sat down.

"Kal ka route dikhaao," he said. "Haan abhi nahi bol raha. Pehle dekhunga."

The organizer turned the sheet towards him.

Outside, the trolley's parking space was still empty. For the first time that morning, the empty space did not mean nobody was moving food.

Over the next seven days, the transport sheet kept changing every morning. Sometimes it changed between two calls.

On the second morning, the driver who had asked to see the route accepted one run with the current public contact and a return time written into it. He did not take the next one. The hall used the bus consignment and the trolley again instead of treating his first yes as something which could be inherited by every later shift.

On the third, a kitchen which had lent grain received part of it back. The cook there checked the share before putting it into her own store. She still needed the remainder. The post's organizer left that debt on the page where the next worker would see it.

On the fourth, the woman from the boat brought her father home.

---

She had spoken to the clinician about the journey and arranged help at both ends. Her relative's bruised arm was improving, but he was not the lifting plan. A local vehicle took the old man along the checked longer road. The broken foot crossing stayed closed.

The child watched it through the window from well above the landing.

"Woh theek nahi hua?"

"Nahi. Isliye iss raaste se jaa rahe hain."

At the common gate, the caretaker met them with the helper. The entrance shift was a residents' arrangement with a police contact, not a private pledge of household labour. People could pay or volunteer for agreed work. Nobody's room key had become security for somebody else's night away.

Her father needed a pause before the last few steps. He was irritated at the pause and the people politely pretending not to notice it. She let him be irritated while the chair was brought closer.

Inside, she put the medicine tin back on its shelf. The child's sandals went beside the cot. Her own phone charged where she could reach it without leaving the room.

The municipal hall kept the departure recorded as a chosen return to the existing address. Another family took the space they had used. Their mat did not have to stay empty for the story of their rescue to remain true.

That night she called the hall worker herself and said they were inside, the entrance had stayed staffed and her father had found the right comb unsatisfactory after all.

He had wanted it for so long that the teeth now seemed too far apart.

The inspection report on the crossing came that week.

The far joining section had failed where its support timber had deteriorated around a fastening. The technical team had recovered damaged wood and part of the connection. A remaining piece had not been found. They could not turn a missing piece into proof of who had handled it last, or certify the whole event from that one damaged joint.

The near joining boards had slewed after the crowd changed direction. That movement had its own witnesses and damage. The technical team had found no cut there.

The post displayed the closure and repair instructions, not photographs of the families.

When a man said this meant the private guards had done nothing, the worker who had watched the rope come down pointed to the separate accounts.

People had still been stopped from stepping onto a bank. A man had still followed a family to treatment and swung a staff. The technical finding did not erase what people had seen, any more than the witnessed assault proved a hand had cut the far connection.

The woman read the short public summary later with the caretaker.

She asked when the crossing would reopen.

There was no safe date to give her yet.

She folded the notice about the longer route and kept it beside the phone.

In Mirzapur, the worker with the disputed watch deduction received a response at the time written on her copy.

Part of the amount had been restored. The remaining part was still being pursued from the people who had taken it. The account worker brought the money and the revised entry to her, rather than sending a message that she should leave her shift and come looking for it.

She counted it herself. Then she asked what would happen at the next wage issue.

The cooperative had stopped labelling the private deduction voluntary. It was not collecting another one through the same pay envelope. The unresolved claims stayed with the affected workers, who could choose whether their accounts were shared for the wider complaint.

That did not make the damaged cloth saleable. The customer accepted the undamaged part after inspection and granted time on the rest. The cooperative lost money on the sorting and the smaller delivery. Repairing the frame took paid hours from another task.

The kitchen women kept their meal arrangement because it spared families the loading court, not because they had acquired the right to decide the cooperative's wages.

Aadhya heard the first resumed packing shift from the clinic window. Ira asked the runner who was meeting the departing vehicle and whether the workers had been told the actual revised payment date.

They had.

The answer let her close her eye and rest. She did not need to stand beside the vehicle to make the departure count as work the team had helped defend.

At the end of that week, Rudra walked to the examination room's window and back with the physiotherapist beside him.

The distance was ordinary enough to be insulting until he reached the turn.

His ankle had tolerated more controlled loading over the intervening days. The adapted support still protected the wrist. At the window he began to pivot, remembered the instruction and moved through the smaller turn she had shown him instead.

The return hurt less than the first attempt after Varanasi. It still hurt.

"Distance badha hai," the physiotherapist said when he sat. "Permission ka matlab nahi badla."

Travel with support and rests, short seated tasks, prescribed movement. Not running, fighting or finding out how much a frightened crowd might make him forget.

Veer had his own reassessment. Some ordinary shoulder movement was easier, with the sling used as instructed rather than as a badge he could discard. His knee still caught after too long in one position. The physiotherapist changed the timing of the movements and the rest between them. She did not invite him to discover a stance which made pain irrelevant.

They left with small, separate improvements. Neither had the other's clearance.

---

The collar dressing came off after the clinician had checked the closed surface.

The skin was no longer open. Rudra turned his head carefully while the place was cleaned and inspected. A brief tightening across the chest brought gold into view above the loose collar.

Vedant held the mirror where Rudra could see without twisting farther.

The branch they had drawn after the assault had not disappeared. Its end now reached nearer the older broken line. A narrow strip still separated them, thin enough that Rudra had to wait for the reflection to settle before he could be certain it remained.

He did not try to make the light brighter.

"Aaj hua?" he asked.

"Aaj dekha," Vedant said. "Kab badha, uska record nahi hai."

The clinician finished examining the healed skin. Vedant marked the changed outline on a separate sketch, beside the earlier one. The two accounts were kept distinct. No ruler had been laid against the light; no measurement would be invented to make the fear look precise.

Rudra had not made a full form during those days. He had not trained through the night's call, fought at the market or tested the old count when nobody was watching.

That did not establish what had carried the damage farther. It did establish that he could not use the absence of another battle as proof that it had repaired itself.

He remembered wanting every visible wound to close so he could stop being a patient. Now the most reassuring part of the examination was the part least able to answer this other question.

"Dono jud gaye toh?" he asked.

Vedant looked at the two broken ends.

"Nahi pata. Isliye jod ke dekhna nahi hai."

Rudra let the mirror be lowered.

Afterward, Sitara lowered the lamp in Mirzapur. Vedant gave the late post phone to the next volunteer in Prayagraj. Neither mentor stayed awake to prove that somebody competent had to be watching every breath.

Beyond the far bank, the woman put her father's medicine where he expected it and settled her son on the cot.

The caretaker's entrance shift changed outside. She heard the next worker give his name, then the ordinary scrape of a chair being moved out of the lane.

She checked her room's latch herself.

The key with the red cotton loop lay on the shelf. When she closed the door for the night, nobody outside asked which list gave her permission to stay behind it.

---

At the post, Rudra was settling against the pillow when the warmth came above his collarbone.

He had not counted for a form. He had not put his foot down or loaded the braced wrist. The skin beneath his fingers remained closed.

He called Vedant before trying to understand it alone.

The clinician was still in the adjoining room. He came back and began the ordinary checks while Vedant held the mirror where Rudra could see without twisting. The volunteer went to the family contact with the facts: awake, being examined, new symptoms, more information after the checks.

Gold showed along the familiar broken outline.

The small strip between the branch and the older fracture was no longer whole.

A dark line crossed it while Rudra watched, joining the two irregular ends. The rest of the visible outline remained. There was no great light, no force filling the room, no strength returning to the foot on its support.

"Yeh wala," he said. "Jo beech mein tha."

Vedant had seen it.

Within the joined lines, a small patch above the collarbone stayed dark while the gold around it held.

The clinician asked Rudra to leave his hand down. There was still no blood on the closed skin.

The small dark patch did not light with the rest.

# CHAPTER SEVENTY-FOUR — THE LINE AROUND THE RING

---

> *"The bell permitted a contest.*
> *Later, men tried to make it mean that every body inside the line had agreed."*
>
> *— first-day tournament review, medical objections appended*

---

The clinician checked the skin where the light had failed.

It was warm beneath his fingers. Closed. There was no bleeding, no new blister or torn place which could explain the shape Rudra had seen in the mirror.

He checked Rudra's breathing, pulse and ordinary responses before asking him to describe the sensation again. Vedant kept the mirror lowered. Neither man asked for another flash to make the account easier.

"Dard?"

"Garmi pehle thi. Ab kam hai."

"Saans mein badlaav?"

Rudra answered what he could feel, not what he thought would let him remain on the bed at the post. The dark patch had frightened him enough that pretending not to be a patient suddenly looked like another way of being alone with it.

The clinician wanted him observed where further assessment was available through the night. The ordinary checks had not explained the change. They had not made it safe to ignore.

---

The transport was for Rudra, not for the whole receiving post.

A vehicle came through the approach which the local contact had just checked. The supported transfer protected the wrist and ankle. His bag went with the earlier medical notes and the two drawings, the observed light kept separate from the account of the closed wound.

Veer stayed at the post with the volunteer and the clinician's instructions for his own knee and shoulder. When the stretcher came through, his good hand tightened on the edge of the mattress. He let it go.

"Phone rakhna paas," he said.

Rudra looked at the lead which had finally been freed from the table leg.

"Charger bhi."

The volunteer put it into the bag without making either of them bend for it.

Vedant went in the vehicle. The organizer took the remaining calls. Food, work and the people waiting at the hall did not become unimportant because one of the visitors needed a hospital.

---

At the hospital, the receiving doctor read the actual report before asking about the story already gathering outside it.

Rudra had not fought that evening. He had not called a full form. The skin had closed before the new sensation. A small section within the broken outline had stayed dark while the surrounding gold remained visible, then the light itself had faded.

Fading was the last observed event. Nobody wrote repaired.

A nurse fitted the pressure cuff around his unbraced arm and put a clip on one finger. The doctor listened to his chest, checked the area again and asked about any new faintness or loss of feeling. Rudra had neither to report.

The doctor arranged observation and further checks, keeping the earlier injuries supported through the examination. He did not offer a name for the part he could not examine in the same way. When the cuff came off, the nurse stayed to settle the foot before moving the trolley.

Vedant described the joined lines without extending the account to the whole chest. He made no measurement he had not taken. The earlier drawing stayed beside the new one.

"Dobara dikha sakte ho?" a junior attendant asked before the doctor finished.

"Nahi karenge," Vedant said.

Rudra realized he had already begun preparing an apology for the failure to provide a better demonstration.

He let the apology go.

---

Rukmini was still awake when the fuller update reached her.

The first message had said awake and being examined. She wanted the place, the next step and the person who would call if it changed. Vedant gave all three before passing the phone to Rudra for the short conversation the doctor allowed.

"Main bataane waala tha" would have been an old excuse. He did not need it tonight. Somebody had already told her while he was being checked.

"Hospital mein hoon," he said. "Observation. Abhi garmi kam hai. Baaki ka reason nahi pata."

She asked whether he had tried to make the outline return.

"Nahi."

"Theek. Jo nahi pata, uske liye khud ko phir mat chot dena."

Ram was resting in the step-down ward. Rukmini would give him the update with the staff's advice; he did not have to be woken and made to reassure a son before his own next examination. Dhruv would help send any earlier notes the treating team needed, without leaving the hometown arrangements uncovered.

Rudra heard Dadi ask for the receiving number in the background. His mother repeated it slowly until she had it right.

He handed the phone back before the small conversation became a promise that he would be well by morning.

---

Sitara spoke to Vedant after the admission.

She wanted the observation, not a conclusion about what lay under the seal. The phone could carry the description and the plan. It could not carry her into the room or make a familiar lesson into treatment for a change nobody understood.

The girls were told where Rudra was and that he was being watched. Ira's eye review in Mirzapur remained on the next morning's plan. Aadhya's hand instructions did not turn into permission to travel through the night.

Vedant sat beside the hospital bed until the next observation was done. Then he took the offered chair outside the bay while the nurse stayed on duty.

Rudra closed his eyes without finding a count which promised that the missing light would return.

---

Two full days of observation followed.

Rudra reported no further warmth like the first episode during those checks. The doctor was careful about what that meant. The light had not been called again. Nobody had seen the little patch restored. A quieter night could be entered as a quieter night without becoming proof that the broken outline was sound.

The earlier team received the notes. The local plan kept Rudra under staffed care while the next assessment was arranged. His ankle and wrist still needed their own support and prescribed movement. He was not discharged to demonstrate that rest had worked.

In Mirzapur, Ira attended the eye review promised before the alarm. The left covering remained. Her ordinary right-eye periods were still limited, and any change had its own return instructions. She told the clinician what she had actually used the eye for, including the short cooperative visit. No second sight had been hidden inside that account.

Aadhya stayed with the hand plan. She asked for the clinic's next update after it had been made, not for a journey to Prayagraj which would turn both girls' care into another unplanned transfer.

---

The tournament notice reached the post on the second afternoon.

The local akhada was opening a run of contests between Kul clubs and individual entrants. Rakshak and Prakriti representatives were among the first entries. The printed sheet had a schedule, a registration table, a medical check and a purse made up by the participating clubs and traders.

It did not have a court seal. Winning a bout would not make the river landing, a public road or a household belong to the winner.

That did not stop a man at the noticeboard from saying the streets would finally learn who their real protectors were.

The organizer asked him to leave that sentence off the meal-service notice beside it.

The contests could happen. The hall's food would not be awarded by their score.

---

Veer read the schedule during his short seated interval.

For the first time in days, he asked a question which was not about somebody's blocked route or overdue payment.

"Dekhne jaa sakte hain?"

The clinician asked where he would sit, how he would get there and how long he meant by watching. Veer gave the first eager answer, then the answer he could actually keep.

One short session. A supported seat away from the standing crowd. The sling as instructed, room for the knee, no lifting a rope for someone else, no demonstration if an announcer recognized him. A return vehicle would wait at the checked outer entrance. If the symptoms changed, the session ended.

The clinician agreed to a spectator visit under those limits after the morning review. He wrote spectator where the club helper could see it.

Veer did not ask him to leave the word out in case it embarrassed him later.

---

On the third morning, Veer's review kept those limits in place. Vedant then went over the arrangement with the hospital staff before taking him.

Rudra would stay in the observation bay. The nurse had the current contacts; a post volunteer Rudra knew would sit with him for part of the morning. Another person could fetch the bag or make a call. Vedant was not the hospital's only safeguard, and leaving for a short visit did not mean pretending there was nothing to safeguard.

He told Rudra where they would be.

"Tum bhi dekhna chahte ho?" Veer asked over the phone.

"Haan."

There was no shame in the answer and no permission hidden in it.

"Aaj nahi," Rudra added. "Jo hua woh sunaa dena. Commentary mein apne points mat badhaa dena."

Veer looked at the paper which permitted him to watch.

"Mere points abhi kursi pe milenge."

Rudra laughed once, tired but real. He asked the volunteer to put the phone down within reach afterward. They ended without making his friend responsible for returning with a victory to cheer him up.

---

At the post entrance, the porter from the dealer's gate was asking the club helper about the registration hours.

He had come for a short work discussion at the post, not another load. The helper had the tournament sheet open on his knee. The porter pointed to the allowance paid when an entrant actually began a bout, then to the separate prize.

"Naam sabke saamne?" he asked.

The helper said the public board used the entry number and the Kul or club the entrant chose to represent. The medical desk needed the actual name privately. A household address would not be announced.

The porter pulled his sleeve over the wrist which had been protected at his last check.

Veer noticed the movement from inside the waiting vehicle.

"Bas dekhne?" he called.

The man looked up.

"Pehle poochh raha hoon."

The helper folded the spare sheet and gave it to him. There was no agreement on it yet.

Vedant checked that Veer's foot space was clear before closing the vehicle door. They would not be standing at the registration table all morning to make anybody else's choice for him.

---

The Prayagraj akhada stood behind a public lane wide enough for the return vehicle to wait without blocking its entrance.

Inside, the packed-earth ring lay below a low verandah. A thick chalk boundary marked the contest; a rope set farther back kept spectators off the officials' walking strip. On the opposite side stood the registration and medical tables. A side passage ran behind the verandah to a quiet room and the outer exit.

Vedant checked that passage before the club helper brought Veer's chair.

The supported seat was at the end of the row, not in the standing crowd. A folded cloth raised the knee. The vehicle contact had the return time. Veer could see the ring without having to prove he could stand over everyone else's head.

Below him, two competitors were already having the morning's rules read back to them.

---

These were preliminary control bouts.

First to three points: a clean touch on either shoulder tab, or an opponent's whole foot beyond the chalk. Each awarded exchange stopped and reset. Face, throat and eyes were not targets. No cutting contact, no full form in this format, no continuing through a referee's stop or medical withdrawal.

The referee showed the stop signal to both corners. A competitor could concede without waiting to be hurt enough for the crowd to believe him.

Around one stretch of the ring, a narrow strip of rooted grass had been left in the earth. The officials checked it with the surface before the first pair entered. A Prakriti contestant could work with that living source, not demand a new forest from the dry centre. Any movement which endangered the spectators would end the bout, not earn a louder point.

A steward wiped old chalk from the scoring board.

The first two club representatives took their corner bands, green and white. The announcer gave their entry numbers and the Kuls they represented: Prakriti for the woman in green, Rakshak for the man in white.

They were strangers to Veer. He watched their feet before deciding what their labels told him.

---

The woman tested the earth with the heel of her palm.

A little movement passed through the grass near the boundary. She released it and stood. The man brought a curved pale guard close to his forearm, opened it once and let it thin again.

Neither display transformed the body carrying it.

The bell sounded.

White moved first, short steps keeping the curved face between his leading shoulder and her hand. Green gave him ground towards the living strip. Her left shoulder tab stayed visible just beyond the guard's rim, close enough to invite him to lengthen the next step.

He did.

She dropped her palm heel to the earth.

A runner tightened where his forward foot had reached the grass edge. It did not lift him off the ground. His heel turned a little under the change, and he checked the balance with his free hand.

She came up beside the guard, touched the white tab at the unprotected shoulder and moved clear.

The referee stopped them before the crowd finished its first shout.

One point, green.

The woman brushed grit from the palm she had put down. A red scrape showed at its heel. The runner slackened back into the grass; it had been pulled, not made inexhaustible by answering.

---

Veer had leaned forward.

His knee reminded him before Vedant did. He settled it again and watched the replay the two bodies offered by walking back to their corners.

The pale guard had done what it could do. It had not covered the far shoulder merely because its owner had wanted to reach her. The root had changed one small contact with the ground, not won an argument against the weight of the man.

There had been a gap. She had been quick enough to use it.

Veer felt the old pleasure of understanding a good exchange before he felt the grief of being unable to take one himself.

He did not have to spoil the first feeling to admit the second.

---

At the reset, White came forward with the same apparent offer.

Two short steps. A little opening at the far shoulder.

Green moved towards the strip, palm lowering.

This time he stopped before putting the foot where it had been. His guard remained close instead of reaching after her. The tightening runner moved against empty earth while her weight was still travelling down towards the hand.

He stepped round the small curve of her shoulder and laid his free fingers on the green tab.

The referee's hand cut between them.

One point each.

The man did not finish the movement as a throw. The woman did not pull at the grass again after the signal. She stood, took the accepted point against her and flexed the scraped palm before returning to the mark.

Beside the scoring table, a boy kept clapping long after the others had stopped. His father lowered the hand gently so he could hear the reset instructions.

On the verandah, Veer looked towards the medical table. A club helper was pointing somebody with a lowered sleeve towards the examination chair.

Then the next exchange began, and he turned back to the ring.

---

White took the next point at the boundary.

He did not try to stretch the guard over both shoulders. He kept it close and advanced by the half-steps which left him free to stop. Green retreated towards the strip, found the runner she had already strained and chose not to pull it again from that angle.

She slipped sideways instead.

His guard turned with him. She cleared its edge, but the whole of her trailing foot came down beyond the chalk.

The line judge raised a hand.

Two–one, white.

Green looked down before she looked at the official. Her footprint lay plainly outside. She nodded and returned to the centre.

The next exchange was shorter. She showed him the same escape, waited for the curve to turn and touched the shoulder he had left behind it.

Two each.

White went back to his corner breathing through his mouth. A dark crescent of sweat had reached the neck of his vest. The guard had stayed small; holding even that much through the repeated exchanges was work.

Across from him, Green held her scraped palm away from her clothes. Her corner offered water. She took it with the other hand.

---

They returned without being hurried.

The last point began near the centre, too far from the grass for the easy opening White had now learned to expect.

He offered the far shoulder again.

Green did not take it.

He waited. So did she.

Somebody behind Veer shouted for them to do something worth the entry fee.

Below, the man shifted his weight and brought the pale curve forward to make the invitation harder to refuse. The near edge flickered. He pulled it back towards his forearm.

Green moved on that return, not towards the offered shoulder but inside the space his own hand had just vacated.

Her uninjured fingertips touched his tab.

The referee was between them at once.

Three–two, green.

White let the shape go. The woman stopped with her hand still open, close enough to touch him again and choosing not to. Then she stepped back until the official lowered his arm.

The crowd wanted the winning exchange repeated. Neither contestant owed it another attempt.

---

The medical helper washed the grit from Green's palm beside the ring. White sat on the low stool at his corner while somebody checked the forearm he was rubbing.

The grass did not spring upright for the photograph. One runner lay flattened where she had worked it. A grounds worker marked the damaged length before the next pair could use that stretch.

When the announcer called her forward for the result, Green first waited for the dressing to be secured. The official put the bout payment and the winner's addition into her good hand.

She counted neither for the audience.

White came over once he had been checked. They touched their uninjured hands briefly. He pointed towards the last opening, asked something Veer could not hear and laughed at the reply.

Veer found he was smiling too.

He had expected the ring to hurt only because he could not enter it. For a little while it had been possible to enjoy somebody else's skill without borrowing their body to imagine his own restored.

Vedant passed him the water.

"Aakhri waala?" he asked.

"Usne shield ko haraaya nahi," Veer said. "Haath waapas aane diya."

He lifted his own good hand a little to show the path, then stopped before the shoulder followed it.

Understanding was not a movement permission either.

---

The porter came up the side passage while the grounds worker renewed the chalk.

He had no corner band yet. The wrist protector was in his other hand.

Veer made room beside the chair without asking him to sit where the knee needed space. The man remained at the verandah post.

"Doctor ne kya kaha?" Vedant asked.

The porter looked towards the medical table.

"Purana note manga. Wahaan se mangwa rahe hain."

"Tab tak?"

"Ek baar chal ke dekhna hai. Bina zor. Woh alag se bolenge."

The medical decision was still pending. Vedant asked the man not to take an introduction or a warm-up for clearance. The porter turned the protector in his hand until the seam lay beneath his thumb.

"Maal uthaane waali jagah meri jagah koi aur hai," he said.

There was no accusation in it. The replacement had done the work and earned the pay. Knowing that did not put a full day's wages into the injured man's pocket.

Veer looked at the winner leaving the medical table with a small envelope in her good hand.

"Tumhara kaam chhoota hai," he said. "Chot nahi."

The porter nodded. His eyes stayed on the ring.

A club helper called for the next entrants to assemble below. The man put the protector back on and said he would hear what they wanted before agreeing to anything.

---

The next scored bout was delayed while an entrant's earlier treatment note was checked.

The announcer filled the interval with a club introduction. People would show their basic guards and positions, he said. No score would be kept. Each participant would receive a smaller appearance payment. Veer saw two helpers bring the entry board round to the waiting side and turn it face down.

A child on the lower step asked whether the winner had already won the whole tournament. His father showed him the other spaces on the schedule. Green's result had earned her the next match, not made the rest unnecessary.

Near the exit, she stood with the dressed hand held clear while her club companion argued about the best place to buy breakfast.

Veer wished he could stay for all of it.

He checked the time himself before Vedant had to ask. One session had been the arrangement, not the first session plus every interesting thing which followed.

The return vehicle was ready outside. Vedant asked the helper to leave the side passage clear while he checked the distance from the chair to the exit. Veer would use the support and the agreed pauses. He let the helper place it beside his good hand.

---

At the hospital, the post volunteer had just finished reading Rudra a message from home.

Ram had received the update. His own rest and care continued. Rukmini wanted Rudra to know that she had spoken to the ward, not only listened to the hurried family account. She would wait for the next planned clinical update unless something changed.

Rudra asked the volunteer to type that the nurse had helped him with the prescribed movement and that the wrist was back on its support. The light had not been tested. He had asked about the next assessment; the answer remained pending.

Then he dictated a message for Veer.

*Kaun jeeta? Aur time se waapas aana.*

He asked the volunteer to read it back. The second sentence sounded very much like something he had spent years pretending not to hear from his mother.

He left it in.

---

Veer heard the message on the verandah while the helper brought his walking support.

He sent the result, three–two, and said he would tell the rest when he returned. He had barely put the phone away when the porter stepped through the gap in the spectator rope.

A helper had tied a blue corner band above the wrist support. Another club member waited opposite with a red one. The announcer called their entry numbers for the introduction, not for a scored match.

Veer looked towards the medical desk. Nobody there had waved the porter in.

"Note aa gaya?" he asked the helper beside his chair.

The young man looked at the registration copy.

"Number toh hai."

"Medical ka poochha."

He checked the copy again. It showed the entry payment and the number. He went down the verandah steps towards the table.

Vedant stayed long enough to put Veer's support where he could take it without twisting. Then he followed through the officials' walking strip, not across the contest line.

---

The porter was telling the referee what he had agreed to do.

One walk forward, the basic shape held at the side. No contact, no throw, no falling practice. He wanted the introduction allowance; the prize would require a bout he had not yet been cleared to enter.

The referee turned towards the assembly helper.

"Medical?"

The helper held up the registration copy he had brought from the waiting line.

The man at the far corner opened a small pale curve beside his forearm. His friends called for him to turn so the verandah could see it. He smiled towards them, then faced the porter again.

Veer could hear the cloth of his own sling moving with a breath. He wanted to stand high enough for the official to see him. The knee caught at the first shift, and he sat back before that wish put another patient into the passage.

"Referee!" he called instead.

Below, the porter had taken the agreed step. A thin arc formed at his protected wrist, wavering where the support stopped him from taking the angle he usually used.

This was the hand which had made room for the constable at the gate. It was not a fresh hand because a different crowd was watching.

---

At the medical table, the clinician looked up as Vedant reached it.

He had requested the earlier note. He had not cleared the porter for contact, scored or otherwise. The introduction had not been put to him as a separate proposal.

He stood at once and raised the stop signal towards the ring.

The referee had lowered the registration paper. He saw the medical arm go up and turned back with both palms out.

"Roko. Dono."

Blue tried to let his small shape go.

Red had already stepped forward to meet it for the display. The pale curve at his forearm touched Blue's wavering edge and pressed it back towards the wrist inside the old support.

The porter drew his elbow in. His shoulder turned with it; he stepped back across his own heel, trying to take the pressure out of the hand.

Red heard the stop and began to release. He could stop pushing. He could not take back the weight which had already travelled through the contact.

The thin arc at the porter's wrist went out.

His injured hand went down to catch him.
