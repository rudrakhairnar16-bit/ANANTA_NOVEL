# ANANTA — BOOK 04 — MERGED

# CHAPTER SIXTY-ONE — THE FIRST BARRICADE

---

> *"The first boundary crossed a road we had used to carry the wounded.*
> *By noon, men on both sides were calling it protection."*
>
> *— relief driver's deposition, collected after the first road closures*

---

The first relief van to return to the hospital that morning brought back all its food.

Rudra saw the covered pots before he saw the driver. They were being lifted down beneath the corridor window, their lids still tied with the kitchen's strips of cloth. An attendant touched one with the back of his fingers and called somebody over. Steam escaped when the knot came loose.

The food was still warm.

The man who should have delivered it sat on the low wall beside the ramp, pressing a gamchha against his cheek.

"Neeche kya hua?" Rudra asked.

The nurse finished securing his ankle dressing before looking towards the window. His chair stood well back from the glass, the foot raised. She had brought him there while the observation bed was being changed. It was not a place from which he could lean out and direct the people below.

"Pata kar rahe hain. Pair yahin."

He left the foot where she had put it.

Downstairs, a security guard took the driver towards the treatment entrance. Another stayed with the van. The kitchen attendant carried one pot away instead of letting it sit in the sun.

Behind Rudra, a trolley wheel squeaked. The smell of fresh phenyl came through the open bay door.

The hospital had gone on being a hospital after they stopped laughing in the courtyard.

---

Ram had needed oxygen through the night. The chest drain remained in place. There had been no new collapse in pressure, but the doctor wanted another examination and another day of close watching before saying more.

Rudra had heard the update with his mother. The doctor had waited for their questions before returning to the unit.

Rukmini was back in her bay now, Dadi sitting beside her. The left shoulder stayed supported; the dark pressure marks below her jaw had spread into colours Rudra disliked looking at. She had asked whether Ram's breathing hurt less. The doctor had answered about the treatment for pain, not promised that the injury had stopped hurting.

Rudra had brought that distinction back with him.

Now he watched the second pot being carried away and wondered how many people would have been waiting for it at the other end.

Vedant arrived with the answer to the smaller question first.

"Gaadi roki thi. Hospital ke peechhe, anaaj-mandi ke chauraha pe."

"Aegis?"

"Driver se sunte hain. Naam hum nahi bharte."

The words were quiet, without rebuke. Vedant pulled a chair across, leaving room for the trolley to pass.

The driver came after his cheek had been cleaned. A small dressing covered the cut. He held a hospital slip in one hand and two other pieces of paper in the other. The security guard stayed at the bay entrance; the nurse gave them a few minutes while she entered the dressing change.

"Rasoi se nikle the?" Vedant asked.

"Subah. Ghat waale parivaar school mein hain na. Wahin khaana."

At the grain-market crossing, men with a handcart across the road had stopped him. They wanted the pots unloaded under their supervision. He had shown the kitchen's delivery note.

"Bole, parchi badlo. Rakshak suraksha mein jaayega. Maine kaha, rasoi ka hai. Sabke liye."

The slap had knocked his cheek against the edge of the van's door. He touched the dressing, then lowered his hand when the nurse looked up.

"Thappad kisne maara?" she asked.

"Jo cart ke paas tha. Naam nahi pata."

She wrote down his answer. Vedant did not interrupt the medical account to ask about the man's strength.

The driver had reversed into the adjoining lane. There another group had stopped him, beside a shuttered seed shop. A woman there had read the first slip and torn off its corner.

"Boli, Prakriti ke raaste pe unki parchi nahi chalegi. Apni di. Maine poochha, school toh ek hi hai. Khaana kahaan utaaroon?"

"Kya kaha?" Rudra asked.

"Pehle tay karo kis taraf se aaye ho."

Through the window, the van's rear doors stood open. An attendant was passing down the last pot.

The driver laid both slips on the empty chair. One had a thumbprint beside a demand for supervised passage. The other carried a signature and the name of the seed-shop lane. Neither bore a hospital order or the kitchen's agreement.

"Inhone apna Kul bataya," Vedant said. "Itna pakka hai. Poora gharana kya kar raha hai, abhi nahi pata."

Rudra nodded, though his eyes had gone to the road beyond the ramp.

When the wounded first reached that road, they had needed every pace of it. This morning somebody had decided the way belonged to him.

---

Aadhya heard about the food while a porter moved her chair back from the hand clinic. Both hands rested on the pillow again. The right thumb and index finger had not begun to feel the cloth beneath them.

Ira was waiting outside the adjoining eye room, turned away from the window. The covering over her left eye was clean. She listened while Vedant repeated what the driver had actually seen.

"School ko phone hua?" she asked.

The kitchen had reached its volunteer there. Breakfast had not arrived. They had some dry food left; they needed to know whether to keep waiting or use it.

"Use karne ko bolo," Ira said. "Aur yeh slips rakhna. Inke kehne pe rasoi ki parchi mat phenk dena."

She asked for the route names to be read once more: the two turns where a driver had been stopped, and which way he had finally come back.

Vedant called the kitchen. The hospital desk had already informed the police about the obstruction and the assault.

Before he finished, Veer's mother came along the corridor beside her son's chair, bringing him back from the dressing desk. Veer was in yesterday's sling. Sleep had not made his knee fit to carry him up a hospital ramp.

His mother stopped beside Vedant.

"Bahar ek aadmi milne aaya hai," she said. "Veer se. Keh raha hai, apne Kul ke hain."

---

Veer looked down at the sling before answering.

Its edge had begun to cut into the back of his neck. His mother loosened the fastening while he held still, then folded a small clean cloth beneath the strap. She had tied his wrist-thread for journeys before. This knot she checked with two fingers underneath it.

"Dheela ho gaya?" he asked.

"Jitna chahiye. Haath ko sahaara mil raha hai na?"

He started to touch the cloth, then stopped when the movement tugged at the dressing on his right elbow.

"Haan."

"Bas. Zyada kasne se achha nahi hota."

She smoothed the back of his collar. Waited for his answer about the visitor.

Veer looked towards Vedant.

"Paanch minute. Yahin. Maa saath rahengi."

The nurse pointed them towards the recessed visitors' seats beyond the clinical door. No blocking the passage; no one going into the bays to find a patient. The guard brought the man there and stayed within sight.

He wore a pale kurta with one cuff rolled higher than the other. A row of small stitches ran along the exposed forearm. When he sat opposite Veer, he settled the injured arm carefully on his thigh.

"Kal se dhoondh rahe the," he said. "Ghat pe tumne jo kiya—"

"Abhi uski baat nahi," Veer's mother said. "Kya kaam hai?"

He accepted the interruption with a nod.

He said his group had come to guard the grain-market crossing. Rakshak families had lost men and property in the siege; other households had begun moving stores before the rubble was cold. There were medicines being taken from one godown and seed sacks from another. If no one held the road, everything would vanish.

"Kiski dawa?" Vedant asked.

"Hisaab hoga. Pehle rokna padega."

"Khaane ki gaadi bhi?"

The man glanced at the two slips in Vedant's hand.

"Baantne ka zimma lenge toh gin ke lenge. Kal phir kami padi toh hamaare darwaaze pe hi aayenge."

The food was not his, Vedant said. The school had asked the kitchen for it. The hospital had given the van a place to turn and load.

"Hospital se jhagda nahi hai," the man replied. He looked back at Veer. "Tum saamne aa jaao. Baithkar hi bolna hai. Itna ki apne logon ka raasta hum rakhenge. Log tumhari sunenge."

Veer's good hand lay flat on the chair arm.

"Aur doosre log?"

"Unke apne hain."

Aadhya's chair had stopped a little behind his, on the shaded side. The man's gaze reached her pillow, the bandages, then her face.

"Tumhare liye bhi gaadi aayegi. Prakriti waale apne ilaake mein le jaayenge. Yahan saath rehne se baat aur uljhegi."

"Kaun le jaayega?" she asked.

He named the seed-shop lane, not a relative she knew.

"Wahan jo khade hain, unse poochhna. Hamaare saath unki nahi banti. Par tum unki ho, baat maanenge."

Aadhya kept her hands where they were.

"Mere doctor ki baat abhi sun lenge? Yeh haath lekar kahin nahi jaana hai."

"Main ilaaj chhodne ko nahi—"

"Toh gaadi mat bhejna."

The man leaned back. His injured forearm slid; he caught it with the other hand and drew breath through his teeth.

For a moment nobody spoke.

Veer's mother asked whether the stitches had been checked that morning. He shook his head. She pointed out the dressing desk before looking back at her son.

"Tumne jawaab nahi diya," the man said to him.

Veer watched the careful way he was holding the arm.

"Raasta bachane aaye ho toh raasta kholo."

The man's mouth tightened.

"Ek baar bolkar khul jaata toh main tumhare paas nahi aata. Udhar jo baithe hain, woh bhi Kul ka naam le rahe hain. Tumhare doston ka."

"Mere naam se inka raasta band nahi hoga," Veer said.

Rudra saw Aadhya turn towards him. Not in surprise. As if a thing which should not have needed saying had nevertheless needed to be heard.

From inside the clinical door, Ira asked for the nurse. She had heard enough; the uncovered eye was watering in the brighter light. The nurse took her chair back first.

The guard checked the time and stepped nearer.

Vedant's phone rang before the visitor could try again.

He listened, then stood.

"Mandi ke chauraha pe? Gaadi ka engine band karwaao. Kisi ko pahiye ke neeche mat bhejna. Police ko wahi location do. Main aa raha hoon."

The visitor had risen too.

"Kya hua?"

"Gaadi kheench rahe hain. Aadmi phans gaya."

For the first time the man looked towards the hospital entrance without looking back at Veer. He went with the guard, moving quickly despite the arm.

Vedant asked the nurse to tell Sitara where he was going. She would stay with the patients. Two constables had reached the gate to take the driver's account; he would meet them downstairs.

"Main—" Rudra began, and stopped.

Vedant waited.

Rudra looked towards the observation bay, where the driver had left the delivery note.

"School ko bata dena. Doosri gaadi usi raste mat bhejein."

Vedant nodded. "Abhi."

He took his shallow basin from the bag beside the wall and left carrying it low beside his knee.

---

Two turns from the hospital, a sack of flour burst against a shutter.

White dust hung across the crossing. Through it, Vedant saw the nose of a small goods carrier, a handcart tipped towards the gutter, and a man lying on his side beside its trapped wheel.

The cart's long shaft lay across his lower leg.

"Engine band!" one of the constables shouted.

The carrier's engine stopped. Someone kept sounding its horn until the other constable reached the cab and struck the door with his palm.

"Band matlab band! Peechhe log hain!"

The horn stopped too.

Beyond the cart, a woman stood at the seed-shop corner with one hand against the trunk of a roadside tree. Roots had broken through the old mortar below it. One ran along the gutter, thickened round the cart's axle, and disappeared beneath a fallen sack.

Opposite her, a broad man held the cart's front rail. A pale edge stood along his other forearm. It caught the constable's raised lathi and turned it sideways when he tried to get between the two groups.

The constable staggered into a bystander. Both stayed upright.

"Prakriti ka maal chhod!" the woman called.

"Tere godaam se nahi liya!" somebody shouted back.

The injured loader tried to pull his knee towards his chest. The cart shifted with him. He stopped with a sound which made Vedant put the basin down at once.

"Isko chhodo pehle," Vedant said.

Neither side answered him. The woman pressed her hand harder against the trunk. The root drew tight; the axle grated across the gutter stone.

The loader screamed.

The man from the hospital came out of the turning behind Vedant.

"Haath hatao!" he shouted at the broad man. "Neeche aadmi hai!"

"Dikh raha hai. Udhar se kheenchegi toh—"

"Toh chhod!"

He caught the cart's rail with his good arm. The other man did not release it.

Vedant went down on one knee beside the loader, keeping outside the space under the shaft.

"Naam?"

The answer came broken by breath.

"Theek. Meri taraf dekho. Pair mat kheenchna. Abhi nahi."

The loader's hand closed on his sleeve.

At the goods carrier, the second constable was getting the driver out on the side away from the struggle. The first had moved the bystanders behind a parked delivery truck and was calling for another vehicle, giving the junction and warning that people were using powers in the road.

An upended drinking-water drum lay against the seed-shop step. Water continued to leak through its broken tap, finding a course towards the flour-clogged gutter.

Vedant set the basin beneath the run. The driver, following his pointing hand, dragged a length of discarded packing wood off the carrier's bed.

"Pahiye ke peechhe. Upar se nahi. Side se."

The driver pushed the wood along the ground until it met the rim. A chock, not a lever. If the root let go, the wheel would not roll farther into the gutter.

Vedant looked towards the woman.

"Tumhari jad ke neeche phansa hai. Dekho idhar."

"Inko bolo gaadi se hato."

"Main tumse iske pair ki baat kar raha hoon."

She looked down.

For a moment her fingers stayed spread against the tree. Then two lifted. The root loosened a little round the axle.

"Abhi kheenchna nahi," Vedant told the loader.

The hospital visitor set his feet apart and put both hands under the rail. His stitched forearm shook. The tipped cart rose just enough for daylight to show beneath the shaft.

"Nikaalo."

Vedant and the driver reached for the loader's shoulders.

The broad man jerked the rail towards his side of the crossing.

"Hamaara maal—"

The wooden chock cracked.

The axle turned inside the root's loosening coil. A sideboard sprang free, swinging down towards the loader's head with one iron corner leading.

Vedant lifted his free hand from the basin.

The gathered water rose in a shallow curve. It met the board across its broad face, not at the corner. Water flattened, sprayed. The board turned enough to strike Vedant's forearm instead of the man beneath it.

He grunted. Kept the curve there.

"Ab!"

The driver pulled. The visitor lifted again. Blood showed along the stitches above his rolled cuff.

The loader's leg came clear.

Vedant let the water fall. The board struck the road. A sack rolled off the cart and split into the wet flour already pasted across the gutter.

For one breath all the shouting stopped.

The loader was crying. The driver knelt behind him, keeping his shoulders raised away from the wheels. The woman took her hand off the tree. The root stopped moving; it did not sink tidily back into the paving it had broken.

Then the broad man shoved the visitor away from the rail.

The first constable stepped between them again, this time with two men from the delivery truck beside him. The second had a chair from the seed shop. They brought it under the loader rather than trying to make him stand; the driver folded a sack under the injured leg and stayed crouched beside it.

A siren sounded from the main road.

"Sab peechhe!" the constable shouted. "Ab koi gaadi haath nahi lagaayega!"

The broad man backed towards his companions, the pale edge still raised. The woman remained beside the tree. Neither had a clear path to the flour. Between them lay the broken cart and a strip of water turning grey.

Vedant stepped out of it. His forearm had begun to swell where the iron corner had struck. The basin rattled against his knee when he picked it up.

The visitor looked at the blood crossing his wrist.

"Yeh rasoi jaa raha tha?" he asked the driver.

"Parchi teen baar dikhaayi," the driver said.

No one asked to see it a fourth time.

The police vehicle reached the turning. One constable directed it across the junction to hold the two groups apart; the other went with the loader towards the hospital. The injured visitor followed. His companions called after him. He did not turn until the chair was clear of the broken paving.

On the carrier's bed, the sacks which had not been taken down remained tied in place.

They would have to wait for another road.

---

By the time Vedant returned upstairs, the four had been taken back inside. Veer's morning assessment was not finished; the nurse had put him on an observation bed with the injured leg raised.

A porter had met the loader at the entrance with a trolley. The man from the visitors' seats was at another treatment desk, his reopened wound wrapped in fresh gauze. The constable had stayed to take accounts after the injured were examined.

"Dono ek hi gate se aaye," Veer's mother said.

She was speaking to her son, but Aadhya heard from the next bed. The curtains had been drawn back between them while the nurse checked their dressings.

"Unhone kya kaha?" Veer asked.

"Abhi kuch nahi. Ilaaj ho raha hai."

He nodded. The cloth his mother had tucked under the sling had worked slightly sideways. He asked her to straighten it rather than reaching across the injured arm.

Vedant came in with Sitara beside him. A cold pack lay against his forearm.

Rudra looked from it to the basin hanging from his other hand. Dried flour marked the rim.

"Aapka?"

"Laga hai. Dekh liya doctor ne. Ungliyaan chal rahi hain. Thanda rakhoon, phir dobara dekhenge."

Sitara took the basin before he could find a place to balance it. She did not take away the cold pack.

Vedant gave them the junction in order: the goods carrier stopped on its way to the kitchen, sacks being taken onto the cart, the root round the axle. The driver and loader had both said the flour was for the relief meals. The two groups disputed who had the right to move it. Neither had shown him an order from the kitchen.

"Unko rok diya?" Rudra asked.

"Us chauraha pe police hai. Log nikal rahe hain. Tooti zameen pe gaadi nahi jaayegi abhi."

"Aur woh log?"

"Wahin. Apni-apni taraf."

Outside the window an ambulance crossed the ramp. Its siren gave one short warning to a reversing car, then stopped.

Rudra listened until it had passed.

There was no lever at that crossing which could be broken and make this end. The men guarding the road called the woman a thief. The woman called the sacks stolen. Neither accusation had lifted the cart off the loader.

He looked at the cold pack. The swelling beneath it was the part of the answer Vedant had brought back on his own body.

"Maal kisne diya tha?" Ira asked from the shaded bed.

Vedant had the driver's number. The kitchen would check its purchase and donation entries against the load. The police had asked the same question.

"Dono slips ke saath rasoi ki parchi ki copy bhi dena," she said. "Sirf unka daava pahuncha toh baad mein wahi sach bana denge."

Sitara found a loose sheet in her bag. Ira dictated the two route names, the assault on the first driver, and which accounts still needed checking. She left the exact ownership of the flour blank. Beside it she asked Sitara to write *verification pending*.

Her fingers had begun trembling against the coverlet. She rested one hand over the other and closed the uncovered eye while Sitara finished the line.

"Bas?" Sitara asked.

Ira listened to the short list read back.

"Haan. Ab bas."

The sheet went into Vedant's bag. It was enough for the next call. No one asked her to open the register and turn a morning's injury into a finished history.

---

Aadhya had been staring at the door.

"Woh jad rok sakti thi," she said.

Vedant put the cold pack down long enough to adjust the cloth beneath it.

"Usne roki. Der se. Phir doosre ne cart kheencha."

Aadhya looked at her numb fingers.

She remembered the care with which the elder had put three paper twists of seed before her. Mustard. Rice. White peas. The lesson had begun with looking at what was already alive.

At the crossing a woman had put her hand against a tree and kept pulling after a man cried out.

For a few seconds Aadhya could not make those two hands belong in the same thought.

Veer shifted on the next bed. His mother's hand stayed near his supported knee until he settled.

"Hum bhi toh wahi hain," he said.

Aadhya raised her head.

He looked towards his sling, then at her pillow.

"Unke apne. Tumhare apne. Bol aise rahe the jaise humne pehle hi haan kar di."

"Maine nahi ki."

"Maine bhi nahi."

Neither smiled at the agreement.

Rudra watched them across the small distance between the beds. At the ghat, that distance had been crossed with a warning, a hand, the turn of a shield. Somebody outside was trying to stretch it across a street and put a different duty on either side.

He wanted to say they would not let it happen. The visitor had not needed permission to say *your people*. Veer had refused him, and the flour still lay in the gutter.

"Jo bola hai, woh ghar pe bhi bata do," Rudra said. "Kisi aur se na sunein."

Veer's mother was already reaching for her phone.

---

The porter took Rudra past his mother's bay on the way back to his own observation space. Sitara walked beside the chair. He asked her to stop at the open curtain.

Rukmini had heard there was trouble on the road. Dadi had not tried to hide the sound of the trolley or the constable asking directions.

Her good hand rested across the wrist of her supported arm. As Rudra came into view, two fingers moved once at the pulse, then lay still.

He showed her the raised foot without trying to stand.

"Yahin tha," he said. "Andar."

She looked at Sitara, who nodded.

Rukmini's hand settled. The left shoulder remained exactly where the nurses had supported it. Dadi drew the sheet clear of the shoulder dressing and sat down again.

Rudra let the porter take him on.

He did not need his mother to thank him for staying in his bed.

---

Dhruv came before noon.

He had gone back to the ghat after sleeping, checked the relief watch and then followed the account of the grain-market fighting to the hospital. The angle-ring stayed in its pouch. A clean dressing crossed his palm; he used the other hand to bring the chair close to Rudra's bed.

"Neeche?" Rudra asked.

"Jo band hua tha, band hai. Gharon mein abhi bhi kisi ko nahi jaane de rahe."

The broken device had not come back to life. The people who had lost their rooms had not got them back either.

Dhruv had seen them leaving the school in pairs to find water, a charge for a phone, news of a missing relative. They had begun asking at the relief post which lanes they were allowed to use.

"Kaun rok raha hai?" Rudra asked.

"Jis mod pe jo khada hai. Naam alag bata rahe hain. Hamaare watch waale har jagah nahi hain. Police ko jagah bheji hai."

Some of the people calling themselves guards had helped carry stretchers during the siege. Dhruv knew two by face. He had told them to move their barricade. They had moved it far enough for him, then drawn it back across the lane behind him.

"Aapko jaane diya," Rudra said.

"Mujhe. Gaadi ko nahi."

Dhruv laid his dressed hand on his knee. The ring made no sound inside its pouch.

Beyond the curtain, a nurse was explaining to a frightened family that no one would be transferred merely because a visitor had offered another vehicle. The treating doctor would decide whether a patient could move. The family could ask the doctor themselves.

Rudra listened to the relief in the woman's answer.

It lasted until she asked how the rest of them were supposed to get home.

---

The school had used its dry stores. The kitchen's first returned meal had been served to people waiting at the hospital before it spoiled. A volunteer was checking another approach on foot before a fresh load went out.

These small pieces of news reached the bay through Vedant. He made the calls sitting down, the cold pack balanced on a folded towel. When he needed to read a number from the sheet, Sitara held it at an angle which did not force his bruised arm to turn.

Rudra watched the two of them work without calling it a plan for the whole city.

There was a flour load to establish ownership for. A school to feed. A junction the police had partly secured but which still could not take traffic. Beyond it were roads none of them had seen that morning.

"Aap sabko bol sakte hain na," he asked Dhruv, "hospital aane waalon ko mat roko?"

"Bol raha hoon. Aur bolenge. Sunenge ya nahi, uske liye log chahiye. Sirf naam nahi."

Rudra looked down at his hands.

The wrist support held its shape when he relaxed. His knuckles still pulled beneath the dressings. The seam at his neck caught against the clean collar if he turned too quickly.

For a little while at Manikarnika, he had been able to stand inside a force great enough to break the frame. He remembered it with a frightening clarity. He also remembered needing someone else to press a dressing against his neck afterwards.

He had not learnt how to call that force again. Even if he had, he could not stand at every turning in Varanasi.

The thought did not make the people waiting at those turnings less real.

"Jo raste check ho gaye," he said, "unke naam desk pe de do. Time ke saath. Puraana jawaab aage na bhejte rahein."

Dhruv nodded. He would give them what his watch had actually checked, and the number for a fresh call before any vehicle left.

He stood, leaving the chair close enough for the next person who came to sit.

---

The nurse brought Rudra his prescribed medicines and waited while he took them. Then she lowered the light on his side and told him to rest.

From the adjoining bay came Veer's voice, low on a family call. He was repeating the words *maine nahi kaha*. Aadhya had asked Sitara to reach her mother directly. Ira was asleep, the loose sheet already put away.

Rudra lay back.

Outside, a porter argued gently with a wheel which wanted to turn left. The hospital's lunch trolley went past. Somewhere behind the next wall, somebody who had been silent all morning began asking for tea.

He shut his eyes and let the ordinary sounds remain ordinary.

Then the phone at the corridor desk rang.

The nurse answered. Said the hospital's name. Listened.

"Kaunsi gaadi?"

A pause.

She called the duty doctor, then asked someone to find the constable still taking statements downstairs. Dhruv's voice answered from farther along the passage.

Rudra opened his eyes.

The ambulance had gone to the school for an injured man whose breathing had worsened. It had avoided the grain-market crossing. The northern approach had been clear when the driver checked. Now someone had pulled a barrier across it.

The nurse put the call on speaker for the doctor.

"Peechhe patient hai," the driver was saying. "Hospital ko baat kara raha hoon. Doctor hain."

A fist struck the ambulance's body. Not the quick knock of a family member telling a driver to go.

"Naam bata diya," the driver said to the desk. "Maan nahi rahe."

The doctor moved nearer the phone.

"Phone dijiye. Main baat karta hoon."

Another voice came through, close now, almost courteous.

"Doctor-saab, naam nahi poochh rahe. Patient kis Kul ka hai?"

# CHAPTER SIXTY-TWO — THE PATIENT IN THE BACK

---

> *"They called it the road of no Kul after the first ambulance got through.*
> *The man in the back remembered only the ceiling."*
>
> *— ambulance dutybook, account added after the northern closures*

---

The doctor asked the man at the barrier to give the phone back to the driver.

"Pehle aap bataaiye—"

"Mujhe patient ki saans sunni hai. Driver ko dijiye."

His voice did not rise. Beside the desk, the nurse had already written the location on a slip and put it in Dhruv's uninjured hand.

A scrape sounded through the speaker. The ambulance driver returned, breathing hard with anger rather than effort.

"Doctor-saab."

"Medic paas hain? Unko dijiye. Gaadi se patient ko abhi utaarna nahi."

The next voice was a woman's. She gave the patient's condition, the oxygen she was using, the change since leaving the school. The doctor listened. Asked two short questions. Told her what he wanted checked while they waited for a way through.

Rudra could not see the numbers she read from the monitor. He could hear somebody behind her trying to finish a sentence on too little air.

Then another woman said, "Bhaiya, inko pahunchaa do. Jo naam likhna hai likh lo."

The driver moved the phone closer to the medic. The plea went on, quieter now, beyond her account.

Rudra turned his head towards the curtain.

The seam caught against his collar.

---

The constable came up from the statement desk with his notebook still open. Dhruv gave him the location. He read it back, called the vehicle outside, and went down before the call ended.

Vedant rose from the chair beside the bay. The cold pack slid off its towel. He caught it against his thigh without closing the bruised arm round it.

"Tum raho," Dhruv said.

"Chal sakta hoon."

"Pata hai. Hospital waalon ko yahan bhi ek aadmi chahiye jo doosri taraf ki khabar sun chuka ho. Main pahunchkar phone karta hoon."

Vedant looked towards the desk. The nurse was asking that the main gate remain clear for the returning ambulance, not filled by people going out to watch.

He sat again.

Sitara stood at the open end of the bay until Dhruv saw her. She nodded once: the patients would not be left without someone they knew.

He checked the knot of the pouch at his waist. The angle-ring stayed inside it. Beneath the fresh dressing, his palm had begun to ache simply from the morning's walking and questions.

At the stairs he paused for a porter bringing a cylinder up. The porter thanked him, scarcely looking at his face. Dhruv waited for the load to cross the landing, then went down.

---

Veer had heard the call from the next bay.

"Kis taraf ke hain?" he asked when Vedant came back within hearing.

"Barrier waale? Abhi unse sunenge."

"Patient."

Vedant looked at him.

Veer's face changed before he answered his own question.

"Nahi. Mera matlab—"

"Pata hai," Vedant said.

He put the cold pack back over the towel. Veer stared at the ceiling for a moment, then turned his good hand palm-up on the sheet.

"Bas wahi poochh diya."

Nobody reassured him that a habit could not enter through words used by someone he hated. Aadhya, sitting across the bay while her hands were checked, asked whether the family had somebody at the school who could meet the ambulance if it had to reverse.

Vedant called the volunteer there. They needed the road behind the vehicle kept clear as well as the road in front. The school sent two people out to turn approaching handcarts back before they joined the queue.

It was a small useful task, finished before any of them had found something brave to say.

---

Outside, the afternoon had the flat white glare which makes every shutter seem hotter than the wall around it.

Dhruv followed the constable past the pharmacy and along the northern approach. The police vehicle had gone ahead by the wider road. He could hear its siren starting and stopping beyond the next row of houses.

At the tea stall a man pointed without being asked. A woman coming the other way held a schoolbag against her chest and said the van was still there.

"Aage mat jaiye," the constable told her.

"Mere pati hain usmein. Mujhe bahar bhej diya."

He stopped. Asked her name and whether she had come with the patient.

She had. The medic had needed space; men at the barrier had begun asking her questions through the open door. She had stepped down to answer them, thinking that would make the delay shorter.

Now she was on the wrong side of the crowd.

Dhruv turned his body to give her room beside him.

"Hum gaadi tak jaa rahe hain. Aap saath rahiye. Jahan bolenge wahin rukna, theek?"

She nodded, still clutching the schoolbag.

"Kapde hain," she said, seeing his glance. "Do jode."

He did not tell her she would not need them.

---

The ambulance stood behind a low farm trailer slewed across the lane. Its towbar pointed into a brick stack. A rope ran from its other end to the iron post outside a closed shop.

Enough room for a person on either side. Not enough for the van.

Three men stood by the rope. Behind the ambulance, a motorcycle and two carts had become a second obstruction. The driver had left a few feet between his bumper and the trailer, as though the small visible distance could persuade someone to clear it.

A young man at the post was writing on a board balanced against his stomach.

Across its top, in thick blue letters, was the word **PROTECTION**.

---

The constable went to the post first.

"Kiski gaadi hai? Hataao. Ambulance nikaalni hai."

The young man lowered his board. Beside him, an older man put a folded towel on the head of a boy sitting in the shade. The boy's nose was bleeding. He wore the same narrow cloth band round his upper arm as the men beside the rope.

"Hamaara bhi aadmi ghayal hai," the older man said.

"Usko bhi bhejo. Pehle rasta khol."

"Udhar Prakriti waale gaadi rok ke baithe hain. Unko khulwaaiye pehle. Rakshak ke aadmi yahan se hat gaye toh yeh poori line unki."

The constable gave the ambulance one look, took his radio out and repeated the request for the vehicle to come to the post, not stop at the wider junction.

Dhruv moved along the van's side with the patient's wife. He did not ask her to argue for the road.

The driver opened the sliding side door far enough to let him hear the medic. The air from inside smelled of vinyl, sweat and the sharp clean edge of spirit.

"Kitna time?" she asked.

"Police aa rahi hai. Main dekh raha hoon."

"Mujhe andaaza nahi chahiye. Agar gaadi nahi niklegi toh doosra tareeka dekhiye. Inko pahunchna hai."

Dhruv nodded. She let the wife speak to her husband for a few seconds, from the step, without climbing across the equipment.

"Aa rahe hain," the woman said. "Hum aa rahe hain."

The patient could not turn towards her. His eyes found the roof light, lost it, found it again.

---

At the trailer, the constable had untied the rope from the post.

One of the men caught the free end. A pale curve flashed above his wrist and stood between the constable and the trailer's handle.

"Haath peechhe," the constable said.

The curve held.

A phone rose above the crowd. Another followed it. The man with the towel looked towards the lenses, then pushed one down. Its owner recoiled; the screen struck the closed shop's shutter and cracked.

The sound brought more people to their doors.

Dhruv looked past the post. The trailer was loaded with paving blocks. Without it, the lane was wide enough. Beside it, the gap narrowed at a jutting stone doorstep, not much wider than his shoulders.

He could send a person through there. Not a stretcher kept level.

The constable spoke into his radio again. No flourish with the lathi this time. His free hand held the phones and the shield-man apart.

Behind the ambulance, a cart owner began backing his empty vehicle. The school volunteers had reached the rear of the queue. They turned the motorcycle sideways into a doorway and made another space behind it.

Dhruv went back along the van, looking at the buildings rather than the men.

"Us taraf se?" he asked the wife.

He pointed to the narrow iron gate beside the shut shop.

"Kapde dhote hain andar," she said. "Peechhe gali hai. School se aate the hum. Ab gate band hai."

"Kiske paas chaabi?"

She named the woman who lived upstairs. Someone in the watching crowd called up to her before Dhruv could ask.

A window opened. A grey head appeared above the clothesline.

"Kya?"

"Dono gate kholna hai! Mareez hai!"

The woman looked down at the trailer, at the ambulance, then at the man whose pale curve still held a policeman away from a public road.

"Aa rahi hoon," she said.

---

The medic came down to inspect the passage as soon as the first gate opened. The driver stayed with the patient. Dhruv and the woman led her through a washing courtyard striped with wet sheets.

A second iron gate stood at the far end. Beyond it, a service alley ran past the back of the pharmacy towards the hospital. The building owner unlocked it. She took two buckets out of the path, then went back for the clothesline which sagged across the centre.

"Yeh utaar do," the medic said.

"Saaf kapde hain. Upar kar dete—"

"Sir pe lagega. Poora utaariye."

The woman unknotted it without another word. Wet cotton settled in a heap on her verandah, touching the floor she had just washed.

Dhruv walked the route again, this time looking at the thresholds. At the back gate a broken drain cover left one dark opening along the side. He pointed it out to the medic. She measured the usable strip with her eyes and asked whether the hospital could meet them at the alley mouth with a trolley.

He called Vedant. Gave the exact gate and the drain, not merely the name of the road.

"Wheels ke liye nahi hai," he said. "Stretcher uthaake. Seedha."

The medic made the decision. They would transfer with the portable oxygen and a carrying team, if the hospital sent bearers to the back entrance. Not the wife, not a few excited men grabbing whichever end they reached first.

Dhruv relayed that too.

Outside the first gate the man with the board had stopped writing.

"Yeh kiska ghar hai?" he asked.

The woman came back from dropping the clothesline.

"Mera."

"Aise khol dogi toh sab aayenge."

She took the empty buckets off the step so nobody could trip on them.

"Abhi ek aayega," she said. "Usko jagah do."

From the ambulance came three quick taps. The driver wanted the medic back inside.

She went at once.

Dhruv followed her as far as the open door. The wife had stopped repeating that they were coming. Both her hands were pressed flat against the outside of the van.

---

The porters arrived through the back alley with a carrying stretcher and another attendant. They left the trolley on the hospital side of the broken cover. Dhruv went ahead of them, pointing out the opening before anyone could step beside it with a load.

At the van, the medic told the wife to wait inside the courtyard.

"Main saath—"

"Aap saath hi hain. Bas abhi haath lagaane ki jagah chahiye."

The woman went through the gate. The building owner took her schoolbag before its strap could catch the latch.

They brought the ambulance stretcher level with the carrying one. The medic counted for the transfer. Staff hands moved together. The patient's mouth opened; no full cry came out. She checked the oxygen line and put two fingers where she wanted the leading bearers to slow.

Dhruv stood back. The lifting was not his work to improve by adding an injured hand.

The man with the pale curve left the rope.

"Ruko."

The constable moved across his path.

"Mareez jaayega. Tum yahin raho."

"Yeh doosra raasta kisne khola?"

The rear bearers had already turned towards the courtyard. One had to take a half-step back to keep the stretcher level through the first gate.

The man lunged for the iron leaf.

Dhruv caught it with his left shoulder before it struck the bearer. His dressed palm hit the latch plate. Pain shot across the old rope burns.

He did not reach for the pouch.

"Andar," he said to the carrying team.

The constable caught the man's elbow. The pale curve scraped the gate, taking a thin white shaving off the paint. A second man came forward from the rope.

The building owner seized the inside handle with both hands.

"Mera gate chhod!"

A neighbour reached her from the verandah. Together they pulled the leaf towards the wall, not shut across the patient. Dhruv got his shoulder out of the pinch and stood between the hinge and the passing stretcher.

At the street's end the police vehicle turned in. Its front wheel mounted the edge of the paving to get round the waiting carts. Two more constables got out before the driver had straightened it.

The men by the post stopped advancing.

The stretcher crossed the first threshold.

---

The courtyard was not long. Under a load, every object in it acquired an edge.

A bucket hook at the height of a sleeve. A patch of soap-water near the tap. The bent foot of a washing stand. Dhruv named them as the bearers approached. The neighbour moved the stand against the wall; the building owner kicked a folded sack over the wet patch and warned the men not to put a heel on its loose end.

The medic walked at the patient's head. She did not look back at the shouting.

"Seedha. Ab dheere."

The wife went behind the oxygen, close enough to hear her husband breathe. She was no longer promising him arrival. She named the things passing above him instead.

"Kapde hain upar. Ab gate. Roshni aayegi. Aankh band kar lo."

At the rear threshold the leading porter stopped.

The drain opening lay exactly where Dhruv had said. The route had space for the stretcher only if its left edge stayed close to the gatepost. He put his uninjured hand flat against that post.

"Meri taraf. Thoda. Bas."

The porter placed his foot on sound stone. The man behind him waited until the weight settled before stepping through. Cloth whispered against brick. The oxygen cylinder cleared the latch by the width of two fingers.

Then they were in the alley.

The trolley waited at the far end. A nurse had come with it. The medic handed over while the porters made the next transfer, giving the delay along with the condition in which the man had left the school.

The wife tried to follow when the trolley began moving. The courtyard owner had come after them with her schoolbag.

Dhruv took it with his left and held it where she could reach.

She caught the strap. Went after the trolley.

There had been no moment in which the patient rose and thanked the people who had carried him. The doors took him while his wife was still walking quickly enough to lose a sandal.

A porter brought it back from the ramp.

---

By the time Dhruv returned to the first gate, the shield-man had been put beside the police vehicle. His pale edge was gone. One constable watched his hands while another asked the shopkeeper to describe what had happened.

The older man still held the towel against the boy's bleeding nose. He looked towards Dhruv's dressing, where a dark line had appeared.

"Hamaare ladke ko bhi—"

"Le chalo," Dhruv said. "Yahin hospital hai. Kisi ki parchi nahi chahiye."

The man opened his mouth, then shut it. The boy got up slowly. His cloth band stayed on the arm; no one made him remove it to be examined.

At the iron post the writing board lay face down. The cracked phone's owner stood beside the constable, angry enough for his account to need repeating. The constable let him finish before asking for the sequence again.

The police were bringing a towing vehicle for the loaded trailer. Until then, the opened courtyard could carry people in a single controlled direction, with someone at each gate.

The owner looked at the wet clothes on her floor.

"Mere bachche upar hain," she told the constable. "Aap chale gaye toh?"

He asked his senior to leave a man at the gate. When permission came, he gave her the post number and showed her which vehicle would stay.

Only then did she bend to pick up the clothes.

Dhruv helped with the part he could do one-handed. His palm dressing would need changing. The gate had a bright scrape through its paint. Neither had been the largest possible cost.

That did not make either imaginary.

---

Rudra knew the ambulance patient had arrived because the doctor left the desk in the middle of a sentence.

Not running. Moving with the particular speed which made everybody else get out of the way.

The nurse who remained brought the man's wife to a bench and asked her to wait while they examined him. She had the schoolbag again. One sandal was on crookedly.

Rudra saw her through the opening between the curtain and its rail.

He had once believed the worst part of bringing someone to a hospital was being stopped outside a door. He could picture another door now, in a washing courtyard, which a woman had needed permission from nobody to open and protection from somebody to keep open afterwards.

Vedant came to his bedside.

"Aa gaye. Treatment shuru hai."

"Saans?"

"Oxygen pe hain. Doctor andar hain."

Rudra let the answer be as small as it was. Across the passage the wife folded and unfolded the loose end of her bag's strap.

"Unko pata hai?" he asked.

"Wahi bataaya hai."

The woman looked towards the treatment doors. Rudra looked away before she had to acknowledge another stranger watching her wait.

---

Dhruv came back with his palm redressed and a warning not to lean on it again. The angle-ring had stayed in the pouch throughout. He set the pouch on the chair beside him rather than wearing its cord against the new padding.

Veer studied the thicker dressing.

"Gate ne kaata?"

"Latch ne."

"Complaint gate ko deni padegi. Latch toh employee hai."

Dhruv looked at him until the corner of his mouth moved.

"Tumhara review kitne din chalega?"

"Jab tak discharge na kar dein."

Veer's mother, returning with water, said she could arrange an early end to the commentary without troubling any doctor. He accepted the glass in a chastened silence which lasted until she had turned away.

Aadhya smiled. Then her right hand slipped a little on the pillow, and she had to ask for it to be placed back properly. The numb fingers had not noticed the edge.

The smile faded while she watched the nurse protect them from a pressure she could not feel.

"Kal bhi yeh covered rahega?" she asked.

"Doctor dekhenge. Feeling aa jaaye tab bhi jaldi kaam mat shuru kar dena."

Her mother was coming for the evening review. Aadhya had asked for an ordinary change of clothes, loose sleeves, nothing which had to be pulled hard over the hands.

Not a uniform. Not clothes in which somebody could photograph a champion leaving for her own territory.

She asked Vedant to tell the desk that only her family should be told where she would rest after the doctor permitted her to leave. Anyone claiming to have sent a vehicle could wait outside.

He took the request to the nurse rather than promising to guard every gate himself.

---

The recorder-lady arrived near the end of the afternoon.

Ira heard her before opening her eye: the familiar small clearing of the throat, never quite a cough, with which she had announced herself at a desk when she thought its occupant had been sitting too long.

"Kya laayi?" Ira asked.

"Khaana."

"Aur?"

"Chamach."

Ira opened the uncovered eye. The older woman stood between her and the strongest light until Sitara adjusted the curtain.

"Kaam nahi laayi?"

"Tumhe dekhne aayi hoon. Office kholne nahi."

The food went on the little table. Sitara had already checked what Ira could eat. The recorder-lady placed the cup on her right, described where it stood, and waited for her to find it herself.

Ira's fingers trembled round the handle. She put the cup down before lifting it.

The older woman slid a folded towel beneath it so it would not scrape away while Ira changed her grip.

"Main de doon?"

"Nahi. Bas paas rakhna."

"Yahin hoon."

For a while their conversation concerned the thickness of dal. Too thick to drink, too thin to accuse anyone of having cooked a proper meal. Ira argued that the hospital had probably commissioned it to prevent patients from staying for the food. The recorder-lady told her to complain softly; the cook might take a complaint as an invitation to add more water.

Ira laughed once. It hurt behind the dressing. She let the older woman move the bowl closer and stopped trying to turn her head towards every sound.

Rudra heard none of the joke clearly from his bed. He heard the change in her voice.

There had been a chair at Rajghat which she had chosen to sit in. There were people who came when she was hurt. No one at a checkpoint had the authority to make those things temporary.

---

The school called before evening.

A small delivery had reached it by the checked approach, with a police escort for the last stretch. Not the full flour consignment. Enough food for the people who had begun stretching their dry stores into another meal.

Vedant gave that news to the group without calling the roads safe.

The northern trailer had been moved. A constable stayed at the courtyard gate until the owner could secure it for the night. Elsewhere, another driver had been turned away from a lane whose entrance had been open an hour before.

Rudra asked for a fresh update to go to the critical-care desk as well. His father did not need to be moved; the people going home after duty did.

He was thinking of the nurse who had waited while he cleaned his hands before a visit. She would have to leave this building eventually. So would the man who had taken Ram's chest image. So would the porter still bringing cylinders up a stair.

The hospital was not an island merely because he had learned to remain inside it.

---

The man with the reopened stitches returned to the visitors' seats after his statement.

He did not ask for Veer to be brought out again. He gave the guard a folded receipt and asked that Vedant look at it.

"Flour waale ka hai," he said. "Jo gaadi mein bacha hai, woh bhi chala gaya toh kal mujhe jawaab dena padega."

Vedant met him at the recess, leaving the bays quiet. Sitara stayed where she could hear the patients if they called. The recorder-lady came to the clinical door with Ira's permission to hear anything concerning the kitchen records.

The receipt had been kept in a plastic sleeve. Its corners were worn, but the amount and the words *paid in full* were clear. There was a dispatch number beneath them and a promised collection at the grain-market warehouse.

"Kisne paise diye?" Vedant asked.

"Humne jama kiye. Chaar ghar se. Rasad ke liye. Hamaare log hain school mein bhi."

"Naam ki jagah Kul kyun poochh rahe the phir?"

The man looked towards his freshly wrapped forearm.

"Pehle chori hui thi. Bola tha, maal uth gaya. Doosri taraf pahunchaa diya."

"Kisne bola?"

"Jahan se khareeda."

He tapped the seller's line on the receipt.

Vedant asked the constable to hear the account. A piece of paper presented after an assault could be evidence of a transaction; it could not turn the assault into an official duty.

The constable wrote the man's statement in his own notebook and asked him to identify the men who had stood at the cart. The visitor named two. When asked who had struck the first relief driver, he said he had not seen that blow.

His account stopped there. Nobody filled the gap for him.

---

At the clinical door the recorder-lady asked whether the school kitchen had kept its purchase note.

Vedant showed her the copy sent by the cook. The original was with the driver, who had been told to keep it dry. The surviving flour sacks were still awaiting a documented move from the carrier; police would not let another group simply take them while arguing about yesterday's losses.

She studied the note without announcing a verdict.

"Transport waale ki kitaab dekhni padegi. Aur bechne waale ki. Yeh alag kaagaz hain."

Ira called softly from the bay. The older woman went back at once.

"Kya hua?"

"Aawaaz aa rahi thi."

"Isliye khadi thi darwaaze pe. Tumhe uthna na pade."

Ira asked what the receipt actually said. The recorder-lady read only the relevant lines. No one put the small print in front of an injured eye and asked for a miracle.

"Maal diya, yeh likha hai?" Ira asked.

"Abhi is copy mein paise liye likha hai. Dispatch number bhi hai."

"Toh number ka matlab poochhna. Apne aap gaadi mat maan lena."

The older woman looked at her over the paper.

"Wahi karungi."

Ira lay back. Her right eye shut before she finished asking when the doctor would come again.

"Shaam ka review abhi hai," Sitara said. "Kal ka kal dekhenge."

---

Aadhya's mother brought the loose-sleeved clothes. She also brought a comb and asked which way her daughter wanted her hair tied.

Aadhya said the usual way.

Halfway through, she asked for it to be looser.

Her mother undid the tie without saying it had always been comfortable before. The hands which normally did it were lying on the pillow between them.

The doctor examined those hands again that evening. There was no sudden return of sensation. He described how the dressings would be managed outside observation if the next check allowed it, what help she would need, when to return. Permission to rest elsewhere would not mean permission to put the injured fingers to work.

Ira's eye team would examine her again in the morning. Rudra still had no weight-bearing clearance. Veer was to leave only with family help after his remaining checks, not walk out because a better rumour was waiting outside.

The ordinary future returned in pieces: a place to sleep, a lift to arrange, clothes with sleeves which would pass over a dressing.

Rudra heard his mother's bay settling for the evening. Ram remained in critical care. The new patient from the school was still being treated; his wife had been given an update and somewhere to sit.

A whole day had passed without making either man's next day certain.

---

Just before the visiting queue thinned, the guard brought another envelope to Vedant.

A woman from the seed-shop group had left it. The recorder-lady opened it with him at the visitors' recess, beside the constable who had returned for the first receipt's details.

Inside was a second paid claim for flour.

Different purchaser. Another collection amount. The same warehouse seller's stamp.

Vedant put the plastic-sleeved receipt beside it.

The recorder-lady ran one finger down each page, stopping below the words *paid in full*.

"Number ek hai," she said.

The constable turned his notebook back to the visitor's account.

From inside the bay came the small sound of Ira asking for the light to be lowered. The older woman waited for Sitara to answer, then looked again at the two receipts.

Both sides were claiming to have paid for the same promised flour.

Only one load had reached the crossing.
