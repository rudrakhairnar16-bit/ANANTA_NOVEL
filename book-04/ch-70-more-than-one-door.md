# CHAPTER SEVENTY — MORE THAN ONE DOOR

---

> *"They closed the gate and reported the distribution stopped.*
> *The second kitchen had already lit its stove."*

> *— supply inquiry, statement omitted from the first summary*

---

The woman at the hall held up the false cancellation and asked the receiving worker to read the name beneath it.

Veer Dikshit.

He had been at the post with his knee raised when somebody used that name to close a meal service he had never controlled.

The worker kept the paper instead of tearing it. He called the known post number, not the contact printed beneath the private watch's mark. In the doorway, the first family had already tied its bedding into a bundle.

"Abhi phone pe baat ho rahi hai," he told them.

"Toh khaana hai ya nahi?" the father asked.

The worker could not answer a question about breakfast by showing him a safer telephone number.

---

The organizer told the hall exactly what had changed.

The large private pledge had been withdrawn after its new conditions were refused. The hall had not been closed. Food already there would be served; the next meal would have to be smaller unless the calls for other stock succeeded. Nobody could honestly promise the old quantity tonight.

The receiving worker repeated the message with the cook beside him.

A woman began to cry from anger rather than surprise. She had kept her children there because the board had said morning meal. The board could be corrected in a minute. Her decision to stay had already been made with a hungry child asleep across her lap.

The worker let her finish.

Then he asked the cook to show what was actually available and who needed to be told first if another supply failed.

---

At the post, Vedant asked Veer whether he could give a short correction from the bed. Not a speech about the war, and not a promise to feed everyone himself.

The clinician agreed to that brief call, then rest. The volunteer held the phone where Veer would not pull at his shoulder.

"Maine woh notice nahi diya," Veer said. "Mera naam bina poochhe likha hai. Hall band nahi kiya. Khaane ki kami hai, uski baat organizer se seedha poochho. Private watch mein naam dena yahaan rehne ki shart nahi hai."

The hall's worker knew his voice from the earlier call. He let the families hear, then asked the organizer to repeat the practical arrangement. Veer's fame could correct a claim about Veer. It could not turn into the hall's only proof that its own workers were telling the truth.

A copy of his brief denial went with the preserved paper to the officer. The post and the hall put up their actual contacts together. Volunteers went to the places where the false notice had been seen, removing only those papers and speaking to the people still waiting there.

They could not call back everyone who had already left.

---

The family with the tied bedding chose to go to a relative on a checked road.

The hall worker helped arrange the departure. He did not make staying a vote for the shared kitchen.

Others stayed. The woman with the red-looped key took her father's bowl back and found the food cold. Her relative warmed it with the cook's help. Her son wanted to know why the man at the door had said they would have to leave again.

"Galat likh ke gaya tha," she told him.

"Phir aayega?"

She could not answer that from a board either.

She showed him the receiving worker and the woman at the stove. If anybody told him to leave, he was to come to one of them or to her. He was not to follow a stranger who said her name.

Then she sat down and ate the portion she had been saving for later. It would not become two portions by making her too faint to carry him.

---

Before the lamps were lowered, the organizer confirmed the first purchases with the district contact and the suppliers willing to open early.

The emergency money would buy less than the lost pledge. Some stock was farther away than the vehicles could fetch before breakfast. The smaller driver could make a run, but not carry the whole gap. Two local kitchens offered part of what they had set aside for their own next day; that debt would have to be replaced, not forgotten under thanks.

The cook changed the first meal. She kept the portions needed for patients separate and wrote what still had to arrive before anyone could claim the second meal was secure.

At the upper-market yard, the dealer brought his opening worker to meet the public contact and the constable assigned to the morning approach. They checked the gate, the turning space and the covered strip beside the wall. The dealer locked his commercial store separately.

The porters would meet the first small load there at dawn. Another cart would go straight to the municipal hall, never through the yard.

For the first time since the crossing broke, the next morning did not depend on everybody reaching the same entrance.

---

Dawn found the smaller vehicle outside a wholesaler's shutter, waiting for the man with the keys.

The driver from the post had finished his night run. He drank tea standing beside the bonnet while a kitchen worker checked the purchase and the available space. The porters had already refused one cracked plank laid across the vehicle's bed. A sound replacement had to be found before the load went on.

They were late before they moved.

The wholesaler opened only the part of the order he could honestly sell. Another buyer's paid stock remained behind its own marking. The emergency money did not make it unclaimed.

The cook's worker called the hall with the revised arrival. The people there began the smaller meal from what had come the night before. They would add what they could when it arrived, not keep children watching a cold pot while an absent truck became a promise again.

A second cart turned off for the hall before the smaller vehicle took the upper-market road.

The driver saw it pass and sounded the horn once. The cartman raised a hand without stopping to make a procession of it.

---

At the dealer's yard, both front leaves opened inward onto the clear turning space. The covered stock strip lay to the right. The scale stood under the left roof, clear of the vehicle's wheels. At the back, the dealer's own commercial store remained locked.

A narrow side door through the left wall opened into the next lane. People could use it. A laden cart could not.

The opening worker kept that door clear while the first bags were lowered onto the dry pallets. The dealer counted with the public volunteer instead of leaving his entire yard to a stranger's receipt.

One porter wrapped cloth round a rubbed place on his wrist before taking the next sack. A shallow pale arc showed beside the cloth when the rough binding slipped. He let it vanish once the other porter had taken the bag's lower end.

"Bandh lo pehle," the other man said.

He did. The little edge had spared the binding from slipping across his skin; it had not made the weight his to carry alone.

The constable stood at the approach, keeping the unloading path separate from people coming for the first dry portions. The gate had not become a public invitation to push past a reversing vehicle.

A woman waiting with her vessel asked whether this was the same watch as on last night's paper.

The opening worker showed her the actual notice and the public contact. She chose a place out of the path and sat down to wait.

---

The private watch arrived before the scale had finished its first batch.

Three men stopped beside the front entrance. One carried folded armbands. The man ahead of him asked the dealer why grain was being issued there when the protected collection was at the post.

"Yeh mera yard hai," the dealer said. "Relief ka hissa alag rakha hai. Police ko pata hai."

"Humko kyun nahi bataya?"

The constable stepped closer.

The man lowered his voice, as though he were offering the dealer a way to end an embarrassing misunderstanding.

"Subah-subah tamaasha mat karao. Board humara laga do. Kholte raho. Koi rok nahi raha."

He put an armband on the scale table.

The dealer left it there.

He had known there might be a price for opening. Knowing did not stop his eyes moving towards the locked commercial store when the man asked where next week's supply would come from.

The kitchen worker heard the question.

"Unke stock ko beech mein mat lao. Humne jo liya uska hisaab yahaan hai."

The man did not ask to see it.

"Hisaab kaagaz pe rakhte rehna. Raste pe kaun leke aayega?"

---

The constable told them to move away from the unloading mouth. Any actual complaint could be given at the public desk. They had not been appointed to supervise the yard or its recipients.

One man moved back into the street. The other two stayed.

The man nearest the tied right-hand gate leaf caught the fastening.

The opening worker put a hand out.

"Chhod do. Gaadi andar hai."

The man pulled the knot loose.

The leaf began moving across the turning space, towards the handcart being loaded beneath the right roof near the entrance. The worker caught its upright before it could gather weight.

The constable reached for the man's arm.

A pale curve rose along the man's wrist between the uniformed hand and the gate.

The constable drew back from its live edge and called for support on the radio. He told the waiting people to move into the clear street, away from the leaf and the vehicle.

The driver stopped the engine.

At the scale, the armband lay beside the unfinished measure. The porter with the wrapped wrist put his end of the sack down before turning towards the gate.

---

The gate struck the worker's shoulder.

He had stopped its first swing with his hand. The man behind it pushed again. Wood drove him sideways against the loaded handcart.

One wheel climbed the edge of the drainage channel at the yard's mouth.

The kitchen worker shouted to leave the sacks and clear the wheel. The porter at the cart shafts caught the load, but not before a lower bag tore against the rough gate bracket. Grain spilled into the channel. Water carried the first handful beneath the vehicle.

The porter let the bag go.

He kept both feet out of the moving wheel and held the shaft while the driver reached him from the cab.

Behind them, the opening worker was still caught between the leaf and the cart's side.

---

The constable's lathi met the pale curve. It slid off with a sharp knock instead of moving the man's wrist away.

The porter with the wrapped arm came in from the leaf's open side.

"Gate chhodo."

The man looked at the cloth round his wrist.

"Apne ho toh—"

"Rakshak hoon. Tera naukar nahi."

A small arc rose beside the porter's forearm.

He did not try to cover the yard with it. He put its face against the other curve and turned his body just enough to take that pressure away from the constable's reach.

The cloth at his wrist darkened. His arm began to shake almost at once.

The constable moved while the angle was open. He caught the gate-pusher's upper arm and pulled him away from the wood. The first of the called support officers came through the approach and took the other side.

The curves broke contact. The gate-pusher lost his footing, and the shape at his wrist wavered and went out.

The porter let his own go before trying to catch anybody. He stepped back, holding his wrist against his stomach.

He had made room for a grip. That was all the room he had.

---

The driver and the other porter brought the handcart level.

The dealer came round behind the leaf. Together they drew the gate away from the man's shoulder and secured it against the wall again.

The worker sat down hard.

His foot had taken a blow near the cart wheel. He could not stand on it without pain. The cook's helper brought a chair through the side door and kept people from gathering around him while medical help was called.

The officer at the street stopped collection at the yard. The group which had arrived with armbands was separated from the waiting families. Two men had been close enough to take part in the struggle; their accounts would not be made into the same account merely because they had come together. The third had stayed back and was asked to remain as a witness.

The driver moved the vehicle only when the unloading mouth was clear and the officer told him where it could wait. It still held stock which had not been weighed out. Leaving it across the gate would not protect that stock or make treatment easier.

The kitchen worker covered the remaining bags.

Nobody scooped the wet grain out of the drain and put it back with food to be served.

---

The yard was closed to collection for the time being.

The dealer heard the words from his own threshold. He looked at the secured leaf, the opened bag and the worker waiting for transport.

"Ab bolo," he said to the organizer when the call connected. "Maine gate diya tha ya jhagda khareeda tha?"

At the post, the organizer listened. Vedant stood beside the table but did not take the phone to tell the man what courage ought to sound like.

The dealer wanted his worker examined, the food loss recorded and the public contact to remain while the unsafe approach was cleared. If the yard opened again, it would not be because a visitor had promised nothing more could happen.

The organizer agreed. He called the hall next.

---

The cart which had turned off before the market was already being unloaded there.

The woman with the red-looped key helped keep children away from its wheels. Her father remained with the relative inside. The cook checked the bags against the call, then added the expected share to the meal she had begun from the smaller stock.

The message from the yard reached the receiving worker while he was finding another ladle.

He did not close the hall's pot.

He told the people arriving from the upper market that collection there had stopped and the officer was dealing with the incident. They could wait here if there was room, or receive the next verified direction. No one was to be sent back into the blocked yard merely because that had been the morning's first instruction.

One man demanded to know why they had not brought all the sacks straight to the hall.

The receiving worker looked at the cart which had reached them because it had never gone to the yard.

"Sab ek hi jagah hota," he said, "toh yeh bhi wahin rukta."

---

The opening worker and the porter were examined before the yard argument was taken any further.

The worker's foot needed protection and another assessment. He would not finish the loading shift. The porter had strained the wrist he had already rubbed raw; the dressing did not turn his brief use of the arc into permission to lift again.

A kitchen helper brought food to the examination place. Both men had missed the first serving while making the morning's stock available to other people.

The porter asked who was paying the replacement hands.

"Pehle tumhara naam duty se hataayein," the organizer said.

"Naam hata diya toh paise bhi?"

The organizer looked at the entry. These were paid hours, not a donation somebody had invented after the man was hurt.

He wrote the work already done and the injury-related stop separately. The agreed pay would not vanish with the unfinished shift. Further lost time and treatment support would need the actual records and the available public arrangements; he would not settle them with a promise no account could meet.

The cook stayed beside him until the first payment was made.

---

At the post, Rudra heard about the yard while the clinician checked his collar dressing.

He asked where Vedant was.

"Yahin," the clinician said. "Phone pe."

Rudra could see the edge of the new drawing beneath the note from his review. The small branch had not been drawn to make him feel brave. He wanted, with a cold clarity which made the wish difficult to admit, to arrive at the yard in the shape other people feared and let the men at the gate discover how little their permission mattered.

His wrist hurt when he moved towards the bed's edge.

He stopped before putting his foot down.

The clinician waited. He did not congratulate Rudra for remaining in bed as though the people at the yard had become less urgent.

"Medical gaadi mil gayi unko?" Rudra asked at last.

It had. The call from the yard had gone to the local service as well as the post. He asked the volunteer to check that the cook knew where the injured workers had been taken so their food would not wait at the wrong door.

It was a smaller thing than the force he had wanted to carry there.

It reached them.

---

Veer's next brief call was to correct his name on the officer's account, not to claim the work at the gate.

The officer had the hall's false notice and the organizer's description of the pledge conditions. The man with the live curve was being held over his own actions. The armbands, the paper and the accounts would be examined; resemblance of wording did not by itself identify the person who pasted the cancellation.

Veer asked about the porter.

"Unhone gap diya. Tab pakad paaye," the officer said.

Afterward, the volunteer beside Veer reached for the blank line under the yard report.

"Aapka message likh doon?"

Veer shook his head.

"Uska kaam likho. Naam woh bole toh."

He had spent the night trying to get his name off another man's promise. He would not put it over the work of somebody whose wrist now hurt for a reason of his own.

The volunteer wrote the action and left the personal details in the separate contact record.

---

In Mirzapur, the kitchen-lane arrangement served its first meal while the cooperative office was still taking statements.

A worker came to the clinic with the girls' bowls and the news that families no longer had to wait among damaged cloth to hear whether food was ready. The cooking women had sent a runner before opening the pot. The caretaker's entrance shift had kept the loading way clear without taking anyone's household paper as security.

It had not been seamless. One family had gone to the office twice, and a worker had lost an hour walking between the wrong doors. The public notice was being changed to show a person to ask as well as a place.

Aadhya listened with her hands resting on the support. The time written on her rest instruction was drawing close; it meant the next examination was due, not that a clock would return feeling to the right fingers. Sitara had confirmed the review before any return to the cooperative could be discussed.

Ira asked the runner to tell the office about the lost hour as well as the successful meal. She did not open the notebook herself. He could carry an ordinary spoken correction and bring back the answer later.

The window was open a little. Thread brushed the line outside without catching either girl's bed.

They ate while the food was warm.

---

By late afternoon in Prayagraj, the dealer agreed to reopen the covered strip for a smaller collection period.

The officer's contact remained. The gate fastening had been checked, the cart moved out of the turning space and replacement workers told what had happened. The dealer's injured opening worker was not brought back to demonstrate the place was safe.

The lost grain was entered as lost. The next load would cost more: smaller purchases, extra cart journeys, wages for work which had not produced a full delivery. Part of the emergency money had gone sooner than planned. The kitchens which had lent stock still needed it replaced.

When the dealer called his usual supplier, he was told future loads would require cash in advance. He set the phone down without saying whether that was coincidence or retaliation. He could not afford to ignore it either way.

He kept the yard open for the agreed period.

At the municipal hall, the cook began counting for the evening meal. The post still had its clinical reserve. Neither place had closed when the yard did.

---

At the hall, evening food was served without a private countersignature.

There was less variety than the cook had planned. People who came late had to wait while another pot was brought forward. The receiving worker kept the next day's uncertainty on the board instead of covering it with the story of a successful morning.

The woman from the boat saved a place beside her father for the relative who had fetched their belongings.

He said he could eat only a little. He had found an evening unloading shift at the covered warehouse beyond the upper-road bus stand. The pay would help with the journeys between the hall and their rooms, and he needed to go before the crew was chosen.

"Aaj hi?" she asked.

"Kal se kaam nahi kiya. Khaali baithne ka paisa nahi milta."

She put a little more food into his bowl.

"Phone charge hai?"

He showed her. It was nearly full.

She could hear her own question as a piece of yesterday following him out, and was glad he answered it without laughing. Her father asked him to bring an ordinary comb from the room when he next went there. The man nodded, touched the child's hair as he passed and left for the paid shift.

The child asked whether they could go home with him afterward.

She said they would speak to the caretaker and the officer about the return in the morning. The open gate was one part of that answer. She wanted the rest before taking her father back through it.

---

The dealer closed his yard at the agreed time with his own key.

The remaining relief stock had been counted and left under the arrangement he had accepted. His commercial store remained his. The public worker would return before the next collection; nobody required him to sleep beside the grain to prove the point had stayed open.

At the post, Vedant checked the next medical transport, handed the late calls to the relief volunteer and rested his forearm. The boys' supports and follow-up papers had not changed into trophies of restraint. Veer's knee still caught when he shifted. Rudra needed help placing the pillow so the new dressing would not rub.

No outline rose to reward either of them for staying where the clinician had left them.

Rudra called his mother before the evening became another reason to wait until tomorrow.

He told her there had been trouble at the yard, that other people had dealt with it, and that he had not gone there. He also told her about the workers' injuries. Leaving those out would have turned the truth into a cleaner story than the day deserved.

"Tumne khaaya?" she asked.

He had. He asked about her appointment before asking for Ram.

The review had not taken away her movement restrictions. Dadi was with her in the nearby room. Ram was resting in the step-down ward after the day's care; there was no need to wake him to prove the phone worked.

Rudra said he would call again in the morning.

Afterward, Veer called his own mother and gave the fuller review before she had to find it between his jokes. The volunteer outside their door took the next post call. For a few minutes the room held only the sounds of two people being allowed to finish a day.

---

In Mirzapur, Sitara set out the papers for Aadhya's reassessment. No one packed her bag for the cooperative in advance.

Ira's new notebook lay closed beside the bowls. The runner had brought back the corrected kitchen notice and read it aloud. It named the worker who would answer questions at each place. The people using it could ask for another correction tomorrow.

The four spoke briefly at the planned check-in. They compared what had actually stayed open, where the instructions had failed and who needed rest before the next shift. Nobody promised to come running across the distance if a call was missed.

When they ended, the two cities remained two cities.

At the hall, the stove was cleaned. The woman laid her son's sandals beside the mat with their buckles open for morning.

---

Her relative called before she lay down.

He was still at the covered warehouse. The unloading was finished. The crew had been told they could not leave until each household supplied an adult for the private night watch.

"Paise?" she asked, not understanding.

"Paise nahi. Aadmi. Apne ghar se bulaao, warna khud chalo."

She took the phone to the receiving worker. He called the officer on his own handset while she kept her relative talking. The warehouse name and bus-stand approach were repeated and checked.

Behind the voice on her phone, a vehicle's side door slammed.

"Humein baahar le aaye," her relative said. "Watch ki gaadi mein—"

Someone told him to put the phone down.

The receiving worker gave the officer that update at once. The woman asked which way the vehicle was facing.

For a moment she heard only an engine.

Then, very close to the receiver, another man spoke.

"Ghar se koi nahi aaya? Isko bithaao."
