# CHAPTER SEVENTY-EIGHT — AFTER THE LAST BELL

---

> *"The final had a winner.*
> *The grass had a bill, the injured had another appointment,*
> *and the people outside the gate still had their own names."*
>
> *— district tournament account, corrected after the closing announcements*

---

Yellow opened his holding hand.

The pale rim went out before it reached the jaw.

His arm did not stop with it. The bare forearm brushed beneath the bearded man's chin as the loosened clod gave way under the planted palm. The man twisted his face clear, put the other hand onto sound earth and came down on both knees.

Yellow stepped out of the crossing bodies. His heel held. The hand he had released from the parry stayed open instead of catching the other man by a wrist.

The referee was between them with both arms raised.

No bell for a point.

"Ruko. Wahin. Shape nahi."

The clinician came through the officials' strip. The helper reached the kneeling man first and asked him to keep still until he had been checked.

The board remained four–four.

---

There was a red mark beneath the jaw, not an opened throat.

The clinician checked the bearded man's mouth, jaw movement and the place where the arm had touched. He asked about swallowing, dizziness and any blow to the head. The man answered for himself. His head had not hit the ground. The fresh scrape on the catching palm stung when the helper cleaned the dirt away.

The clinician did not convert those answers into permission to jump up for the crowd. He wanted a rest, another examination and instructions for any change before discussing a further exchange.

Yellow sat on the corner stool for his own check. The holding forearm hurt after the sudden release. He showed where and did not make the ache sound smaller because the other man had been closer to the live edge.

The clinician examined it and ended all shapes for the moment.

The men outside the rope began arguing about whose point the interrupted movement ought to have been.

Neither athlete answered them.

---

The referee spoke to the line judge before returning to the board.

No free scoring hand had reached a tab. The stop had come before the bodies separated. No fifth point had been earned, and the accidental brush beneath the jaw was not a legal target quietly renamed because it had missed being worse.

He left the marks where they were.

The ground crew lifted the loose clod from the working patch. The torn runner came with it, a length of pale root hanging from the underside. The roots had pulled a shallow pocket out of the soil. The worker separated the firm edge from the loose part before letting anyone put a foot beside it.

They closed that stretch.

One helper began smoothing the surface with the back of a rake. The grounds worker stopped him until the damage had been inspected properly. Making the top look flat would not put the rooted support back.

---

On the verandah, Veer let the breath out which he had been holding since the ridge moved.

The volunteer asked whether the shout had hurt his ribs.

"Thoda."

He said it before deciding whether the answer would make somebody send him home. The shoulder stayed in its support. The knee had not left the cloth.

Below, the bearded contestant had stood with the helper after the examination. Yellow was still seated, listening to the clinician. There was care being given to both, not a patient waiting for Veer to become well enough to provide it.

Veer checked the time.

"Chalte hain," he said.

The driver had the return message. The side passage remained clear. He could leave by it without crossing the place where the two men had almost made a much worse memory.

A man at the steps asked whether he was going before the winner was decided.

"Haan."

Veer took the walking support from the volunteer.

The man looked disappointed, as though a famous spectator ought to be able to buy more minutes from an injured knee than an ordinary one.

Veer did not offer it another loan.

---

He was back at the post before the ring reopened.

The clinician checked the effect of the shout and the journey, kept the next rest interval and asked whether there had been a new movement he had not mentioned.

There had not. He had wanted to stand. He had called instead.

The volunteer put the phone where he could reach it without twisting. A message from Rudra was waiting, asking for the story when the visit ended.

Veer sent the first part honestly.

*Abhi winner nahi. Dono ki jaanch ho rahi hai. Main waapas aa gaya.*

He read it before sending. Then he asked the volunteer to leave the phone down until the rest interval was over.

At the hospital, Rudra heard the message after his own nurse had finished. The part about coming back mattered enough that he did not ask for the last point first.

---

The ring did not reopen on a promise that the grass would hold next time.

The damaged side was taken out of use. On the sound packed earth, the grounds crew set a smaller chalk circle clear of the loosened edge. The officials walked it before the waiting contestants did.

There would be no active techniques in the remainder of the programme. No guard, no worked root. Shoulder touches and the whole-foot boundary would remain. The change was read to both sides, not hidden inside an announcement about better safety.

It was an altered contest. Anyone could decline it without losing money already earned. The unresolved semifinal would remain suspended unless both men accepted a safe way to finish it.

The bearded contender asked to hear the limits again without his corner answering.

His two hands had their own reason to make the question matter.

---

After rest and re-examination, the clinician discussed one ordinary control exchange with each man.

Yellow's tired forearm was to stay out of shape work. The bearded man had no new dizziness or difficulty with the jaw at that examination; he was to report any change and keep both palms out of weight-bearing. His little catching-hand scrape had been dressed. Nothing in the conversation promised that pain could not appear later.

Both could choose to stop. Both chose to continue under the limited terms, with the clinician present and the sound surface checked.

They walked the new circle together.

The bearded man tapped the air above the marked-off grass with his empty hand, then let it fall by his side.

"Yeh ab nahi."

"Mera bhi nahi," Yellow said, showing the bare holding arm.

The referee confirmed it. Both heard the same answer.

The board was brought round with the four marks still beside each number.

---

The deciding exchange began upright.

Yellow came towards the nearer tab, stopped short and waited for the second hand which had beaten him earlier. The bearded man did not offer that answer again. He moved his feet towards the open side, keeping the shoulders level enough that either hand could finish the approach.

Yellow gave ground.

The circle was smaller. The line could not be treated as if it had stayed where they had first learned it.

The bearded contender saw it and checked the advance before driving the other man out. His free fingers came straight for the high tab instead.

Yellow turned the shoulder, not the wrist. His own hand stayed close until the approaching arm passed the place where it could have touched.

One short step inside.

His fingertips reached the other tab.

The bearded man was still upright when the referee stopped them. No palm hit the ground, no late shape arrived to make the result louder.

Five–four, yellow.

---

Yellow did not throw his holding arm into the air.

He looked towards the clinician first, then offered the good hand. His opponent touched it with his fingertips instead of closing a painful palm around a grip for the photograph.

They stood apart while the official gave the result.

There was an actual finalist now. There was also a man with a tender jaw and dressed hands who had played eight good points and lost the ninth. Both descriptions could remain on the same afternoon's account.

The helper took the bearded contender back for the planned check. Yellow went to rest rather than demonstrating the last turn to the waiting row.

The grounds crew kept the torn clod outside the ring in the tray of material which needed disposal. It did not become a trophy awarded to a Kul.

---

The other semifinal was held later on the checked surface under the same no-active-technique terms.

A Rakshak woman with a tightly bound braid won five–two against a Prakriti entrant. She was not the green-corner woman from the opening day. Her turns were shorter, and she had a habit of letting an opponent finish committing to a route before using the space he left.

The final would be the following morning, after rest and fresh individual examinations. Two Rakshak contestants. Not two versions of the same body and not a road settlement decided in a family courtyard.

The steward put both numbers on the board and took the remaining competitors' payment questions before closing the table.

---

That evening, the porter returned from the clinic review which had been arranged for him.

The new fracture still needed immobilization. The review had not turned the introduction money into permission to lift. His companion put the next instructions beside the earlier ones and checked the return time with him.

He asked for the semifinal result after the arm was settled.

Five–four. Yellow. A final tomorrow, without active techniques.

The porter listened, then asked the caller to give his regards to the bearded man if he wanted them passed on.

He had no obligation to turn the other's stopped bout into a comforting version of his own fall. There was room to care about somebody else's hands without deciding that all hurt hands had lived the same day.

---

The final began the next morning.

The surface had been checked again. The overnight work had secured the excluded edge, not restored the torn living strip. Both finalists had rested and had fresh examinations before accepting the same limited format. Yellow's forearm remained sore. No guard work was being smuggled into a warm-up because the printed word final looked important.

The woman tied her braid more tightly, then asked the official to straighten one shoulder tab which had folded beneath the vest seam.

Yellow checked his own and held out the free hand.

They touched fingers. The referee brought them to the marks.

At the post, Veer heard the bell through the ordinary call the club volunteer had offered for this one bout. The phone rested where he could listen without holding it. He was not at the venue and did not claim to see the turns the caller would have to describe.

---

The woman took the first point by waiting.

Yellow showed the open shoulder which had drawn three different answers over the earlier bouts. She did not reach after it. She let him finish the turn, stepped across the route of his recovery and touched the tab he had expected to bring safely behind him.

One–nil.

He answered on the next exchange with a shorter approach and an honest change of feet. She moved to avoid the hand, took too much room and put the whole rear foot outside.

One each.

Two more points went to her, neither through a worked root or a pale face. On the first she caught a shoulder in the pause between his steps. On the second she refused the same bait twice, then used his own retreat to bring the boundary behind his heel.

Three–one.

Yellow went back breathing hard. The woman accepted her water, asked her corner for the cloth and wiped the sweat which had started running towards one eye.

The clinician kept the scheduled pause.

The crowd could be impatient without making either forearm less tired.

---

Yellow came back to the fifth exchange with the free hand lower.

His opponent gave him a little of the high shoulder. He did not look up for it. He moved towards the foot which had been keeping her ready to turn, stopped outside contact and waited for her to make the first correction.

She changed the leading leg.

He crossed in that instant and touched the tab beside her chin, flat fingers against cloth, safely below the forbidden target.

Three–two.

At the reset, he offered the same low hand and used it differently. She came to intercept the route he had just taken. He stepped back into the space her foot had vacated and reached the other tab before she could close the turn.

Three each.

The woman went to her corner smiling without looking pleased.

Yellow had changed. She had noticed just too late. Her corner held out the bottle and left her enough silence to decide what to do with that fact.

---

The seventh exchange stayed in the dry centre longer than either had planned.

Hands rose, stopped short and withdrew. Yellow refused the first offered shoulder. The woman refused the opening beneath his free elbow. Neither was a mistake simply because somebody in the standing row shouted for them to stop thinking.

Then Yellow let the leading foot travel another hand's width.

She moved on the landing.

Her hand did not chase the nearer tab. Her body turned towards the far one, making him choose which side to bring out of reach. He chose the shoulder she had shown him first.

The other touch landed.

Four–three.

The referee stopped them before her weight could follow the fingers into a shove.

Yellow looked at the place where she had arrived, then at the earlier route his own foot had offered. He nodded before the corner called him back.

---

On the last exchange, Yellow came for the centre of her turn instead of either edge.

She let him approach.

He had room for the first step. On the second, the path he expected her to leave remained occupied by a body which had not yet decided to move.

He checked the advance rather than putting his shoulder through hers.

She used the check.

One outside step. A level hand. Fingertips on the tab while his other arm was still returning from the approach he had stopped.

The referee raised the scoring hand.

Five–three.

For a moment the woman stood exactly where she had finished, making sure there was no later motion for anybody to misunderstand. Then she stepped back, let out a breath and bent to rest her hands on her own thighs.

The final bell rang.

---

The caller gave Veer the score twice over the noise.

He had preferred Yellow. He found he wanted the woman to hear the applause anyway. He asked the caller to tell both corners it had been a good match if they wanted the message, then ended the call as promised.

At the akhada, somebody complained that a final ought to have shown more light.

The winner straightened as the medical helper approached.

"Tum roshni lene aaye the," she said. "Main point."

Then she went for the examination before anyone asked her to do it again for a camera.

---

The winner collected the purse after the check.

She counted the notes beside the steward, found one stuck to the back of another and counted again. Yellow received the runner-up payment separately. His earned money was not handed to her to make the first pile look larger for the photograph.

The remaining claims had their own place at the table: treatment, transport, the grounds work, the porter's lost working days. The expense reserve had paid the first clinical charges. It could not be announced as if that had settled every other loss.

The organizer gave the people making claims their copies and the time at which the outstanding part would be discussed. Nobody had to call the final a bad match to keep asking for what the earlier injury had cost.

The braided woman put her money away before accepting the little cup.

Then she loosened the braid which had been pulling at her scalp since morning. The relief on her face looked nothing like a lineage taking possession of a city.

Yellow asked whether she had known he would check the last approach.

"Nahi," she said. "Tum aa jaate toh mujhe hatna padta."

He looked at the space between them, remembering how little of it there had been. Then he laughed.

Her corner companion asked whether they were finally buying sweets. She gave him a note from the purse and asked for a small box, enough for the people who had actually stayed, not a mountain which would look impressive and go uneaten.

When it came, she offered Yellow the first piece. He took one without being asked to call losing a gift.

The bearded man accepted one with his fingertips. The helper carried the box to the grounds workers next.

For a few minutes there was nothing to interpret in the quiet chewing, except that a woman had won something difficult and wanted the taste of it to last beyond the announcement.

---

The bearded semifinalist had come for his follow-up and to hear the final.

His jaw remained tender. The new scrape stayed dressed; the clinician's instructions did not end with the event. He congratulated both finalists without offering a full-palm grip to prove that he had accepted losing.

Someone told him he might have won if the grass had not given way.

"Ho sakta hai," he said.

He did not have to settle an unwitnessed version of the match in order to give the woman her due. She had beaten the man who had beaten him on the surface and terms they had actually used.

The grounds worker passed with the tray containing the torn roots. He stopped to ask whether the palm had been checked again. It had.

The turf would need ordinary repair. The workers who would do it had a paid job for the following day, not an instruction to wait until a Prakriti visitor made the scar disappear.

---

The first large version of the final reached the tea stalls before the scoring board was put away.

It said the whole tournament had been won without anyone showing a single shape.

At one stall, a customer repeated it to a porter whose arm was supported beside him. The man listened, then asked the speaker whether the earlier introductions had been erased as well.

The customer hesitated.

"Final ki baat kar raha hoon."

"Toh final bolo."

The correction did not make the woman's five–three less real. It put the other days back around it.

By afternoon, some accounts carried both the bright opening bouts and the quieter last stage. Some still preferred a cleaner tale. A boy drew two shoulder marks on his shirt in chalk and asked a friend whether he wanted to play. The friend agreed, then made him wait while he put the hot tea glass down somewhere safe.

Nobody at the stall stopped needing to go to work because a local champion had been named.

---

In Mirzapur, Aadhya heard the final score while the woman helping them arranged the next short college readback.

"Dono ek hi Kul?"

"Haan," Sitara said.

Ira asked which one had won. Not which side the winner represented. They got the entry number and the account the caller had actually given, and let the missing movements remain missing.

Aadhya liked the idea that sharing a Kul did not mean having the same answer to a moving hand. She kept the thought separate from any expectation that her own right fingers ought to wake because another woman had learned to turn well.

They used the next interval for the college page. Ira stopped with the left covering still in place and the ordinary right eye tired. Aadhya let the helper move the heavier book. A correct answer did not change the hand instructions.

---

Rudra got the score after his afternoon round.

He asked whether Veer had stayed at the post for the final. He had. The reply had a complaint about hearing the crowd more clearly than the points.

Rudra sent back that this was how everybody else had experienced Veer's commentary for years.

The answer arrived with a promise to dispute the charge when both had enough energy for a serious argument. Not tonight. No date for travelling home or standing together at a ring was hidden inside it.

Later, Rukmini called from Varanasi after Ram's care update. Her own shoulder was still supported. She wanted the ordinary part of Rudra's day as well as the ward's account, and he told her about the handout question neither he nor Vedant had solved.

"Toh kisi aur se poochho," she said.

He looked towards the nurse helping another patient settle a pillow.

"Poochh raha hoon."

For once, he was not answering only about the page.

They ended before the call became another task his tired father had to wake for.

---

By late evening, the sounds from the akhada had become messages people could choose to hear tomorrow.

At the hospital, the volunteer asked Rudra what he wanted left within reach for the night. The phone, its working charger, the ordinary page with the unfinished question. The drawings would stay where the clinical team could find them, not under his hand for a private examination every time the curtain moved.

The volunteer lowered the page stand after asking.

Rudra had expected a tournament to make him want to be stronger. It had done that. It had also made him want, with an intensity he found harder to describe, to walk out of a place after a good afternoon and think about whether he had enough money for the bus, not whether the thing inside his skin would stay closed until the next morning.

He wanted an ordinary problem to be allowed to remain ordinary for more than a few minutes.

The nurse came to check the supported ankle. He told her it had begun aching in the position he was holding. She adjusted it under the current plan, waited for his answer and left it where the discomfort eased.

That, at least, did not need a mystery to explain it.

---

Veer called before his own last rest interval.

He wanted to know whether Rudra had heard the final result. He had.

"Agli baar apni commentary record karna," Rudra said. "Points bhi sun lenge kabhi."

"Agli baar tu baithna paas. Galat bolun toh wahin tok dena."

They were quiet for a moment after that, each knowing how many separate permissions a careless next time had begun to contain.

"Date nahi di," Veer added.

"Maangi bhi nahi."

Rudra could hear somebody at the post asking whether the last kettle had been emptied. The ordinary noise made the distance between them feel smaller without pretending it was gone.

"Kal phone karna," he said.

"Kuchh hua toh pehle."

"Kuchh na hua toh bhi."

Veer agreed. They ended with no task waiting for either to perform in order to deserve the next call.

---

In Varanasi, Ram listened while Rukmini gave him the smaller part of the update.

A local final. Two contestants of the same Kul. Their son still in hospital, asking for his college work.

Ram asked whether somebody had actually sent the page.

"Haan."

He looked at the nurse folding the corner of his own blanket clear of the bedside lead. There was breathing support close enough to use if he tired; the step-down ward had not disappeared because his son was in another one.

"Kal waala bhi bhejne ko bolna," he said.

Rukmini repeated it to Dadi later. No training instruction went with the request. For that night, a father had asked for something which might leave a little of his son's future unclaimed.

---

The new warmth began after the late ward round.

Rudra had not practised a count. The final had ended hours earlier. He was not watching a recording, moving a load or asking the light to prove whether rest had worked.

He pressed the call button and told the nurse before touching the collar.

The doctor was called. The nurse checked him and helped loosen the shirt enough to examine the area without loading the braced wrist. The skin above the collarbone remained closed.

Gold showed at the old broken outline.

The small dark patch was still there, with the short lower offshoot they had seen three evenings earlier. That was frightening enough. Rudra began to describe it, then stopped when the nurse looked farther down.

A separate line of gold had become visible below the breastbone.

The doctor arrived while it held.

---

He checked Rudra before bringing the light closer. Breathing, pain, ordinary observations. Rudra described the warmth and a pressure which had not been part of the ankle ache. The nurse kept the foot supported; there was no useful reason to make him stand for a better view.

"Yeh pehle dekha?" the doctor asked.

"Is tarah nahi."

Not a claim that it had never existed. An answer about what Rudra had actually seen.

The doctor asked for the earlier notes and continued the assessment. The nurse notified the duty team and contacted Vedant with the new symptoms and the place where the doctor was examining them.

Rudra asked for the family contact to get the same actual update. The phone went to the volunteer; he stayed under the clinician's hands instead of trying to make everyone else's fear manageable first.

Above the new bright line, the older gold remained broken but visible. It had not all gone out.

Then a dark nick appeared in the separate line below the breastbone.

The two bright edges pulled a little apart while Rudra watched.

"Abhi," he said.

The doctor had seen it too.

There was still no blood on the closed skin. The neighbouring gold held its shape. But below the old patch, another unbroken line had gone dark.
