# CHAPTER 22 — SPONSORED (Book 2, Ch 2)

Thursday brought the flyer.

It was handed out at college gates and paan shops and the wrestling pit's fence — yellow paper, well printed, a colour-blocked sun over a stylized river:

**MERIDIAN FOUNDATION presents**
**OPEN AKHADA — All Banaras Challenge**
*India's youth, India's strength. Open wrestling bouts at the old riverside ground. Prize ₹5,000 for any local who lasts three full minutes with our State Champion. This Saturday after the public lecture.*

Every chai stall in the district got fifty copies. They did not go home with their evening count. People want to see things the city can now afford to look at: an akhada opening, a prize, India's name folded three times into third person.

By evening the college boys had renamed it, predictably, *paanch-hazaar-ka-chance* — and nobody that heard the name all afternoon was too poor to hope.

Veer was one of them.

---

The old akhada had reopened Thursday morning. Actual contractors did the work — proper shovelling, new sand worked into the ring, three parachute tents for shade, a visitor gallery fenced off, two black vehicles parked under what used to be the pavilion's left pergola. Birju was declared the State Champion at three PM.

All Banaras knew Birju. Temple-hair pehlwan, wife's flower stall across the Hanuman temple, six-foot-two of akhada stubbornness, once banned a year over a suspension nobody ever explained, back from Pune camps a June, runner-up at the state level. And yet it was not entirely the same Birju. He was quieter. He declined every ceremonial boast strongmen start bouts with. In the pits between rounds he walked with a shallow, careful gait like a man walking another's legs.

First bout: a local club-kid in his twenties offered himself as warm-up. Birju caught him by the waist, lifted him off his feet with the energy of somebody hoisting a sack, and set him down flat on the sand-ring side of the boundary — so gently it was almost an apology. Second and third, same. Four district regulars in twenty minutes. Each thrown without visible violence; none bruised. All of them glared at their own surprise for a while afterward like customers charged for meals they don't remember eating.

Then Veer saw the thing nobody else in the crowd quite examined — the gauntlet.

On Birju's left forearm, under the sweatband: a flat micro-cuff of brushed steel the width of a wristband, sealed snug to the skin underneath — bolts? adhesive? — something medical-looking anyway — and on its outer face, eight hair-line ticks around a dial the size of a Hindi full-stop.

Same device — *almost* — as the gate-clerk's clipboard meter. Almost identical, except for the single production detail no villager in that crowd was equipped to check: this one's needle was not at zero.

The dial's red needle swung hard up when Birju gripped a man, held its reading through the throw, and settled only when the hands emptied. Load: up. Delivered: down. It behaved exactly like a scale being asked to weigh loads.

Veer identified, then, the other two such devices in the pit gallery — one per controller, tent-shadowed at either end of the visitor rail, identically suited, one dial-face glimpsed under each cuff, same brassy municipal stillness. The nearer one had a woolen-bound notebook and wrote constantly in it with the indulgent attention of somebody billing an incident report.

Veer left the akhada a little after nine. Twice on the walk home he stopped and looked at nothing in particular — the old wordless habit of a man who has noticed something and is still letting it rise. At home, in the courtyard, Ira was already waiting with the chai. They looked at each other over it with the exact quality of two pieces of the same game laid by different people.

"Dekha maine," Veer said. "Unke haath mein joote naapne waale sensors hain — cuff waale. Pehlwan ke bhi, gate ke bhi."

"Ab humein bhi pata hai," said Ira, equally flat. "Roz shaam poora halchal dekh ke hi uthi ayi hain naap umhre saath. Saturday tak harek number kho lenge humare tarah."

On Friday morning Rudra went himself.

His father had said not to go near their gates. That covered gates. It did not cover a crowd rope at eleven AM at an akhada Rudra had legal rights to, older than the rights of anyone employed to patrol it. He wore full sleeves, kept his chest wrapped plain — no tape, Ram's rule — and stood inside the second row of the afternoon's shove-along public with his hands folded like any local student on a Friday off.

Birju worked two bouts. The Foundation's chosen champion was exactly as Veer had described: quiet, methodical, empty of ceremony, throwing full-time district heavyweights with the calm energy-budget of a man setting down orders his body already understood. Between bouts the two controllers noted things in their register and changed the dial-face on his cuff twice — a lieutenant boy-wrencher walked from tent to champion with the intimate short history of boys sent by places to keep speaking the whole truth to the wrestlers.

Rudra watched and measured. The wrist-strip — on the wrist, not on his body: Ira had strapped it to the hinge of the rope-stand where he stood — flickered hot each time Birju loaded a throw. And each time it flickered, Rudra's chest answered with a small, definite *tremor* — no burn, no pain: a punctuation, as if something in his ribs were disagreeing — patiently, grammatically — with the existence of whatever the cuff was doing.

*The device was not making power,* he understood. It was **borrowing** — borrowing from the same shelf his own chest opened into; some controlled drawer of it, some small, regular, legal nightly closed-off access corridor; and the borrowed power obeyed, all right — but to the fingers of his own storm-hot chest, acclimatized by two decades to a double-lock on the same door, the borrower's note felt arranged exactly like a counterfeit feels to the owner of a shop: the whole design flawless, the *neighbourhood* of it wrong.

Also: it was advertised. Every time Birju threw, two thin wisps of something like burnt-sugar heat triggered from the cuff's underside and damped against his skin — damped *into* him; and each time, Birju's age read back into his face a quarter-day older. Whatever currency was being paid was not being paid by machinery.

And, in the minute after the third bout, when the portable display struggled to clear itself of the boy carried out swinging uncounsciously in the victory appraisal, the nearest controller turned around and looked at Rudra — strictly, cleanly, with the unhurried attention of a performance review — and wrote one more line in the notebook.

Then he turned back to the match.

Rudra left at noon through the west crowd, bought his father an unnecessary packet of salt at the kirana to normalize the walk.

He wrote nothing down until he got home.

Friday's afternoon belonged to Aadhya alone. Shastri-ji's diary lay open on her desk-rack; on the window-sill the potted aloe tilted with the sun the way a deputy clerk follows papers. She took the week's measurable guest list — the pulse-test of 7:32 PM Wednesday night, the cars, the herons leaving, the new survey walls, the bracelet cuff — and tried them, one by one, against the diary's loose-laid final entry: the copier's shorthand pattern of every site they had ever admired for rust of paper.

Only one column had empty internal consistency. She labelled that column simply: **haveli.**

Then, because she had stopped owing herself decorations, she wrote plainly:

**"Unke paas aala hai. Humare paas beej hai. Kalaam lagaane waala beej hi hamesha jeet-ta hai — bas dheere."**

At six PM, in her own kitchen, her mother called her to table in a voice dipped ten degrees off normal: "Aadhya!"

"Baith ja Ammu," Aadhya said from the doorway. "Khaana main laga rahi hoon. Kal chhutti ka din hai — aur college ke bahar hum jhagda nahi karte, dono."

Her mother, who ran this household by the ledger of exact responses followed by suggestion-warnings, suffered herself into the vegetables being arranged by somebody temporarily in charge. In families like this, obedience functions like savings.

What the city did not see that Saturday happened after eleven PM, at the old freight yard by the dead canal spur — no lamp, no advertiser, no audience; which is how certain kinds of theatre should always be, and almost never are.

Ram had walked down there the slow way, eating peanuts from a paper cone like a man whose evening contains nothing at all: because by Friday night his own window-belt survey had finished its ill-news without asking his opinion. The silver-car crews were *sweeping* again — crossing the same alleys as week before, but now with one manpower extra in each car and — his ear, older than every department, could tell at a km — a *second engine* idling one street over, always, holding station, waiting to be asked.

Like patrols. Like crews. Like, quite simply, his children are walking like armies in this city once again.

At eleven-ten a short man in a fitted serious track-training top — somebody's forty-year-old physical instructor, by his build — came along the yard fence as if doing a timing and stopped instead so suddenly that the pacing of it read like an appointment. Looked straight at Ram where Ram stood full against the fence post between two street lamps, unnoticed but not actually hidden. Ram tipped the last peanuts into his mouth, and folded the paper cone flat with the care of a man returning a library book — crushed it small; he put it in his pocket, as if leaving the street tidier counted somewhat.

"Aap," said the instructor politely, "are walking very late, sir."

"Cold weather," said Ram. "Joints behave when the walking happens."

The instructor respected that answer for exactly one second. Then his wrist — the same flat cuff — rose, casual as consulting the time, and the needle on it, across fifteen yards of dirty dark, swung hard past its red mark and stuck there, buzzing with a faint fry like a trapped bee.

Both men looked at the dial.

"Interesting," said the instructor, in a voice being puppets for somebody senior and elsewhere over a phone-line. Inflame response. "Expected, actually. Uncle, we're told to tell: mind your own mohalla's peace. No interference. Your boy is — statistically interesting. Keep him indoors from tonight. Machines get confiscated as part of the guidance, but we wait for that."

Ram stood a good long second taking inventory. Then:

"District ka sukoon toh humney sambhaala hi hai. Tumhare machine ne seedhi baat zubaan se bolne ki saza hamko de rahi hai. — officer bahut bade hote hain, haan. Lekin in —" and he nodded once, mildly, at the cuff, at the needle trembling against the red line, "— se nahi."

The second car heard it — from one street over, an idling engine considering whether it had been summoned.

Three men came out of it anyway.

Ram, from the freight-yard fence, made a single gesture nobody catches on camera — one hand low at his hip, fingers opening: the old signal from the war nobody had yet invited him to fight: *not yet. Not in front of a man holding a paper cone of peanuts.* The street did not change.

The instructor blinked once. Decision arrived in him visibly — even to a blind watcher: the dial had done its sum, and the sum had been *noted upward*. He turned, gathered his men with one hank-horizontal gesture of the flat palm, and walked back to the car. The car rose on its civilized wheels and went, smooth, unbothered, identical to every delivery van in creation. The man at the fence did not move till the taillights were eaten by the bend.

Ram folded his arms over the fence rail then and stood watch until quarter past twelve, by which time three more cars had come and gone quietly and the night's wind over the yard smelled like dust that had never once been bones, and Buried Varanasi itself short still patiently holding its centuries in its own two hands.

He walked home — no limp tonight; that was later — wrote the milk order on the kitchen chit for morning — *what a father does* — and stood by his son's door for one minute listening to sleeping breathing like the sound of a city he had, once again, bought one week for.

**Yudh abhi shuru hua hai.**

On the roof-pipeline of the same sleeping city that night — two kilometers across the rooftops feeding his lower step every Sunday from the smaller sewage of a light-grid anxious Tuesday — Rudra Vashisht slept the deep sleep of a boy whose week had plainly ended: no nightmares, no climbing re-calculation of alarms, only the plain division of himself back among the ordinary dreams. His own fists were in front of his chest, fisted tight around nothing. On the desk near him, in a hard-bound khata, the nero-black notebook-page he had closed after his father's voice in the stair-well lay open to some white spaces of night from Wednesday:
**Kal ayega. Kal kuch zayaada nahin toh bilkul zaroor hoga**, he'd written plainly;

And below it, puzzled at by nobody, one cleanly-pencilled line of arithmetical arrival — four figures, in a childlike upright hand:

*180 sec + needle + unhonein mujhe har jagah dekha — hum bhi dekhte hain.*

Ira's night ledger carried one line:

*Saturday: lecture aur akhada ek hi din. Public sab waahin honge — aur hum bhi.*

And under it, kept plain, which was the whole appetite of that chapter's worth of worry set down at last the way lists are written by people who know what they are for:

*Akhare mein jab naapne waala aayega — asli kacheri tab lagegi. Unke paas jo bhi hai, unko skill nahi aati. Skill hum aake lagayenge hum dono ko. seede vishvaas dono ke hain. — Ye meri line hai is raat ka: itna bharosa naa lo ki aankhein band ho jayein.*

Saturday morning, ten AM: the lecture.

The Badi Haveli's outer courtyard had been set up for it — rented folding chairs in rows of twenty, a low plywood stage with the Meridian sun-and-river banner, a sound system from Patna, bottled water stacked like ammunition. Police permission stamp visible on a clipboard at gate. Chairs: full by quarter past. Veer's matriculation batch was there in a herd; Dadi in a gray shawl with four of her bridge-score retired-teacher friends, on principle; Aadhya's mother, on principle of her own; and, at three scattered points Ira had chosen the previous night — rooftops, window, tea-stall — the owners of this city's private thunderstorm.

The speaker was forty, fitted suit of some civilian gray, Hindi without any district in it, named — said the flyer taped crookedly down on the podium — **Head of Outreach, Eastern Division**. No personal name offered. He began with a joke about the weather, and got the laugh affairs dirigibles of that weight buy for money.

His topic was *heritage.* He spoke of the Badi Haveli's carved foundation beams "found in our surveys to thrum — at frequencies matching old temple bells, gentlemen; science admits the romance"; of a proposal to map similar "*resonant heritage zones*" through the old city, free of charge, public data, "for every child." Applause. He spoke of "lost technologies" and "living patterns" and India owing itself "rediscovery." More applause. Vocabulary excellent. Content: none.

Ira, on her rooftop, heard the applause meet the roof and stop — there were thirty houses between.

The courtyard's *air*, she noted, was doing the absorb work the facade had earlier done down the block. The estate swallowed its noise's bottoms. Sometime in the night, somebody downstairs in the Foundation had re-tuned the pulse schedule for Saturday; her hand-strip answered only every twelve seconds now. Different rhythm, different season.

She wore her anklet anyway, and counted.

Forty minutes in, the speaker requested *volunteers* for a demonstration of "traditional metabolic study" — his words — and seven eager young men from three colleges went up, delighted. A woman in white conducted the demonstration with a padded glove and a clipboard, touches to the elbow and shoulder, a half-minute each: "electrical conductivity mapping," said the speaker, smiling. Each volunteer came back to applause and a coupon for cold drinks.

Ira watched the woman in white's mouth counting with each touch — a little read-off of numbers addressed to some listening place under the stage, each number departing that clipboard like a bead departing a thread.

The whole thing took fifty-five minutes. It ended with an invitation — the akhada, the challenge, five thousand rupees, patriotism. The crowd left warmer than it arrived. The stillness the estate had taken all that way out of the stoveyard was never noticed leaving or coming back.

Ira wrote in her register: *"jaali hai — magar chamakdar jaali."*
---

The open challenge began by three.

Fourteen bouts before tea. The first challenger — a big conscientious boy from the college weight-room — was down in eleven seconds; the fourth in nine. The crowd had begun to migrate from the fenced gallery out to the sand-ring rail, the way crowds migrate toward verdicts.

Then Veer stepped into the ring.

He stood before Birju, stripped at the waist, in his good sleeveless sports shirt with Veer's mother's medical certificate still visible under the shoulder-velcro of his ash-smeared seconds-time sackcloth. Rope-pile behind him, white striping on his wrist-palms. The champion looked him over with the untroubled eyes of advantages.

"Naam?" Birju asked out loud for the announcer.

"Moin-ul-Haq. Veer. Mohalla Peepal-wali-gali."

"Umra?"

"Unnees."

Birju laughed — actually laughed, the first unregistered human sound all day; it startled his own two handlers. "Theek hai. Unnees walon ki mijaaz yehi hota hai."

They began.

The ring had learned a pace. Veer was thrown twice more. Twice more he stood up. The champion, whose wrist-device had begun hissing faintly like a kettle somebody pretends not to hear, and whose handlers were now calling across him in an invented scurry of tactical ferocity, had not, however, thrown Veer once since the third throw. The counters of the tent worked like possessed hands.

This time the crowd did not cheer at every coming. It pulled in.

One hundred seconds. One hundred eighty.

Veer had stopped trying to win at ninety seconds, and started, visibly, to do the thing his grandfather once taught entire villages to do against the passing of rivers: hold. Body block. Spine braced. Gift from nobody. The champion grappled him around the waist; Veer drifted an inch and settled; grappled him again, and the same thing happened again; four times. The tent poles tapped with nervous hands. Someone's jacket was being swept faster than it could breathe. When the timer's second hand passed the 2:30 belt-notification raksh, three hundred mouths began to count aloud in uneven Hindi like a school crowd suddenly authoritative:

*Do sau saath… do sau assi… do sau nabbe…*

**TEEN MINUTE.**

Two hundred forty. Two hundred seventy. For the first time since Thursday, man in the visitors' gallery stood.

In one whole-eighty-second window of their now joining him, mouths in the front rows set down plainly a rumour that would be true by night:

*"Paanch hazaar nahin de rahe. Bracelet waale ka diya hua gaya — haar gaya akeli apaney zor par. Ye haath petrada nahi; uske bracelet ki zubaan ghin bolti hai."*

At two hundred ninety seconds the whole enclosure found the true meter of a Banaras crowd reconfiguring itself around an entertainment: chaotic, broken-bell, hymnal.
The match ended at three minutes flat on the referee's call — a draw, officially; neither official nor unsatisfying. The champion climbed down from the boy slowly, looked at his peeled wrist-cuff as if it had been a borrowed dog that had bitten him, and said in a carrying voice designed for this lane only:

"Tu theek hai ladka. Seedha hai. Is zamaane mein — bada durlabh cheez hai."

"Jai ho dada," said Veer, from somewhere under his own face; and for nobody in particular added, "ye— paanch hazaar kisi or ko mile."

The champion walked away through his handlers without pausing; that, also, must have looked expensive from the tent.

Birju looked at him for one long second — then put his hand, heavy, on Veer's shoulder, in front of everything, in a gesture older than any paperwork that bazaar owned, and walked away into the pits.

Veer grinned through the bruise, accepted three waters, and walked out of that ring a different number than he walked in: a boy with a local title called *the one Birju couldn't.* It never left him after.

From the visitor gallery, one smooth officer with a white cap watched him go — for one honest, unblinking second — before turning his face to the clipboard. Notebook entry. Sentences not recorded.

Ira met him by the ice-pushcart and silently offered a fist. He tapped it with a knuckle that was still shaking.

"Aankhon mein dekha," said Ira.

The machines went on logging the day's long failure deep into the tent-wire shadow under the wall where nobody was arguing any more.

**Roz shaam ka khel hai.**

---

At Friday evening's roof meeting, Ira waited precisely eight seconds before firing, which for her was civilization.

"Veer," she said. "Batao. Andar se jo aaya hai woh bhi batao."

Veer, who had never once in his life chosen anything but the straight road regardless the traffic on it, said: "Main unke Saturday challenge mein jaunga."

Nobody said anything.

Ira looked at him the way she looked at errors that have arrived too early to be mistakes and too late to be student-questions, and waited to be outraged.

"Kal lecture humara line-up hai. Akhada unka hai," Veer said, addressing his own knuckles. "Lekin ye bracelet waala scene — main usse paas se test karna chahta hoon. Darwaze pe nahi. Ring mein. Maine khud pehli baar rozana bharat mein alchemy ka sauda dekha. Us bracelet ke saath jis bazaar wala maamla saamne hai, woh match-record nahi nikalta without exact testing."

"Toh tum maar khaane jaa rahe ho," Ira said. "Saaf-saaf."

"Nahi — naapne ja raha hoon. meri haar bhi data hai unke dial ke liye. Aur unka dial ka data mera hai. Seher sab jagah apna tariff hai."

Aadhya raised one palm. "Veer. Teen minute. Unka instrument tumhe maar ke dikhaata hai tag pehle toh important hai."

Rudra spoke last, and entirely by himself, and in the tone that can only be reached by boys who have decided a thing is worth their one voice only: "Tu jaayega toh — teen minute na poore hue toh?"

"Toh paanch-hazaar ka fayda ke saath," Veer said, "aur teen minute ki report ke saath aaunga. Poora haath — sindoor-ben."

Ira shut her eyes tightly, like somebody closing a ledger against weather. "Ghar pe aake karo. Sone din karo. Aur main liikhti hoon —"

"Ja."

Ira shut her eyes the way a municipal treasurer closes the last register of the year. A long pause. Then: "Theek. Toh Saturday ko main tumhare bahane all aur saath peeche na rahoon. Yeh meri pehli kaamyaabi hai is yagya mein."

Aadhya said, gently, completing it: "Pehli kaamayabi — woh hum sab ki hongi."

The four of them sat in the roof-dark without speaking for a time. Down in the lane the neighbour-aunt switched off her corridor bulb; on the height opposite, over the Badi Haveli, the last lit lamp steadied and stayed lit, a signatory done being tired of watching all letters of that house.

Ira wrote on her last page:

**"Saturday: patience ka test. Meri register se zyada main."**


---

Birju pehlwan reached home past ten that night, slower than the street required.

His wife's flower stall was folded for the night, and she had left the string-light on above the door the way she always did when he had eaten properly that day. He stood under it a while before knocking, working his left forearm against his body for warmth — the cuff still on, tight like a bad ring — and listening, without meaning to, to the small faint hum a street's light makes when somebody else has taken it for a sample between the lines of its electricity bill.

Inside the one-lit main room his wife sat with her needle-folio over the folded square of her marriage's Tuesdays. She did not ask him anything about the results.

He said anyway: "Unnees saal ka bachcha tha. Teen minute roka."

"Main darwaze pe nahi thi," she said. "Lekin gali ki sab news merese hoke guzarti hai. Woh unnees-sala ladka tha na? Haath mein gada-style exercise karne waala?"

He nodded. Took the pakora she had covered under a plate. Ate without tasting.

"Yeh jo bandhaya unhone mujhe," he said at last, touching the cuff once, "main ise phenk sakta hoon. — Kabhi laut ke rakh leta hoon."

She looked at him the long way wives look when calculating a hem against a purse: "Toh phenk de. Sunday ko lautey ghar, hum haathon se soch karenge."

He slept without answering.

Across the district, unseen by any of this, the Badi Haveli's last lamp went dark at 11:46 PM by human decision.

The city hardly noticed: it was a modest office hour, and old Banaras in November slept as it always sleeps: before a doorway, in the calm of its own arrangements.

---

In the Foundation's wet-ink night register — a hard rectangle of a book beside the wire their reports fed ground-fore — entered somewhere toward the back in a small even hand:

*Event 41 — akhada challenge: local subject Moin-ul-Haq, age 19. Duration achieved: 180 sec. Bracelet output maximum sustained: 96%. Subject uninstrumented. Estimated output origin: unknown — pending re-scan. Recommended: approach indirect only. Priority flag: deliberation room — second desk.*

And below that entry, in the same dry hand, the single unalterable truth which no tactics can erase and no confidentiality can revoke:

*Re-scan, Sunday to Thursday. Shehar ke number ab yudh par hai.*
