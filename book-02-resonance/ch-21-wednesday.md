# CHAPTER 21 — WEDNESDAY (BOOK 2, Ch 1)

The first thing Wednesday morning established, with the neutrality of a municipal notification, was that nothing had happened.

The newspapers arrived folded into the same damp squares: monsoon forecast; irrigation tender; a marathon for heart-health. The bridge-road had no frilled bulletins about eight black vehicles. The shops along it opened in their usual staggered order. Varanasi proceeded at the exact pitch of Varanasi, the way a river proceeds at the pitch of a river, and only those who knew where the margins lived noticed that the margins were different at all.

Ira noticed. She had been up since four, re-zeroing everything.

At dawn her building's terrace gave her the whole west: the road, the bridge, and beyond both, along a turn in the river opposite the last of the named ghats, a building she had spent her whole life not caring about — the old cornice-fronted estate of a vanished indigo family, shuttered since before her grandmother's birth, the one the whole city called *Badi Haveli*, the one nobody visited because the herons owned its river steps. Property men approached it yearly and failed; two years back a team of surveyors had measured it for a sports complex and left within the week in silence, instruments packed, like visitors refused at a gate.

This morning, at seven, Badi Haveli was lit.

Every window, top to bottom — and lit in rows, with the discipline of offices, not the warmth of a house. A workman on a ladder was screwing a new header-board into place above the main door, letter by cedar letter:

**THE MERIDIAN FOUNDATION**
*Heritage Restoration & Research*

and below it, small, civic, courteous to the point of insult:

*Public Lecture — This Saturday. All Welcome.*

Ira stood very still. On the notebook's inner flap she wrote, twice, in different versions of the same sentence: *they bought nothing. They inherited something.* And below, underlined twice: **pehla move: paper, darwaza nahi.**

She didn't turn to look for spirits. She copied the sign's every curriculwal in place of it. She measured the distance to the river: two hundred metres. In the ungoverned quiet of her instruments — nosewept seigeing from her own flag for the year — she was afforded a full plain read from nothing but the flange of light passing through her wrist-strip's wrinkles: bees. Those points arrived last night had not dissolved into the city as arrives had way things out had done. They had registered to the address.

They had an office.

---

Veer got it at seven at the news-stand, with a glass of milk-coffee going cool in his hand.

"Arre Veer bhai," said the stand's owner — "Badi Haveli khul gayi. Saari raat kaam. Subah-se subah board laga diya. Kuch naya mission-type sanstha ayi hai. Pata nahin kahan se bheja hai, sahab nazar hi nahi ate. Chillane waale log bilkul nahi."

"Karamchari," the owner corrected himself, borrowing the city's register for people who wore shoes. "Officei waale hain bhai. Cheena idhar, nimbu-paani udhar. Bahut tez."

"Log pehchane huye?"

"Koi ek bhi chehra nahi. Sab bilkul naye. Aur seedhi baat —" the older man lowered his spine mysteriously, with the street-sage's tone — "jo itni raat bhar kaam karwaata hai aur kisi ne usko chai na diye bulane tak — uska iraada *khuli neeyat* ka nahi hota."

Veer walked home faster than he had walked since Friday. He had got five lanes into the doing of not running when he saw his grandmother standing at their gate-hook, fully dressed, sandalwood dot on her forehead, chunnat pulled an iron inch forward from the neck.

"Veer." Her voice had already decided the syllabus. "Andar aao."

"Dadi kya baat hai—"

"Aaj sab log alag alag kaam chhodo. School-dakhal band. Aaj panna paltna hai."

He stood still with his steel glass going lighter in his hand and read her face the way one reads the last page of a community paper. "Aissa kya—"

"Char gaadiyan. Wahi kaali jo pul pe dikhi kal raat. Paanch baje se andar-kaar hui hain. Municipality ke ipar warapa par sign kara ke bheja hai — *marammat* ka kaagaz, restoration ka." She spoke with her old even breath: "Veer — is sheher mein marammat ke naam pe kabhi koi bheed bali ki jagah haath nahi rakhta."

He accepted. Both of them loaded the gate down together as the admonition was finished, side by side, exactly as the sun clears a shoulder, and madeword into the courtyard.

"Dadi," Veer said at length, "yaani ye log—"

"Ye log *kaagaz* se aa rahe hain," she said. "Aur kaagaz se sirf woh aa paate hain jinhe zameen ka bojh nahi chalana padta." Then, adjusting her chunnat like a shawl worn in court: "Shaam ko sab ke sab bulaa lo. Tum chaaron, Rudra ke saath uske pehredaar se mil kar aao. Kabhi-kabhi puraane dewaar ka chehra bataa leta hai naye chhithkyon ka maqsad."

Veer, for the first time of his whole life, stopped asking her questions at dawn.

Aadhya found out at nine, in her own way.

The kitchen way of her house. Her mother fed the rope-pillar cow at the courtyard crossing in the same cadence as three days a week for years, and without turning — in a voice rendered absolutely even by a stressful economics of breakfast — she said:

"Beta. Subah nau baje ghar ke neeche se ek naye daftar ka aadmi guzra."

Aadhya sat down on the charpoy's edge with the milk-pot in her hand. "Kaisa insaan, Ammu?"

"Naya endeavour waala insaan." Her mother worked the feed through her fingers grain by grain, categorizing mundanely, as if riding the words through the toughness of her state. "Kapde utne crisp ke mujhe breaking karne ki zarurat ho. Haath mein file, paon mein shoes ki awaaz aisi jo sirf *mandate* waalon ki hoti hai. Usne rasta poochha —" a breath — "magar ghat ka rasta bataa ke bhi usne aakhir mein yeh bola: *Madam ji, hum log yahan project laga rahe hain — heritage restoration — shahar ki seva hai.*"

She turned, at last. Her face carried nothing her hands had not already said.

"Aadhya. Is sheher mein jab koi *seva* shabd bahut seedha bole na, toh samajh — uske peeche panch pehre ki bandobasti hai. Ziada nischay bhaari dikhta hai jo aadmi saamne ho, woh ya toh ustad hota hai ya khadku." The tone was the family's special measure for outsiders. "Saf ki seedhi chaal — print-ready — alag hi hoti hai."

Aadhya listened at the scale of registry she was beginning to pay everything.

"Kal raat ke bare mein mujhse kuch mat poochna — mujhe nahi pata kya hua. Magar —" she shifted the feed-bowls down one hook — "jitna mujhe samajh aata hai, us raat ke baad jo bhi dikh raha hai mujhe today morning mein — sab ke baare mein jhooth mat bolna mujhse."

Then she rose; shook the pallu forward over her shoulder with the competence of a teller closing her window; and halfway up the courtyard stair, without looking back at all, she filed the morning's entire standing order:

"Shaam ko Rudra ke baba ko bulao. Unse zaroori hai baat."

PAN: The city of Varanasi, at 5:55 PM on a Wednesday on which nothing had happened.

Ira's tabulation waited on the chai stall's tin table under the stone, written in her smallest hand with the pencil from college thin enough to nearly share the margin's privacy: signature-count at 11:54 PM, Tuesday — **20.** Dissipation overnight: none of the known kind. By dawn, nineteen of the twenty had cancelled their separate footprints — collapsed, merged, withdrawn — inward, in a ratio she did not like at all — into *one* answer. And that one stayed exactly where nothing in her own census of the last several months had ever stayed: point-steady, shore-boring, at one location, repeating on a schedule of three deadbeat seconds like a lighthouse paying its board, with the confidence of a broadcast.

The old kind had come to drink. It moved always, it withdrew nightly, it filled itself quickly and left no forward address because it had none — because a water-siphon with a handle has no use for a letterbox. And what now first rose clear as nothing out of the measuring had different money, that the shape of the tax return; what moved into Varanasi on Tuesday night with eight cars had *rent* and *employees* and a *Saturday public lecture.*

"Wo bhoot nahi banke shehron mein utar rahe," Ira said to nobody in particular, which is a thing she did scarcely and with extreme justification. "Wo panchayat ke upar se decree karake khud ko judge dalne ki tayaari kar rahe hain. Pahle law — phir ladai."

Nobody at the chai stall overheard her except the chaiwala, who gave her the ginger order gratis and didn't comment, as the unlettered proprietors of chai stalls have done during conversations of qualification since caste measures were stops.

Her new notebook-cover line she had just then written, later underlined even further, composed the whole of the read of the remaining one closet verdict for Book 2's purposes different ways:

*Bees aaye the. Unnees pighal ke samet gaye. Bees ka maamla — ek chhokdi pe aa ke bhaari ho gaya.*

*Daftarkhaana banake baith gaye.*

One further measure of the estate was noted by nobody and carried home by the river-sweepers: the herons had left the Badi Haveli's river steps in the night. Forty years of colonized ones — sixty birds by the conservancy of ghat myth — had risen before dawn and gone down-stream to park themselves along the old sand-spit beside Dashashwamedh, where they were standing on the morning of Wednesday waiting in a thin serious thread exactly like a processional queue of priests whose temple has sold itself to newcomers.

The sweepers reported this without commentary. The city filed it, as hearts do, without believing it.

---

Rudra and Veer walked past the Badi Haveli at noon, sharing the ends of a bamboo pole between them from which hung Veer's mother's day's weight of electrical fittings — a delivery shift neither of them had ever done before, invented by Veer with eighteen minutes' notice and the absolute confidence of an idea whose usefulness has never yet been tested.

The lane opened on the river-bend road where the estate's wall began. Since Ira's dawn reconnaissance the old building had acquired three new ornaments of order: a brass visitor's bell freshly mounted on the doorframe; a survey-nipple drilled through the wall's stone and painted over two shades brighter than the wall; and a neat little rack by the gate-office window of laminated forms — cheerful clips — beside the region's lotus seal cut into the wall's threshold band.

"Yeh log," Veer said, under his breath, between strides, "bahut saaf planned ki hui baat rakhte hain."

"Seedha chal," Rudra answered. "Bojh mat chhodo."

They paused before the gate office while a bruised Ganges-crossing truck maneuvered out of the inner lane like a wounded cockerel shedding the logistics of a street. The gate clerk — young, tight-moustached, the sort whose moustache seems applied with a template — looked up at Rudra, and past him, with the intelligence of somebody sighting a bird by its flight path.

And Rudra noticed — standing very still — something that should not have been left in the open at all.

Fixed to the side of the clerk's clipboard-harness was a small boxed dial — watch-sized, with eight hair-lines around its face — and at the precise second Rudra came within three metres of the gate, the dial was spinning. Not fluttering, not shaking: *spinning*, like a watch running backwards the whole hour when somebody leans over the back of it. And the moment Rudra shifted two steps to the side to make room for a pedestrian, the little needle found its rest-point and settled.

The clerk looked at the dial. Then at Rudra. Then, with one of the two compulsory front-desk smiles of certain kinds of organizations:

"Bhai, kuch delivery ho toh andar rakh do — office open hai aaj."

"Nahi sir," said Rudra. "Bas guzar rahe the. Load hai haath mein."

The clerk's eyes put a name on him gently — not a name aloud, a name among columns, a name with a pencil. Rudra did not hurry out of them. He had, after nineteen years' worth of five AM grounds and four strange ghat-weeks, learned better than to rush the prices of being read.

When the corner was turned at last and the wall interrupted the line of sight, Veer exhaled for both of them.

"Ek cheez notice ki tumne?" Veer asked.

"Haan."

"Woh kya—"

"*Woh mere liye tha.* Gate ke teen metre mein ghusa — needle ghoomi. Peeche hata — needle baith gayi." Rudra looked at his own palms as at papers returned unfiled. "Unke paas munh bhar sensor hai wahan. Aadmi dekh raha hai. Aankh mein sms likhwa raha hai."

Veer walked the bamboo's other end with this under his hat for a while. "Toh ab samjho hum jaate waqt bhi inki nazron ke saamne se seedha—"

"Ab seedha inke saamne nahi jayenge kabhi" Rudra said. "Aur aaj ke baad, gate se nahin. Hum inke darwaze se guzarte rahenge — delivery ke naam pe, anna ke naam pe — magar *main* nahin ghusunga unke teen-metre ke andar. Woh meter meri cheez ko bachne ke dhang se jhur-nahi karte — naapne ka kaam karte hain."

"Matlab?"

"Matlab," Rudra said, "unhone wahan aise log rakhe hain jo naap sakte hain. Aur main unka table pe likha gaya ek entry ban gaya hoon — bina kabhi andar aaye."

And Veer, who a week ago used to carry all his news in his rolled-up sleeve, found himself laughing aloud at the sheer used-bookshop unfairness of it: that they were, at last, being **measured by somebody else's instruments.**

Wednesday also acquired, at four, one thing which did not belong to the war at all.

At five-thirty Aadhya's mother came, alone, in a cream cotton sari with its old gold border, and stood in the front veranda of Ram's house, and greeted Rukmini with the accent of the coast where she had been born and never discussed.

What was said between the two mothers was very little. What was measured between them took approximately eleven minutes and a pot of chai.

Rukmini learned that Aadhya's mother had been, each Monday and Thursday for a month, walking twice around the ancient ring of the panch-kosi pilgrim route before sunrise — alone, unlit, in a private mode of bribery legal in every religion — and that she had begun it without telling her husband, and intended to go on without telling anybody who could be induced to forbid her. "Seedhi baat hai," she said, setting down her glass. "Jo humke bachche ke liye kar sakte hain — main kar rahi hoon. Baaki unke kaam aur unki qudrat hai."

Rukmini learned, equivalently, that the gold thread under Aadhya's finger had been observed by her mother at two-thirty in the morning of some night in a sleeve tossed over a chair, and understood, and never named aloud.

What Aadhya's mother learned was simpler, and sat deeper. She learned that Rukmini had once stood in front of the convergence of the sky to block it for a boy — and that she had believed, from that morning, no power describes in anyone's files accounting more reliable than that housekeeping pressed against a mother's ribs.

They parted at the gate, like neighbours. The sun went down a quarter-inch. The jamun planted on Sunday last had achieved, overnight, between Tuesday and Wednesday, its first five genuinely new leaves, and hung above them like a decision someone else had made and remembered to keep.

"Jai shree Krishna," said Aadhya's mother, at the gate's step.

"Ram Ram," said Rukmini.

It was the first alliance of that house with another mother's house — and the correct elemental guess of all evenings was that neither woman mentioned it to their children, because they were, after all, mothers, and handled history by inventory.

At seven the four of them took Veer's roof — the roof had memory, and the pakora-tray was a civic duty. Dadi stayed below, deliberately, ironing a cotton sari under the hanging bulb, ironing it with a slow precision that had become, by last Tuesday, a form of sentry duty.

Ira opened the meeting the way these meetings always opened now — without opening it, by laying two things on the parapet between the tumblers: the notebook, and her wrist-strip. The strip had kept, since Monday, a permanent tone — darker than its weaving, an ash-line at the pulse-point.

"Bees signatures the kal raat. Subah tak unnees samet liye gaye. *Sirf ek* khada hai ab bhi baki," she said, "aur teen-second ke gap pe repeat ho raha hai. Exact. she said, "aur teeno-second ke gap pe repeat ho raha hai. Exact. Jaise dhak-dhak pulse aamtaur par — teen second, pulse, teen second, pulse. Woh pehli baar maine notice kiya —" she looked at each of them — "yeh woh pehli cheez hai jo *consume* nahi kar rahi. Yeh — *banaane waali* hai. Manufacturing." 

"Yaani?" Veer said.

"Yaani ab tak jo bhi aaya tha woh *paani peeta tha* aur chala jaata tha. Yeh — paani ke bagal mein *kuwan khodke* baith gaya hai."

---
Aadhya looked up from the strip. "Manufacturing of what?"

"Of *capacity*." Ira shut the notebook. "Kal raat unhone naapa. Aaj unhone office chalaya. Board laga diya. Saturday ka public lecture announce kar diya. Socho — chor chori karta hai. Board sirf woh latkata hai jiska daayra poora ho."

"Yeh achi khabar nahi hai?" Veer asked.

"Yeh Type A warn hai," said Ira. "Seedhi baat: chhipe hue dushman akela maar sakta hai. Khulla daftar — battalion maangta hai."

Nobody translated it further. The roof's tank gurgled once. Below in the courtyard Dadi continued to iron the same sari with a patience that was itself a kind of armor.

"Saturday lecture — sab jayenge?" Aadhya asked.

"Sab jayenge," Ira said. "Bilkul peeche. Bilkul ordinary."

"Teen niyam," Rudra said. The roof knew the voice by now; it was the voice of the only student who re-ruled the evening between the toast and the walk. "Pehla — Saturday sab jayenge, bilkul peeche, bilkul ordinary. Doosra — koi kisi ko akela nahi bhejega. Ghat pe nahi. Library ke andar bhi nahi." He paused, letting the pause be understood by the whole roof — "aur teesra — kisi ka haath bhaagega toh utha ke dikhaana. Bachhaana panaah hai. Chhupana nahi."

Translation was left to the roof's tank, which settled into its steel quiet with a thump like punctuation.

"Done," said Veer.

"Done," said Aadhya.

"Done," said Ira last, her eyes still on the western dark. "Kal se sab alag-alag taiyaari karenge — lekin sab ki shaam ghar pe hogi. Chaahe jo ho."

They said goodnight separately and entirely.

The pulse-test came at 7:32 PM exactly.

Veer felt it before he heard it — the arm heard weather before weather arrived; so he was already standing on the parapet when the lane's bulbs went low, ward by ward, south-to-north — not load-shedding, not a fuse — in sequence. They re-lit together. Nothing. Three seconds. Two. One. A second pulse: the same long trough of darkness down the avenues, in clean schools-of-fish procession, and an ending.

The third beat was not a pulse at all. It was a *response.*

From inside every pocket of metal in the quarter — jammed window-rod, ordered appliance, cutlery boxes laid in storage — rose a single answering note, sustained, sub-aural and *found by everyone*, the way one is found in the ground at the end of hiding: iron answering iron, latch answering latch, a nok-jhonk of a bridge-rivet to some unseen oar " — the whole west of the city chimed, once — like a bell struck somewhere far away heard through a well.

Then everything went ordinary at once. Buses. An air-cooler's drone continuing a fight with its own motor. A scooter stalled twice and recovered and was followed by the man's embarrassment audible all the way down the lane.

Ira, bent over her wrist-strip, felt a warm wet line at her lip; she wiped it with the back of her hand — the nosebleed of paying her full attention — and still managed to be the first to write it:

*7:32 PM. Interval 180s. Note: ye calibration nahi tha — ye roll-call tha. Woh poochte hain — sheher, epilogue mein jawab deta hai.*

"Kya likha tumne?" Veer asked.

"Ye," she said, without anger and without hurry, "ki pehle hum usse maap rahe the. Ab — woh humko bula raha hai *apne naam se.*"

The dark took the sentence very completely. Nobody said *bees*. One of the windows above the lane kept a light burning one minute longer than household decency required, and switched off.

---

The last observation of Wednesday came at eleven-eighteen, to whoever still had their eyes open. Few did; the city had been told by exhaustion to sign for the day and go home.

In the west, over the Badi Haveli's lit chimneys of crown, a hemisphere of pale gold rose without seed or authorization, a whole-lung exhalation of light unfolding two storeys high — so exactly the colour of the Monday miracle of Rukmini-yatra that the lay face of it pulled at the gut of anyone who knew that colour, lined only two degrees colder, two degrees smoother around the sides, the way a forged signature is smoother than the handwriting of the man it imitates.

Then it retracted. Two seconds, flat. No follow-up. No debris.

The dogs of the whole west ward rose and were silent, and not one of them turned around. Wednesday officially ended.

---

Late in the evening — late, in that house, meaning the lamps were two-thirds down and the kitchen remembered its last flame — Rudra found his father in the storeroom, backlit, turning over instruments nobody owned anymore: a railway punch, a pair of flat bamboo calipers from the era of somebody's draughtsman grandfather, a school ruler with the name worn off, each held up against a bare angle of the lamp exactly as if measuring the instrument one last time before it changed hands on its own.

"Baith," Ram said, before Rudra had knocked.

Rudra sat on the stool-stack of bound ledgers nobody else ever opened, and waited. He had spent his whole life waiting across this desk: there is a kind of son raised this way whose patience is, by then, mostly made of his father's. The father set the calipers down in the exact centre of the desk.

"Unke gate pe ek meter hai," Rudra said, without ornament. The necessary sentences had stopped being necessary: "Gate se teen metre ke andar ghuste hi needle ghoom gayi — aur baahar aate hi baith gayi."

He laid on the desk a pencil sketch he had made from memory on a torn almanac back-page: the box dial, the spinning needle, the clerk's face whose moustache he had noted as an address of the outwardness of awareness. He narrated the whole thing in that plain sequence — three metres, the spin, the smile the clerk had used at the gate, the kind of people who smile like people who did their caution.

Ram looked at the sketch for a long time. Then he set both hands flat down onto the desk, and looked at his son with the unreadable entire look of a man who has found out — in the time of the spinning needle himself knew — that a thing measured, calculated and now sold exactly as preparedly, was at least reading his particular son, nineteen years lacking in the story:

"Seedha jaao mat udhar ab. Humse bhi jaana mat. Dukaanon mein gayahbe churane waale hamesha khatra”

He stopped. He moved the calipers over the desk's surface half a hand's width to the left — the gesture that had served all his married days as a paragraph-break:

"Tumse poochna chahiye tha mujhe. Ab in logon ke saamne mat niklo. — *Woh tumhe, nahi pehchaante, Rudra. Bas padh hogi likhna aur isliye hamesha banta hai.*"

He closed the storeroom's inner lamp; and on the stair, one hand along the wall for the balance men pretend they don't need, he said one further thing without turning:

"Kal lecture mein bhaiyon. Wahan baithne waale log ghadi pe nahi sochte." The sentence settled around his son's feet like an old coin rolled across stone: "woh log *taksal* pe sochte hain."

Rudra lay awake after all that by his own low lamp, writing for nobody in a bound khata — recording, one by one, every instruction he had received that day from any adult in his life: his mother's word, his father's word, the librarian's old calendar-string condition, his grandmother-modeled thread; the seven-cola instruction from Ira; the whispers of the township that had learned to be contacted all between scenes. He changed the order three times. The orders always ended at the same place: in the old hand-kept item row of something other than roots, something harder to express than warning.

He did not need a name for it. Nineteen years of this house - the house he had been raised under had taught him none of that voice for one lifetime need be added manually.

Then he slept. And outside Ram's door, at three AM, somebody was still awake, standing with their face flat to the terrace parapet westward, watching the last twilit lamp of the windows of the Badi Haveli burn, which also, never, this night, quite went out.
